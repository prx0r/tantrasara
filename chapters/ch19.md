# Tantrasāra — Chapter 19

एकोनविंशमाह्निकम् — Śrāddhadīkṣāprakāśanam

The Funeral of the Enlightened: or, how to initiate a dead bloke, turn his cremation into the final offering, and discover that when a real master dies, you’re apparently supposed to have a bloody festival.


Right then.

Last chapter, we consecrated a guru.

Very serious business.

We established that you can’t simply put on an enormous beard, drape yourself in rudrākṣa beads, receive an elaborate ceremonial anointing and announce that you’re qualified to liberate the entire human race.

You actually need knowledge.

Jñāna.

Rather an inconvenient requirement.

And once the knowledge is established, there’s consecration, six months of daily mantra discipline, the responsibility to initiate qualified disciples, and a prohibition against handing sacred teachings to people who aren’t prepared for them.

Excellent.

But now suppose our properly qualified guru receives some rather unfortunate news.

A disciple has died.

Or perhaps someone who followed another religious tradition has died.

Or someone who previously belonged to the higher Śaiva teachings, but abandoned their observances and never performed the required expiation.

What happens?

Do we simply say:

“Well, that’s that.”

“Terribly unfortunate.”

“Perhaps we’ll see them next incarnation.”

Absolutely not.

Because Abhinavagupta has yet another initiation.

And this time, the disciple is already dead.

Antyasaṃskāra-dīkṣā.

The initiation of the final sacrament.

And then:

Śrāddha-dīkṣā.

Initiation through the ancestral or memorial offering.

Now, you might be thinking:

“Abhinava, mate, we’ve already had two chapters explaining initiation for the dead.”

Correct.

And you remember the extraordinary Great Net of Śambhunātha?

Mahājāla.

The one that supposedly reaches through the cosmic pathways to draw an absent being into the guru’s ritual field?

Well, we’re going to need that again.

Except there’s something different this time.

The corpse is actually present.

And apparently Abhinavagupta has some rather extraordinary claims about what can happen to it.

But don’t worry.

We’ll distinguish the traditional claims from established physical facts.

And by the end of the chapter, we’ll reach one of the most beautiful statements in the entire Tantrasāra.

The death of a genuine knower is not, for the lineage of that knowledge, merely an occasion of loss.

It becomes a sacred festival.

Parvadina.

And the reason has everything to do with the fact that guru and disciple are ultimately one consciousness.

Right.

Let’s begin.

1. Who receives the final initiation?
Abhinavagupta opens with a rather generous provision.

Well.

Generous by the standards of his extremely hierarchical religious system.

He says that even people belonging to lower scriptural traditions may receive this final initiation.

And that includes their gurus.

Let’s examine the Sanskrit.

ATTESTED OPENING — 19.1

अधरशासनस्थानां गुर्वन्तानामपि

Adharaśāsanasthānāṃ gurvantānām api.

Let’s unpack that.

Adhara — lower, inferior.

Śāsana — teaching, doctrine, religious dispensation.

Sthāna — being established in.

And:

Gurvanta — extending even to gurus.

So the sense is:

Those belonging to what this tradition regards as lower teachings, even including their teachers.

Now, remember Chapter Seventeen?

Liṅgoddhāra.

The release from previous religious vows.

We discussed how someone who had been initiated into a Vaiṣṇava or another Śaiva system might, after experiencing śaktipāta, relinquish the earlier commitment and enter the higher Trika teaching.

Well, this chapter considers another possibility.

What if that person dies before the transition has been completed?

And Abhinavagupta says the final initiatory rite may be performed immediately after death.

Maraṇa-samanantaram.

But there’s an important condition.

Mṛtoddhārodita-śaktipāta.

The descent of divine power arising in connection with the elevation or rescue of the deceased.

Now, break that compound down.

Mṛta — dead.

Uddhāra — lifting up, rescuing, liberating.

Udita — arisen.

Śaktipāta — the descent of Power.

So the rite isn’t presented simply as an arbitrary attempt to initiate anyone whom the officiant happens to remember.

Its religious justification is the manifestation of grace associated with the deceased.

And notice something.

Even the guru of a lower tradition isn’t absolutely excluded.

That’s rather significant within Abhinavagupta’s sectarian hierarchy.

Someone may have spent their life transmitting what he considers an inferior doctrine.

Yet, through śaktipāta, they can become eligible for the final initiation.

Now, this is not an objective judgment that other religious traditions are intrinsically inferior. It’s Abhinavagupta explaining eligibility according to his own hierarchy of Śaiva revelation.

But here’s the interesting part.

He immediately introduces a second category.

People who belonged to the higher teachings but abandoned their observances.

And this one is particularly revealing.

2. What if someone was initiated properly, then completely neglected their vows?
Now we’ve got:

Ūrdhvaśāsanastha.

Someone established in a higher teaching.

And two further descriptions.

Luptasamaya.

One whose religious observances have lapsed.

Akṛtaprāyaścitta.

One who hasn’t performed expiation.

Let’s break those down.

Lupta — lost, abandoned, allowed to lapse.

Samaya — religious commitment, initiatory observance.

Then:

A-kṛta — not done.

Prāyaścitta — expiation, atonement.

So imagine someone who has received a formal initiation.

Perhaps they took the samayī vows we discussed in Chapter Thirteen.

They were instructed to honour their guru.

Practise their mantra.

Observe the prescribed religious disciplines.

And maintain the appropriate relationship with their deity.

Very good.

But then, somewhere along the way, they stop.

Perhaps their practice collapses.

Perhaps they abandon the observances.

And when the time comes to address those failures, they don’t undertake the prescribed expiation.

Then they die.

Now, here’s the question.

Is their spiritual situation completely hopeless?

Abhinavagupta says no.

The final initiation may also be performed for them.

And he closes the opening prescription with:

इति परमेश्वराज्ञा

Iti parameśvarājñā.

Thus is the command of the Supreme Lord.

Now, this is important.

The rite isn’t being presented as a sympathetic innovation invented by a particularly kind-hearted officiant.

It’s claimed as an instruction authorised by Parameśvara.

And there are two rather different groups here.

Someone from a lower religious tradition.

And someone who belonged to the higher one but failed to fulfil its commitments.

Both may receive the final consecration.

The significant condition is the appropriate descent of divine grace.

And that should remind us of Chapter Eleven.

Śaktipāta is not ultimately subordinated to an individual’s history of religious achievement.

A person may possess a very impressive religious biography.

Or a rather disappointing one.

The supreme freedom of Śiva isn’t reduced to either.

Right.

We’ve established who’s eligible.

Now we need the procedure.

And Abhinavagupta says something wonderfully simple.

Use the rite I’ve already explained.

Except this time?

Perform it upon the corpse.

3. The corpse becomes the ritual support
The Sanskrit says:

तत्र यो मृतोद्धारे विधिरुक्तः स सर्व एव शरीरे कर्तव्यः।

Tatra yo mṛtoddhāre vidhir uktaḥ sa sarva eva śarīre kartavyaḥ.

The entire procedure previously taught for the elevation of the deceased is to be performed in relation to the body.

Now, remember Chapter Sixteen?

We had the absent disciple.

Parokṣa.

And because the person wasn’t physically available, the guru could use a representation made of sacred grass or other suitable material.

A ritual likeness.

Then the Great Net was employed to bring the individual into the consecrated field, according to the tradition.

Well, here there’s no need to manufacture a little grass disciple.

We’ve got the actual body.

The corpse itself becomes the ritual support.

Śavaśarīra.

The body of the deceased.

And this distinction matters.

Because the procedure is no longer simply a ritual directed toward an invisible absent being through an artificial representation.

It’s integrated with the physical funeral.

And at the end?

Pūrṇāhuti.

The complete oblation.

Remember that?

The concluding offering through which the ritual reaches completion.

But here the complete oblation has an especially dramatic relationship with the funeral.

Śavaśarīradāha.

The burning of the corpse.

Now that’s an extraordinary connection.

The cremation is integrated into the final sacrificial act.

The funeral pyre and the initiatory offering belong to one ritual arrangement.

Think about that.

In an ordinary funeral, a body is cremated.

In this tantric account, the cremation is understood through the completion of a divine initiatory rite.

The dead person’s condition is addressed through the sacred operation.

The corpse becomes the ritual support.

And the burning of the body is joined to the complete offering.

But we’re not finished.

Because Abhinavagupta makes an even more astonishing claim.

He says the guru may perform a sapratyaya funeral rite so that those who doubt can become convinced.

And apparently this involves the corpse visibly moving.

Yes.

You heard correctly.

Now we’re getting into some serious tantric lore.

4. The funeral that allegedly makes the corpse move
Right.

Abhinavagupta introduces:

Sapratyayā antyeṣṭiḥ.

A funeral rite that produces conviction.

Let’s break that down.

Sa-pratyaya — accompanied by conviction, assurance or confirmation.

Antyeṣṭi — the final sacrifice, the funeral rite.

And why is this special version performed?

मूढानां तु प्रतीतिरूढये

Mūḍhānāṃ tu pratītirūḍhaye.

To establish firm conviction among those who remain confused or unconvinced.

Now, mūḍha is a rather strong word.

Confused.

Deluded.

Not understanding.

But don’t imagine Abhinavagupta is simply standing beside a cremation ground calling the grieving relatives idiots.

The relevant issue is their lack of conviction regarding the supposed efficacy of the rite.

And here’s what makes this passage unusual.

He attributes the extraordinary operation to three powers:

Kriyā.

Ritual action.

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Yoga.

Yogic accomplishment.

And their combined power is:

Kriyā-jñāna-yoga-bala.

Now that’s important.

He isn’t merely saying:

“Perform some action and hope something happens.”

He’s describing a guru whose ritual action, knowledge and yogic power operate together.

And what does the guru do?

First:

Saṃhārakrama.

The sequence of dissolution or withdrawal.

The mantras are installed upon the corpse in the order associated with reabsorption.

Nyāsa.

Remember what we’ve been doing throughout the initiatory chapters?

Installing divine powers in the ritual support?

Well, here the support is the deceased person’s body.

But the arrangement is directed toward dissolution rather than the ordinary unfolding of manifestation.

And then comes:

Jālakrama.

The method of the Net.

Remember Śambhunātha’s Great Net from Chapter Sixteen?

The extraordinary visualisation in which the guru’s power is imagined extending through the cosmic pathways?

Well, it’s back.

The deceased individual’s jīva is said to be drawn into the ritual field.

Ākṛṣya.

Having drawn near.

Then Abhinavagupta gives three further operations.

And the words are quite dramatic.

Rodhana.

Restraining.

Vedhana.

Piercing or penetrating.

Ghaṭṭana.

Stirring, agitating or striking into movement.

Now, don’t misunderstand this.

These are technical tantric operations, not permission to physically assault or manipulate a corpse.

Their fuller technical explanations belong to the initiatory system already introduced.

The Sanskrit here lists them without spelling out a complete new procedure.

And then comes the most astonishing statement.

Abhinavagupta describes the movement of vital energy in relation to three bodily locations.

The Heart.

Hṛd.

The throat.

Kaṇṭha.

And the forehead.

Lalāṭa.

And he says:

ATTESTED PROSE — 19.2

इत्येवं शवशरीरं कम्पते
Ity evaṃ śavaśarīraṃ kampate.

“Thus the corpse trembles.”

Right.

Let’s acknowledge just how extraordinary that is.

He’s actually claiming that the corpse moves.

Kampate.

Trembles.

Shakes.

Now imagine the scene.

An elaborate funeral has been prepared.

The guru has consecrated the corpse.

The dissolution-mantras have been installed.

The Great Net is invoked.

The ritual powers are brought into operation.

The gathered people are watching.

And the body is said to tremble.

“Guruji.”

“Yes?”

“Did that corpse just MOVE?”

“Apparently.”

“Bloody hell.”

Now, this is a genuine extraordinary claim in the original Sanskrit.

It’s not something I’ve invented to make the chapter sound more exciting.

But the claim is not established evidence that a deceased body can be reanimated through tantric practice.

Abhinavagupta doesn’t supply a controlled demonstration, independently verifiable observation or medical explanation.

And the sentence does not establish that the deceased person’s consciousness has returned to the body.

Those are distinct questions.

What’s significant for interpreting the text is the intended religious function.

The movement is said to strengthen the conviction of people witnessing the rite.

Pratīti-rūḍhi.

Firm establishment of belief.

It’s a claimed sign of the guru’s power.

And notice the word sapratyaya.

The whole exceptional funeral is directed toward making the operation convincing.

Now here’s what I find particularly interesting.

Abhinavagupta has spent eighteen chapters insisting that the ultimate liberating power belongs to consciousness.

Yet he allows for an outward, visible manifestation intended to establish confidence in people who haven’t understood the higher teaching.

So the bodily movement, within his framework, isn’t the ultimate attainment.

It’s an evidentiary sign within the ritual.

The real objective is still liberation.

And that brings us straight to the next operation.

5. The real purpose isn’t to move the corpse — it’s to unite the deceased with Śiva
After describing the trembling body, Abhinavagupta says:

ततः परमशिवे योजनिकां कृत्वा

Tataḥ paramaśive yojanikāṃ kṛtvā.

Then, having performed the joining with Paramaśiva.

Now, remember:

Yojanikā.

The act of joining.

Bringing into conjunction.

We’ve encountered it throughout the initiation chapters.

The guru purifies the cosmic pathways, brings the disciple’s limited consciousness into a divine condition and performs the corresponding joining.

But here the recipient is dead.

And the intended destination isn’t a particular heavenly realm.

It’s:

Paramaśiva.

The supreme Lord.

Now here’s the distinction.

The movement of the corpse, if we accept the claim within its traditional context, is not the liberation itself.

The liberation concerns the overcoming of the limited condition of the individual.

And the joining with Paramaśiva is the operation understood to accomplish that.

Then the body is cremated.

पूर्णाहुत्या तद्दहेत्

Pūrṇāhutyā tad dahet.

One should burn it in conjunction with the complete oblation.

Now think about the entire sequence.

The deceased body is consecrated.

The mantras are installed.

The ritual claims to draw the departed being into the appropriate field.

The extraordinary manifestation establishes conviction.

The soul is ritually joined to Paramaśiva.

And the corpse is cremated as the funeral reaches its complete oblation.

There is no need to suppose that the supreme Self has suddenly been created by the funeral.

Remember our earliest philosophical distinction.

The individual appears limited through Śiva’s own freedom.

And liberation is understood as the removal of that limitation.

The ritual is intended to accomplish that transformation within the tradition’s account of initiation.

So, from the outside, you’ve got a funeral.

A cremation.

The end of bodily life.

But within the ritual’s theological interpretation, you’ve got a process of reabsorption into the supreme consciousness.

Now, Abhinavagupta has reached the end of the special funeral operation.

You’d think that would be enough.

The deceased has been initiated.

The body has been cremated.

The complete offering has been made.

What more could there possibly be?

Ah.

Well.

Śrāddha.

The ancestral rite.

Because apparently the relatives still have work to do.

6. The ancestral initiation — what happens on the following days?
Now comes the rite that gives the chapter its title.

Śrāddha-dīkṣā.

श्राद्धदीक्षा

Let’s understand the two terms.

Śrāddha is the ancestral or memorial offering associated with the deceased and with śraddhā, faith or devoted confidence.

Dīkṣā is initiation.

So this is not merely a generic memorial meal.

It’s an initiation associated with the ritual offering for the deceased.

And Abhinavagupta says something important.

The rite can be performed for someone who has already undergone the funeral purification.

Antyeṣṭyā śuddha.

Purified through the funeral.

But it can also be performed for others.

Anyeṣām api.

Even for those who haven’t been purified through that particular funeral rite.

So the ancestral initiation isn’t restricted to cases where the full preceding funeral operation was carried out.

That’s important.

Because otherwise someone might reasonably ask:

“What if the person has already been cremated?”

“What if the funeral was conducted by people from another tradition?”

“What if we couldn’t perform the appropriate tantric rites at the time?”

Well, the text allows śrāddha-dīkṣā for those outside the prior funeral purification.

Now, when should it be performed?

The Sanskrit gives this compressed sequence:

ATTESTED TIMING — 19.3

त्र्यहं तुर्ये दिने मासि मासि संवत्सरे संवत्सरे
Tryahaṃ turye dine māsi māsi saṃvatsare saṃvatsare.

Now, let’s be careful.

Tryaham refers to a three-day period.

Turye dine means on the fourth day.

Māsi māsi means month after month.

Saṃvatsare saṃvatsare means year after year.

So the passage associates the rite with the initial days following death, particularly the fourth day, and with monthly and annual observances.

The compressed wording is interpreted slightly differently in the available English translations: some render it as the third or fourth day, while others treat it as a three-day period followed by a fourth-day rite.

We shouldn’t pretend the sentence specifies an unambiguous, fully developed modern funeral calendar.

But the overall pattern is clear.

The rite can recur after the immediate funeral, monthly and annually.

Now, imagine the family.

They’ve just completed an extraordinary initiation involving the corpse.

The guru has performed the final oblation.

Everyone goes home.

And then someone asks:

“Right. Is that everything?”

“Not exactly.”

“What’s next?”

“The śrāddha.”

“When?”

“After the initial days.”

“Fair enough.”

“And then monthly.”

“Right.”

“And annually.”

“Bloody hell, how many ceremonies does this tradition require?”

Well, quite a few.

But here’s the important question.

Why repeat rituals if the deceased has supposedly already been liberated?

Excellent.

Because Abhinavagupta is going to address that directly.

But first, he explains the actual heart of the śrāddha offering.

And this is one of the most philosophically extraordinary passages in the entire initiatory section.

Because we’re no longer merely dealing with feeding the dead.

We’re going to take the deceased’s very capacity to experience objects and offer it into Śiva.

Not just the food.

The structure of objectivity itself.

Now we’re getting somewhere.

7. The food offering that destroys the distinction between enjoyer and enjoyed
Right.

Now we reach:

Naivedya.

नैवेद्य

Food offered to the deity.

Ordinarily you might imagine the procedure is quite straightforward.

Prepare some food.

Offer it.

Perhaps chant a mantra.

And the offering becomes sacred.

Lovely.

But Abhinavagupta is about to explain the śrāddha offering in a far more radical way.

First, the preliminary ritual is performed up to and including:

Homa.

The fire offering.

Then the officiant takes the naivedya into one hand.

Eka-haste.

Now, that sounds rather ordinary.

You’ve got food in your hand.

What’s next?

Ah.

Now comes the contemplative transformation.

And the Sanskrit is a magnificent compound.

पशुगतभोग्यशक्तितादात्म्यप्रतिपन्नाम्

Paśu-gata-bhogya-śakti-tādātmya-pratipannām.

Bloody hell.

Let’s unpack the beast.

Paśu — the bound individual.

Gata — belonging to, situated within.

Bhogya — that which is enjoyed or experienced.

Śakti — power.

Tādātmya — identity or being of the same nature.

Pratipanna — having attained or entered into a condition.

So we’re speaking of a power that has become identified with the power of the experienceable object belonging to the limited individual.

Now, remember Chapter Eight?

We discussed the difference between:

Bhoktṛ.

The enjoyer.

And:

Bhogya.

That which is enjoyed.

This distinction is absolutely fundamental to conditioned experience.

Because the ordinary individual is constituted as someone who experiences things.

“I see a flower.”

“I taste tea.”

“I hear music.”

“I enjoy pleasure.”

“I suffer pain.”

There’s an apparent subject.

And an apparent object.

And the relationship between them is structured through limitation.

Now, Abhinavagupta says the power contemplated in the offering has three characteristics.

First:

Vīryarūpa.

Of the nature of potency.

Second:

Bhogyākāra.

Having the form of something experienceable.

Third:

It has attained identity with the experienceable-object power of the deceased individual.

Now, that’s quite extraordinary.

Because the ritual offering isn’t merely understood as food being presented on behalf of a dead person.

The food serves as a support for contemplating the very power through which that person remained related to objects of experience.

And what happens next?

The power is offered into:

Parameśvara.

The Supreme Lord.

But Abhinavagupta gives him a particular designation.

Bhoktṛ.

The enjoyer.

Now listen carefully.

The object-power associated with the bound individual is offered into the supreme Enjoyer.

And that is supposed to bring about something very important.

The cessation of the individual’s condition of being bound to what is experienceable.

Bhogyabhāva-nivṛtti.

The ending of the state of being an object of experience or being conditioned through objectivity.

And when that happens?

Abhinavagupta says:

ATTESTED PROSE — 19.4

भोग्यभावे निवृत्ते पतिरेव भवति
Bhogyabhāve nivṛtte patir eva bhavati.

When the condition of being an object of experience ceases, one becomes the Lord himself.

Now that is an extraordinary statement.

Patiḥ.

The Lord.

And remember the opposite term.

Paśu.

The bound being.

Now do you see what’s happened?

We began with the deceased individual as a paśu.

A limited subject.

Someone bound within the network of experience.

Then the rite took the power of objectivity associated with that individual and offered it into the supreme Enjoyer.

And through the cessation of that limiting condition, the individual is said to attain:

Pati-bhāva.

The condition of lordship.

Now, this is not merely a case of offering a meal to appease a hungry ghost.

The metaphysical mechanism is entirely different.

The offering is intended to overcome the very distinction through which the individual remains a bound experiencer.

And that’s why the whole thing is so fascinating.

Because remember our earlier discussions of liberation.

The supreme Self isn’t merely another object to be acquired.

You can’t simply add Śiva to your existing collection of possessions.

“I’ve got a house.”

“A dog.”

“A bicycle.”

“And now I’ve got liberation.”

No.

Liberation concerns the recognition of the supreme nature of consciousness.

And within this ritual, that recognition is enacted through the transformation of the relationship between enjoyer and enjoyed.

The limited power of objectivity is offered into the unlimited conscious subject.

And the result is understood as:

Patiḥ eva bhavati.

One becomes the Lord himself.

Now, the claim that this operation liberates a deceased person is religious doctrine, not a verified effect of a physical food offering.

But the philosophical structure is remarkably consistent with everything we’ve been studying.

The apparent separation between subject and object belongs to manifestation.

And the supreme consciousness is not ultimately confined by that separation.

So the śrāddha is not merely feeding the dead.

It’s an initiation directed toward the overcoming of the deceased individual’s condition of bondage.

Absolutely magnificent.

And now Abhinavagupta asks the very question we’ve been wondering about.

If one ritual is sufficient, why perform several?

8. If one initiation works, why bother with the others?
Right.

We’ve now encountered three major death-related rites.

Antyeṣṭi.

The final sacrifice or funeral.

Mṛtoddharaṇa.

The raising or liberation of the deceased.

Śrāddha-dīkṣā.

The initiation associated with the memorial offering.

And Abhinavagupta says something rather important.

Any one of them may accomplish the intended purpose.

Anyatamena api kṛtārthatā.

Let’s understand that.

Anyatama — any one among several.

Api — even.

Kṛtārthatā — the condition of having fulfilled the intended goal.

So, within the ritual’s claimed efficacy, any one of the procedures may fulfil the purpose.

Now you might ask:

“Abhinava, if the funeral has already liberated the deceased, why perform the śrāddha afterward?”

Excellent question.

Because there are different aims.

And, as usual, we’re back to the distinction between:

Bhoga.

Experience or enjoyment.

And:

Mokṣa.

Liberation.

Remember Chapter Eleven?

Some individuals seek immediate recognition.

Others retain desires for particular experiences.

And the initiatory system accommodates these different dispositions.

So Abhinavagupta says that, for someone desiring enjoyment:

Bubhukṣu.

The multiplication of ritual activities is associated with a multiplication of fruits.

Kriyā-bhūyastvaṃ phala-bhūyastvāya.

Now that’s rather easy to understand.

Kriyā — action, ritual activity.

Bhūyastva — greater abundance.

Phala — fruit, result.

So:

More ritual operations for a greater abundance of results.

Now imagine our officiating guru.

“Right, we’ve performed the funeral.”

“Excellent.”

“And the deceased has received the intended benefit.”

“Wonderful.”

“But he also desired certain experiences.”

“Ah.”

“And there are further rites associated with those results.”

“Right.”

“How many?”

“Several.”

“Bloody hell.”

But here’s the subtle point.

The repetition is not necessarily because the earlier ritual failed.

It’s because different rites can be associated with different fruits, particularly for the enjoyment-seeker.

And Abhinavagupta then applies a corresponding consideration to the liberation-seeker.

But now the purpose is different.

Not multiplying heavenly pleasures.

Not acquiring an impressive catalogue of postmortem experiences.

Instead:

Tanmayībhāva-siddhi.

The attainment of identity with that supreme reality.

And he compares the repeated observance to something we’ve encountered before.

The daily religious practice of a living aspirant.

Jīvataḥ pratyaham anuṣṭhānābhyāsavat.

Like the daily practice performed during life.

Now, that’s a beautiful comparison.

Because the repeated rite has the character of sustained orientation toward the divine.

Not merely the mechanical production of additional objects of enjoyment.

And here’s the interesting philosophical point.

An action may be considered complete in its immediate function while repeated worship continues to have a role in establishing identification with Śiva.

So the ritual system accommodates both possibilities.

For the enjoyment-seeker, an abundance of rites may support an abundance of desired fruits.

For the liberation-oriented context, repeated observance is connected with the establishment of divine identification.

But now comes something absolutely astonishing.

Because Abhinavagupta has spent the entire chapter explaining funerals.

The initiation of the dead.

The trembling corpse.

The final oblation.

The memorial offerings.

Monthly and annual observances.

And then, with almost unbelievable confidence, he says:

If the person was a genuine knower of reality, none of this is needed.

Not one bloody bit.

And this is where the chapter becomes truly beautiful.

9. The genuine knower needs no funeral initiation
Here’s the opening of the next passage.

ATTESTED PROSE — 19.6

तत्त्वज्ञानिनस्तु न कोऽप्ययमन्त्येष्ट्यादिश्राद्धान्तो विधिरुपयोगी
Tattvajñāninas tu na ko’py ayam antyeṣṭyādiśrāddhānto vidhir upayogī.

Now let’s translate this properly.

Tattva — reality, truth or fundamental principle.

Jñānin — a knower.

So:

Tattvajñānin.

A knower of reality.

Not merely someone who has read a few philosophical texts.

Not merely someone who has memorised the thirty-six tattvas.

Someone possessing the knowledge of reality itself.

And what does Abhinavagupta say?

For such a person, none of the rites extending from the funeral sacrifice through śrāddha are useful or necessary.

Na kaḥ api … vidhiḥ upayogī.

Not any of these procedures is of use.

Now, that’s absolutely extraordinary.

We’ve spent multiple chapters discussing how the bonds of the deceased are to be removed.

How the subtle individual is to be joined to Paramaśiva.

How the object-power associated with the deceased is offered into the supreme Enjoyer.

And now Abhinavagupta says:

“Well, if they already possess the knowledge, there’s no need.”

Why?

Because the rites are meant to overcome a limitation.

And the knower has already recognised the reality whose concealment constituted that limitation.

Now, remember Chapter One.

The difference between:

Pauruṣājñāna.

The fundamental contracted condition.

And:

Bauddhājñāna.

Ignorance at the level of the understanding.

And the role of true knowledge in overcoming both.

Well, here that earlier teaching reaches a remarkable conclusion.

If someone possesses the knowledge of the supreme reality, the death of the body doesn’t create a new ignorance that must be removed by another initiation.

The body dies.

But the supreme nature of consciousness wasn’t created by the body.

And isn’t destroyed by its death.

So there is no need to produce through ritual a liberation that has already been attained.

Now imagine the disciples.

Their guru has died.

Someone begins arranging the full funeral initiation.

And another disciple says:

“Hang on.”

“What?”

“Wasn’t our guru a genuine knower of reality?”

“Yes.”

“Didn’t he teach us that consciousness is unrestricted?”

“Yes.”

“Didn’t he establish recognition of the supreme Self?”

“Yes.”

“Then what exactly are we trying to liberate him from?”

Ah.

There it is.

The genuine knower doesn’t need to be made Śiva through a funeral.

And Abhinavagupta isn’t saying the body shouldn’t be treated respectfully.

Nor is he forbidding all forms of remembrance or communal observance.

He’s making a specific claim about the lack of soteriological necessity of these death rites for an already liberated knower.

But then comes something even more extraordinary.

Because the guru’s death isn’t merely something the disciples observe.

Abhinavagupta says the event becomes a festival.

A sacred occasion.

And the reason is astonishing.

10. When a real master dies, the lineage celebrates
Now we encounter:

तन्मरणं तद्विद्यासन्तानिनां पर्वदिनम्

Tan-maraṇaṃ tad-vidyā-santānināṃ parvadinam.

Let’s break this down.

Tan-maraṇam — that person’s death.

Tad-vidyā — their knowledge, the knowledge transmitted by that person.

Santāninām — those belonging to the continuity or lineage of that knowledge.

And:

Parvadinam.

A festival day.

A sacred observance day.

Now, that’s absolutely beautiful.

Because the disciples of a genuine knower are instructed to understand the teacher’s death as a sacred occasion.

But why?

The Sanskrit gives the reason:

Saṃvidaṃśapūraṇa.

The filling or completion of a portion of consciousness.

Now, this is a subtle expression.

Let’s unpack it.

Saṃvid — consciousness.

Aṃśa — part, portion, aspect.

Pūraṇa — filling, completion.

So the idea is that the event contributes to the fullness of consciousness within the lineage.

And then comes the really important metaphysical explanation.

एकसंविन्मात्रपरमार्थत्वात्

Eka-saṃvin-mātra-paramārthatvāt.

Because its ultimate reality is nothing but one consciousness.

Now, read that again.

The ultimate reality of the spiritual lineage is one consciousness.

That’s the point.

Not merely a collection of independent minds exchanging philosophical information.

Not merely a teacher and some students forming an organisation.

The guru and the lineage are understood as expressions of one conscious reality.

And that means the death of the teacher can be contemplated in an entirely different way.

The teacher’s body may no longer be present.

But the consciousness that constituted the essential reality of the teacher is not, within this philosophy, an independent object that has simply been annihilated.

And the knowledge transmitted to the disciples is not merely a physical possession that disappears with the body.

The lineage continues.

And its essential reality is:

Eka-saṃvid.

One consciousness.

Now, consider how radical that is.

Ordinarily, you might think:

“The guru is gone.”

“The person who taught us has disappeared.”

“We’ve lost the source of the teaching.”

And, at the human level, the grief of losing someone is perfectly real.

Abhinavagupta isn’t giving a command to suppress grief.

He’s providing a metaphysical interpretation of the teacher’s death.

The guru’s essential reality is not confined to the body.

And the disciples’ relationship with the knowledge need not end because the teacher has died.

So the day becomes:

Parvadina.

A sacred occasion.

And Abhinavagupta compares it to another extremely important day in the life of the lineage.

The day on which knowledge was received from the teacher while alive.

Jñānalābha.

The attainment or reception of knowledge.

Now, that’s a magnificent comparison.

Imagine the day you first received a teaching that genuinely transformed your understanding.

Not merely a new idea.

But a profound recognition.

Perhaps the nature of aham.

Or svātantrya.

Or the non-difference between Śiva and Śakti.

A day that becomes a landmark in the unfolding of your spiritual life.

Abhinavagupta is saying that the death of the realised teacher can become another such occasion within the lineage.

Not because death itself is being celebrated as a desirable event.

But because the continuity of conscious knowledge is being affirmed.

And that’s absolutely beautiful.

The transmission doesn’t end with the body.

Within Trika’s nondual understanding, the ultimate reality of both teacher and disciple remains the same consciousness.

Now, don’t turn this into a claim that every deceased guru is guaranteed to communicate supernatural messages to their disciples.

That is not what the passage establishes.

The claim concerns the metaphysical unity of the knowledge-lineage and the significance of the realised teacher’s death within it.

And here’s the implication.

The genuine teacher’s death does not require a ritual to make them liberated.

Instead, the lineage can honour that day as an occasion that deepens its own participation in the knowledge.

It’s not a funeral intended to rescue the guru.

It’s a sacred commemoration of a reality the guru has already recognised.

And notice how neatly that fits the entire work.

The goal isn’t to preserve a particular body indefinitely.

The goal is to recognise the consciousness that was never limited to that body.

Absolutely bloody magnificent.

Now, Abhinavagupta has one final practical note before the closing verse.

And this one concerns the most important element in the śrāddha ritual.

11. The central worship is Mūrtiyāga
Right.

We’ve discussed the funeral initiation.

The ancestral offerings.

And the special case of the liberated teacher.

Now Abhinavagupta returns to the ritual procedure and says:

मूर्तियागः प्रधानम्

Mūrtiyāgaḥ pradhānam.

The worship or sacrificial rite of the divine form is primary.

Now, let’s break that down.

Mūrti — a form, embodiment or manifestation.

Yāga — worship or sacrificial rite.

Pradhāna — principal, chief, primary.

So:

Mūrtiyāga is the principal element.

Now, don’t immediately reduce mūrti here to a stone statue sitting on a shelf.

The term belongs to a technical tantric ritual context involving divine forms and their worship.

And Abhinavagupta gives the scriptural authority.

Śrī Siddhāmata.

The venerable Siddhāmata Tantra.

Now that’s an important source in the Śaiva tantric tradition.

And Abhinavagupta attributes this teaching concerning the primacy of mūrtiyāga to it.

But there’s a slight problem.

We haven’t yet received the detailed procedure.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Tadvidhiś ca vakṣyate naimittikaprakāśane.

Its procedure will be explained in the exposition of occasional rites.

Now, that is rather typical.

We’ve reached the end of a chapter discussing death and the liberation of the deceased.

And just as you’d like a complete account of the most important ritual element, Abhinavagupta announces:

“I’ll explain that later.”

Thanks, mate.

Very helpful.

But remember, the Tantrasāra is a condensed exposition.

It frequently directs us toward discussions elsewhere rather than repeating every technical detail.

And here the important point is simply the stated priority of mūrtiyāga, based on the authority of the Siddhāmata.

We shouldn’t invent a full procedure that this chapter doesn’t supply.

Now, that completes all seven prose passages.

The GRETIL transcription ends there.

But another Sanskrit witness preserves one concluding verse.

And it is absolutely magnificent.

Because it gathers up everything we’ve discussed about grace, initiation, ritual means and the freedom of Śiva.

12. The actual concluding verse — even a limited means can reveal unlimited Śiva
ATTESTED CLOSING VERSE — CHAPTER NINETEEN

अनुग्रहपरः शिवो वशितयानुगृह्णाति यं स एव परमेश्वरीभवति नाम किं वाद्भुतम्।उपायपरिकल्पना ननु तदीशनामात्रकं विदन्निति न शङ्कते परिमितेऽप्युपाये बुधः॥
IAST TRANSLITERATION

Anugrahaparaḥ śivo vaśitayānugṛhṇāti yaṃ sa eva parameśvarībhavati nāma kiṃ vādbhutam.

Upāyaparikalpanā nanu tadīśanāmātrakaṃ vidann iti na śaṅkate parimite’py upāye budhaḥ.

This stanza is attested in the Devanāgarī Chapter 19 edition and translated in the Chakravarty–Marjanovic edition. The GRETIL transcription of the 1918 Sanskrit edition ends after the mūrtiyāga prose sentence, so the verse is attested but not present in every checked witness.

Right.

Let’s actually understand the Sanskrit.

Because this is one of those verses that can sound rather straightforward until you examine what it’s saying.

First:

Anugrahaparaḥ Śivaḥ.

Śiva, whose orientation is toward grace.

Anugraha — grace, favour, liberating blessing.

Para — devoted to, intent upon, directed toward.

So:

Śiva who is intent upon bestowing grace.

And then:

Vaśitayā anugṛhṇāti yam.

Whomever he favours through his power of mastery.

Now, vaśitā is an important term.

It concerns sovereignty, mastery, the capacity to bring something under one’s power.

Here, that power belongs to Śiva.

Not the little ego wishing to control everything around it.

The supreme sovereignty through which manifestations arise.

Then:

Sa eva parameśvarībhavati.

That very individual becomes the Supreme Lord.

Sa eva — that very one.

Parameśvarībhavati — becomes or attains the condition of Parameśvara.

And then comes the wonderfully cheeky expression:

Kiṃ vā adbhutam?

What’s so astonishing about that?

Now, that is absolutely magnificent.

Because Abhinavagupta has spent nineteen chapters explaining the nature of Śiva’s supreme freedom.

The manifestation of the universe.

The contraction of consciousness.

The different means of recognition.

The descent of grace.

The entire initiatory system.

And now he asks:

Why should it surprise you that the person favoured by Śiva becomes Śiva?

That’s the whole bloody point.

The apparent individual isn’t ultimately a separate reality standing outside consciousness.

So liberation isn’t a foreign substance being inserted into them.

It’s the removal of the condition through which the supreme reality appeared contracted.

Now comes the second half of the verse.

And this is especially important.

Upāya-parikalpanā.

The conception or arrangement of a means.

Upāya — means, method, approach.

Parikalpanā — conception, construction, arrangement.

And Abhinavagupta says that this too belongs to:

Tadīśanā.

His sovereign power or governing will.

In other words:

Even the particular means through which liberation appears belongs to Śiva’s own freedom.

Now, think about what that means.

We’ve encountered so many different methods.

Direct recognition.

Mantra.

Meditation.

Contemplative reasoning.

Sacred bathing.

Fire offerings.

Initiation.

Funeral rites.

Ancestral offerings.

And the elaborate structures through which the guru is said to communicate divine power.

All these are limited means.

They possess particular forms.

Particular conditions.

Particular procedures.

But the ultimate source of their liberating power is not an independent physical mechanism standing outside Śiva.

It is the free manifestation of consciousness itself.

And the verse closes:

Na śaṅkate parimite’py upāye budhaḥ.

The wise person does not doubt, even in the case of a limited means.

Now, that’s beautiful.

Because the means may be limited.

But the ultimate power it expresses is not.

And Abhinavagupta isn’t saying:

“Every ritual anyone invents is automatically effective.”

He’s speaking within his own system of authorised practices and divine grace.

The point is that a limited means need not contradict the unlimited reality it serves.

Śiva can manifest liberating grace through a particular form without becoming confined to that form.

And that brings us straight back to the funeral.

A body.

A flame.

A mantra.

A food offering.

A ritual action.

All particular.

All limited.

But within the tradition’s theology, these can become means through which the unrestricted power of Śiva operates.

And for the genuine knower?

The means is no longer required for liberation.

Because the recognition has already occurred.

Now, that’s an extraordinary way to close the chapter.

End of Chapter Nineteen — Śrāddhadīkṣāprakāśanam
Now, consider what we’ve just learned.

At the beginning of the chapter, a deceased person is eligible for a final initiatory rite.

Even if they belonged to another religious system.

Even if they previously received initiation but abandoned the appropriate observances.

The condition is the manifestation of divine grace.

Then we encountered the funeral.

The actual corpse.

The mantras installed in the order of dissolution.

The Great Net.

The remarkable claim that the corpse can tremble through the guru’s ritual and yogic power.

And the final oblation that brings the deceased into conjunction with Paramaśiva.

Then the ancestral initiation.

Śrāddha-dīkṣā.

The food offering.

The contemplation of the deceased’s power of objectivity.

And the offering of that power into the supreme Enjoyer.

Through which the condition of limited experience is said to cease.

And then the extraordinary distinction.

For the ordinary bound individual, the funeral and ancestral rites may serve as means of liberation.

But for the person possessing true knowledge?

They are unnecessary.

And the death of such a knower becomes a sacred occasion for the lineage.

Because the ultimate reality of the guru and disciples is:

Eka-saṃvin-mātra.

Nothing but one consciousness.

Now, here’s what I find most beautiful.

Throughout this chapter, Abhinavagupta never treats death as something capable of defeating the ultimate reality of consciousness.

The body can cease.

The familiar personality can disappear from the world.

The rituals can be completed.

But the supreme reality itself isn’t understood as something generated by the body.

And therefore liberation is not the production of an entirely new consciousness after death.

It is the overcoming of the contracted condition through which consciousness appeared limited.

That is the metaphysical framework.

And the funeral rituals are historical expressions of it.

But the most profound distinction remains between the person who still requires a means and the one who has already attained recognition.

The latter is not made liberated by a funeral.

Their death becomes an occasion to recognise and honour the continuing significance of the knowledge they transmitted.

Not necessarily a day without grief.

Not a demand to pretend that loss doesn’t hurt.

But a day on which the lineage remembers that its ultimate reality is not a collection of separate individuals.

It’s consciousness.

Saṃvid.

Now imagine sitting with the disciples of a genuine master.

The teacher has died.

Perhaps everyone is grieving.

Somebody asks:

“Has our guru gone?”

And another replies:

“The body has died.”

“But what about the consciousness he taught us to recognise?”

And now you see why Abhinavagupta calls that day a sacred occasion.

The knowledge is no longer merely being received from a living person standing before you.

It must become firmly established in the disciples themselves.

And the lineage continues.

Not simply through an institution.

Or a collection of books.

Or a particular building.

But through the continuing recognition of one conscious reality.

And that’s the philosophical heart of the chapter.

The funeral doesn’t make Śiva immortal.

Śiva’s nature was never subject to death in the first place.

The rite is directed toward the apparent individual who remains bound.

But for the genuine knower, the purpose of such a rite has already been fulfilled.

So there you are.

Nineteen chapters into the Tantrasāra.

We’ve studied the absolute nature of consciousness.

The emergence of the universe.

The limitations of individuality.

The different methods of recognition.

The descent of divine grace.

The whole structure of initiation.

And now death itself.

And Abhinavagupta has arrived at this astonishing conclusion.

If Śiva chooses to reveal himself through a limited means, why should the wise person find that surprising?

The means is limited.

The power is not.

And the person who truly recognises that power?

They don’t need to be rescued from death by a bloody funeral ceremony.

They’ve already recognised the nature of the reality they were seeking.

Absolute magnificence.
