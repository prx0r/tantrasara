# Tantrasāra — Chapter 8

अष्टममाह्निकम् — Tattvasvarūpaprakāśanam

The Essential Nature of Reality: or, how Śiva invented absolutely everything, forgot he was doing it, and somehow ended up as a bloke arguing with his own brain.


Right then.

Last chapter we discussed the spatial universe.

Deśādhva.

We started with a little patch of earth, discovered innumerable cosmic eggs, travelled through 118 divine worlds, ascended the hierarchy of reality, and eventually arrived at the immeasurable consciousness of Śiva.

Absolutely magnificent.

But there’s a slight problem.

We’ve now got this enormous cosmic hierarchy.

Thirty-six tattvas.

All these principles.

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

Mind.

Intellect.

Māyā.

Divine knowledge.

Śakti.

Śiva.

And you’re probably wondering:

“Abhinava, mate, what exactly ARE these things? Are they substances? Categories? Cosmic departments? Who’s making all of this happen?”

Excellent question.

Because today we’re going to investigate the essential nature of the tattvas.

Tattvasvarūpa.

And more importantly, how they arise.

Now, if you’re expecting a straightforward explanation of how one thing produces another, I’ve got bad news.

Abhinavagupta is about to challenge your entire understanding of cause and effect.

And then, once he’s finished dismantling causation, he’s going to explain exactly how Śiva becomes an individual with limited knowledge, attachment, a body, five senses and a rather unfortunate habit of thinking he’s separate from everything.

Let’s begin.

1. What actually makes something a tattva?
Right.

Remember all those worlds we discussed?

Bhuvana.

Countless divine realms.

Countless experiencers.

Countless objects of experience.

Bhoktṛ.

The enjoyer.

Bhogya.

That which is enjoyed or experienced.

Now look at this enormous collection of worlds.

What do they all have in common?

Well, ultimately there’s something present throughout the entire collection.

A single great luminosity.

Mahāprakāśa.

The great light of consciousness.

And because this luminosity pervades absolutely everything, we call it:

Mahāsāmānya.

The great universal.

And what is this universal reality?

Paramaśiva.

The supreme Śiva.

Now, don’t imagine this is a universal property like all chairs having something in common.

We’re not merely classifying objects.

We’re speaking of the conscious reality through which all these worlds and their distinctions appear.

But suppose something pervades only a particular group of things.

Not absolutely everything.

Just some particular class of manifestations.

Well, that is what we’re calling a:

Tattva.

A principle of reality.

For example:

Pṛthivī.

Earth.

Now, the earth tattva isn’t merely that handful of dirt you picked up in the garden.

It’s the principle characterised by qualities such as solidity, hardness and grossness.

And this principle pervades the entire range of worlds associated with it, from Kālagni to Vīrabhadra.

So we’ve got two levels.

The universal luminosity pervading absolutely everything.

And particular principles pervading particular ranges of manifestation.

Right.

Now here’s the question.

How does one tattva produce another?

Ah.

Now we’re getting into trouble.

2. There are two kinds of causation
You probably think you already understand causation.

You plant a seed.

A plant grows.

You heat water.

It boils.

You knock over your cup.

Tea everywhere.

And you conclude:

“Well, the first thing caused the second.”

Perfectly sensible.

But Abhinavagupta says we need to distinguish two kinds of causation.

First:

Pāramārthika-kāryakāraṇabhāva.

Causation in the ultimate sense.

Second:

Kalpita-kāryakāraṇabhāva.

Constructed or conventionally established causation.

Now, this isn’t quite the same as saying:

“One is real and the other is just fake.”

Because the conventional sequence is itself manifested through divine freedom.

But there is a fundamental distinction.

Let’s begin with ultimate causation.

And here’s the extraordinary teaching.

The supreme Lord manifests himself as the entire hierarchy of reality.

From Śiva-tattva right down to earth.

Śivādi-dharānta.

Everything.

And these manifested forms are both distinct in appearance from his essential nature and resting within that very nature.

Now listen carefully.

Śiva doesn’t take some foreign material and start assembling the universe.

There’s no foreign material.

Nothing independently existing outside him.

He manifests himself through his own freedom.

Svātantrya.

And because he’s inherently the agent, kartṛ, this manifestation is his own activity.

So the ultimate causal relationship is this:

Free consciousness manifests itself as everything, while everything remains grounded in free consciousness.

That’s the real causal foundation.

Now.

What about ordinary causation?

The seed and the sprout?

The fire and the smoke?

The potter and the pot?

Ah.

That brings us to a rather interesting argument.

3. A seed doesn’t necessarily need to make a sprout
Right.

Suppose I plant a seed.

A sprout appears.

You say:

“The seed caused the sprout.”

Very good.

But what exactly establishes that particular causal relationship?

Why must a sprout follow a seed?

Why must this cause precede that effect?

Abhinavagupta says the fixed ordering of cause and effect is manifested through:

Niyati.

The power of determination.

And niyati itself operates through the will of the supreme Lord.

Parameśecchā.

So the regularities of conventional causation are not independent laws standing above Śiva.

They’re expressions of his freedom.

Now, he gives a rather provocative example.

A sprout might arise through the will of a yogin.

Yogīcchā.

And in ordinary experience a sprout arises from a seed.

But in dreams, other arrangements of things can appear.

A pot, for example, might appear without the ordinary waking sequence of clay, wheel and potter.

Now, Abhinavagupta isn’t claiming that ordinary plants routinely sprout without seeds.

He’s challenging the idea that one particular causal sequence is metaphysically necessary in every conceivable manifestation.

Within the ordinary world, the sequence is established.

But that sequence itself depends upon divine freedom.

And the ultimate causal foundation remains the same, whether the appearance follows an ordinary sequence or an extraordinary one.

Śiva’s creative agency doesn’t disappear just because a particular event has an apparent intermediate cause.

Now, let’s take an even more familiar example.

A pot.

And its potter.

4. The potter thinks he’s the one making the pot
Imagine a potter.

Lovely fellow.

He’s got his clay.

His wheel.

His tools.

His little workshop.

And he’s making a pot.

Very impressive.

You ask:

“Who made this?”

And he says:

“I did.”

Fair enough.

But Abhinavagupta has a question.

What exactly does the potter contribute that is independent of the whole universe?

His body?

That’s part of the universe.

His hands?

Part of the universe.

The clay?

Part of the universe.

The wheel?

Also part of the universe.

His knowledge of pottery?

That’s an expression of consciousness.

His intentions?

Also consciousness.

Even the circumstances that brought him into the workshop are part of the larger arrangement.

So where exactly are we going to locate this completely independent potter?

Nowhere.

And what’s especially funny is that the potter’s conviction:

“I am the maker of this pot”

is itself something manifested through the supreme Lord.

The potter’s agency is real within the conventional arrangement.

But it isn’t ultimately independent.

The potter’s consciousness isn’t something greater than or separate from supreme consciousness.

And his body belongs to the totality of manifested circumstances.

So Abhinavagupta concludes that, ultimately, the maker of the pot is:

Parameśvara.

The supreme Lord.

Not because the human potter is irrelevant.

But because the potter, his knowledge, the materials and the entire causal arrangement are expressions of one cosmic agency.

And now he makes an important refinement.

The immediate cause of an ordinary effect is not some isolated little object.

It’s the entire relevant complex of conditions.

Sāmagrī.

The causal complex.

Imagine making a pot.

You’ve got clay.

Water.

A wheel.

Heat.

A particular skill.

A particular environment.

Change the arrangement sufficiently, and the result changes.

Abhinavagupta gives the example of Mount Meru.

A pot of precisely the same kind wouldn’t arise there merely because a potter and some clay happened to be present.

The complete circumstances matter.

And because every causal complex exists within the totality of consciousness, even this more sophisticated account of causation ultimately supports the agency of the supreme Lord.

Now consider something else.

Different scriptures describe different causal relationships between the tattvas.

One says this principle emerges from that.

Another arranges the sequence differently.

Should we panic?

Absolutely not.

Because at the conventional level, different causal sequences can be manifested.

Abhinavagupta even invokes traditional examples of scorpions arising from cow dung, worms, the will of yogins, mantras or medicinal substances.

Rather ambitious biological claims.

We’re not endorsing medieval zoology here.

We’re understanding the point he’s making.

A particular causal sequence is not the ultimate source of reality.

The ultimate source is divine freedom.

And now that we’ve established this, Abhinavagupta says:

Right.

Let’s explain the sequence according to our own Tantra.

And this is where the entire thirty-six-tattva system starts unfolding.

5. The five pure tattvas — Śiva’s five powers
Remember what I explained in Chapter One?

Parameśvara possesses five principal powers.

Cit.

Ānanda.

Icchā.

Jñāna.

Kriyā.

Consciousness.

Bliss.

Will.

Knowledge.

Action.

Now, these are not five separate substances.

They’re powers of one supreme consciousness.

But here’s the crucial thing.

Śiva can make any one of these powers predominant.

And depending upon which power predominates, we describe a different tattva.

First:

Śiva-tattva — Cit-śakti
When the power of consciousness predominates, we speak of:

Śiva-tattva.

Pure luminosity.

Cit.

The power of awareness itself.

Now, don’t imagine that the other four powers have disappeared.

They’re still present.

But consciousness is the dominant aspect.

Second:

Śakti-tattva — Ānanda-śakti
When bliss predominates:

Śakti-tattva.

The fullness of delight.

Ānanda.

Not excitement about getting something you wanted.

Not pleasure dependent upon an external object.

The inherent fullness of unrestricted consciousness.

Third:

Sadāśiva-tattva — Icchā-śakti
Now we have the predominance of will.

Sadāśiva.

Icchā.

And Abhinavagupta gives an interesting reason.

Will represents a balanced acceptance of the powers of knowledge and action.

The intention preceding their more explicit differentiation.

Consciousness is oriented toward manifestation, but knowledge and action have not yet predominated separately.

Fourth:

Īśvara-tattva — Jñāna-śakti
Now knowledge predominates.

Īśvara.

Jñāna.

The power of explicit apprehension.

And fifth:

Śuddhavidyā-tattva — Kriyā-śakti
Finally:

Śuddhavidyā.

Pure knowledge, but here specifically associated with the predominance of:

Kriyā-śakti.

The power of action.

The unfolding of differentiated manifestation within the pure divine domain.

Mantreśvaras.

Mantras.

And together they constitute:

Śuddhādhva.

The pure cosmic path.

Now here’s the crucial distinction.

Throughout this pure domain, Śiva is directly the creator.

But something is about to happen.

We’re going to leave the pure path.

And enter:

Aśuddhādhva.

The impure or differentiated path.

And to understand why that happens, we need to investigate something rather uncomfortable.

Because apparently the infinite Lord has developed a peculiar fascination with being incomplete.

6. The great cosmic problem: Śiva wants to experience being incomplete
Right.

Now we enter the impure path.

And the presiding creator here is a divine being called:

Aghoreśa.

Also known as:

Ananta.

He operates according to the will of Īśvara.

And what is he creating?

A world of differentiated experience for limited subjects.

Aṇus.

The contracted individuals.

Now here’s the question.

Why do these individuals require such a world?

Because they’re disturbed by a peculiar longing for experience.

Bhoga-lolikā.

An eagerness to enjoy or experience something.

And Abhinavagupta explains that this longing involves:

Apūrṇammanyatā.

The sense of oneself as incomplete.

Now let’s look at that word.

Apūrṇa — incomplete, not full.

Manyatā — considering or regarding something in a particular way.

So:

Considering oneself incomplete.

That’s the problem.

Not necessarily being metaphysically incomplete.

But experiencing oneself as though something were missing.

Imagine infinite consciousness.

Completely free.

Entirely full.

Nothing outside it.

Nothing it needs to acquire.

And somehow it comes to experience:

“Hmm.”

“Something’s missing.”

“Perhaps I ought to get something.”

Absolutely extraordinary.

What the bloody hell could infinite consciousness be missing?

Nothing.

But that sense of deficiency is the beginning of the contracted individual’s desire for experience.

Now, Abhinavagupta makes a subtle distinction.

This first longing isn’t yet a particular action directed toward a particular object.

It’s simply an urge.

Abhilāṣa-mātra.

Bare desire.

An orientation toward a possible future limitation.

And that fundamental condition is connected with:

Mala.

Impurity.

But not impurity in the sense that you’ve got dirt on your soul.

And not yet another tattva that we’ve forgotten to count.

Mala here is the concealment of fullness.

The condition in which consciousness appears diminished.

Now, distinguish this from:

Rāga-tattva.

Attachment.

Rāga is already a more particular form of desire, conditioned by activity.

You aren’t merely experiencing some vague sense of incompleteness.

You’re now wanting something.

“I want that.”

“I want to do this.”

“I need to experience that.”

The vague sense of deficiency has become directed toward particular possibilities.

And karma — activity — is another matter again.

We’ll explain the relationships properly when we reach the lower tattvas.

But here’s the extraordinary claim.

Mala is not some foreign substance that has invaded Śiva.

It arises through the supreme Lord’s own will to conceal his nature.

Svātma-pracchādana-icchā.

The desire to conceal himself.

Now, that’s going to upset anyone who imagines bondage must have been inflicted upon consciousness by something outside it.

There’s nothing outside Śiva capable of imprisoning him.

The contraction itself belongs to his freedom.

But we need to distinguish the conditions of different limited beings.

7. Some souls are nearly free; others are still absolutely tangled
Now Abhinavagupta introduces two classes of limited subject.

First:

Vijñānākala.

A being whose condition of impurity is moving toward dissolution.

The fundamental limitation hasn’t necessarily been completely removed.

But it is no longer nourishing fresh karmic involvement in the same way.

Then:

Pralayākala.

A being in a condition associated with cosmic dissolution.

Here mala remains active or capable of further expansion.

And when strengthened by it, karma becomes a cause of the extraordinary diversity of saṃsāric experiences.

Pleasure.

Pain.

Different births.

Different circumstances.

All the varied conditions of limited existence.

Now, because these beings retain tendencies toward experience, the divine creator manifests the appropriate world.

The world is the field in which their dispositions and karmic conditions can unfold.

But there’s another important point.

Mala itself doesn’t suddenly wake up and start manufacturing karma.

It’s inert.

Jaḍa.

It has no independent power of action.

So what activates this entire process?

Īśvarecchā.

The will of the Lord.

Again.

Abhinavagupta keeps bringing us back to the same principle.

No matter how complicated the cosmic machinery becomes, the ultimate power of manifestation belongs to supreme consciousness.

And now we’re going to define the aṇu itself.

8. The individual has a rather peculiar double nature
What exactly is an individual soul?

An aṇu?

Abhinavagupta says it appears in two aspects.

Cit.

Conscious.

And:

Acit.

Nonconscious.

Now this is a fascinating distinction.

The conscious aspect is:

Aiśvarya.

Lordship.

The divine nature.

The power belonging to consciousness itself.

And the nonconscious aspect?

That is associated with mala.

Limitation.

So the individual isn’t some entirely different substance from Śiva.

Its conscious reality is divine.

But it appears accompanied by a condition of insentience or limitation.

And because Śiva freely manifests such a condition, there arises a principle capable of serving as the material basis of further differentiated manifestations.

And that principle is:

Māyā-tattva.

Now, be very careful.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to distinguish two things that have the same name.

First, there’s Māyā as a tattva.

An insentient, pervasive causal principle.

The material basis, upādāna-kāraṇa, of the lower manifestation.

And then there’s Māyā as Śiva’s power.

The divine Śakti that manifests this tattva.

Those are not identical in function.

The first is an insentient principle within manifestation.

The second is the conscious divine power through which that principle appears.

Do you see why that matters?

Because otherwise you’d hear that Māyā is inert and immediately conclude that Śiva’s power is somehow unconscious.

Absolutely not.

Māyā-tattva is a manifestation of the conscious power, not the ultimate nature of that power.

And Abhinavagupta says a similar twofold understanding should be applied throughout the lower tattvas, from kalā down to earth.

Each has its particular manifested character, while its reality is grounded in Śiva’s creative power.

Now, at this point he invokes the teaching of his own gurus.

And it’s rather provocative.

9. Even things imagined in consciousness have a mode of manifestation
His teachers give an argument concerning things that appear in conceptual imagination.

Saṃkalpa.

Take a pot.

You can imagine a pot.

And a pot can also appear in an externally differentiated, perceptible form.

The teachers extend this reasoning to subtle principles such as Māyā, kalā, prakṛti and buddhi.

Even to the classical example of an impossible thing:

A sky-flower.

Kha-puṣpa.

Now, this isn’t a sensible excuse to claim that every imaginary object can be found physically sitting in the garden.

The argument concerns different modes of appearance and the relation between what is contemplated and what can be manifested.

And here’s the practical implication.

A yogin who attains direct awareness of these subtle principles may be considered accomplished.

Siddha.

Even if their object is Māyā, kalā, prakṛti or buddhi rather than a gross physical thing.

Because direct contemplative access to these principles has significance within this cosmology.

Right.

Now we’ve established the material basis of manifestation.

Māyā.

And from Māyā, the world unfolds.

Viśvaprasava.

The birth of the universe.

But before we go any further, there’s another question.

Does all of this happen in an actual chronological sequence?

10. Does Śiva create everything one thing at a time?
Right.

Here’s the difficulty.

We’re about to say:

Māyā produces kalā.

Kalā produces limited knowledge.

Then attachment.

Time.

Determination.

And so forth.

But didn’t we establish earlier that supreme consciousness isn’t constrained by time?

How can the ultimate creative act require one thing to exist before another?

Excellent.

Now you’re paying attention.

Abhinavagupta says that in its ultimate nature, creation is:

Akrama.

Nonsequential.

But it can nevertheless appear as:

Krama.

Sequential.

And that’s the important distinction.

The hierarchy is an intelligible ordering of manifestation.

It doesn’t mean Śiva has to wait for one cosmic factory to finish manufacturing a tattva before he can start the next production line.

Moreover, different individuals possess different limiting conditions.

The collection of principles beginning with kalā can differ from one individual to another.

Yet through divine will, these powers may also be brought into a shared arrangement.

Like people participating in one gathering.

Different individual experiences, but a common manifested circumstance.

Now, here’s something particularly important.

The same limiting powers can function differently depending upon their orientation.

If they operate in a way that supports recognition of Parameśvara, they are described as pure.

If they reinforce the contracted experience of saṃsāra, they are impure.

And what brings about the transformative orientation?

Śaktipāta.

The descent of divine power.

We’ll discuss that properly later.

For now, understand that the issue isn’t simply whether you possess particular faculties.

It’s whether those faculties reinforce separateness or serve recognition.

And now, at last, we can descend through the specific limiting principles.

This is where Śiva gets his little individual identity.

11. Kalā — from unlimited agency to “I can do a few things”
First:

Kalā-tattva.

The principle of limited agency.

Now remember the absolute power of Śiva.

Kriyā-śakti.

Unrestricted creative action.

No limitation.

Nothing outside its capacity.

But when consciousness assumes the contracted condition of individuality, its agency appears restricted.

And kalā provides the limited individual with:

Kiṃcit-kartṛtva.

The capacity to do something.

Let’s break that down.

Kiṃcit — something, a little, a particular amount.

Kartṛtva — agency, the condition of being an agent.

So:

A little bit of agency.

Quite a downgrade.

Imagine going from:

“I freely manifest the entire universe.”


“I reckon I can make a sandwich.”

And even that’s assuming you’ve got bread.

Now, how does kalā arise?

Through the conjunction of Māyā and the contracted individual.

And Abhinavagupta makes a crucial distinction.

The individual consciousness isn’t intrinsically altered.

Rather, Māyā undergoes the modification through which limited agency appears.

The conscious principle remains, in its essence, immutable.

But its condition of manifestation is now different.

The result is such intimate conjunction between the individual and kalā that their distinction becomes exceedingly difficult to perceive.

You don’t ordinarily think:

“Ah, here is pure awareness, and here is the limiting principle through which agency appears.”

You simply say:

“I am doing this.”

And take that limited agency to be the entirety of yourself.

Now this brings us to a remarkable discussion of liberation.

12. There are different depths of liberation
Abhinavagupta explains that, through the grace of a divine lord and the exhaustion of karma, different discriminations can arise.

Viveka.

Discernment.

First, you may distinguish the individual consciousness from Māyā.

Māyā-puruṣa-viveka.

This permits the condition of the Vijñānākala, above Māyā.

Or you may distinguish consciousness from kalā.

Kalā-puṃ-viveka.

And rest above that limiting principle.

Or distinguish puruṣa from prakṛti.

Prakṛti-puruṣa-viveka.

And no longer wander within the domain governed by primordial nature.

Now here’s the really important distinction.

Even these liberations are understood according to the particular limitation that has been overcome.

If mala itself is distinguished from puruṣa, there’s an attainment comparable to Śiva.

Śiva-samānatva.

But when the fullness of puruṣa is directly recognised?

Puruṣa-pūrṇatā-dṛṣṭi.

Then:

Śivatvam eva.

Śiva-hood itself.

Not merely escape from one particular realm.

Not merely freedom from some limited condition.

The recognition of fullness.

And notice the recurring principle.

Liberation isn’t about running higher and higher up the cosmic ladder indefinitely.

It’s about recognition of the supreme nature of consciousness.

Right.

Back to our poor little individual.

We’ve given him limited agency.

But there’s a problem.

You can’t do anything intelligently if you don’t know anything.

So we’re going to need another tattva.

13. Aśuddhavidyā — congratulations, you now know three things
Now comes:

Aśuddhavidyā.

Impure or limited knowledge.

And the important function is:

Kiṃcit-jñatva.

Knowing something.

Not everything.

Just a bit.

So we’ve got:

Kalā — I can do something.

Vidyā — I know something.

And here is where the trouble begins.

Because unlimited consciousness doesn’t know things by standing apart from them as an ignorant subject trying to acquire information.

But the contracted individual does.

Now the individual says:

“I know what that is.”

“I don’t know what that is.”

“I understand this.”

“I’ve never heard of that.”

And the power of limited knowledge makes these restricted cognitions possible.

Now, Abhinavagupta introduces something interesting.

Buddhi.

The intellect.

He says limited vidyā apprehends and distinguishes the appearances reflected within buddhi.

Including experiences such as pleasure.

Sukha.

And other forms of experience.

But buddhi itself has a mixed character.

It contains different qualities.

And it cannot, by itself, perform the required discrimination in the way vidyā does.

So here’s the distinction.

Buddhi provides the field in which the object is reflected.

Vidyā makes a limited discrimination of what’s reflected there.

Imagine looking at a cluttered desk.

Books.

Papers.

Cups.

A half-eaten biscuit.

An enormous collection of nonsense.

Buddhi presents the material of cognition.

Vidyā enables particular determinations.

“That’s my book.”

“That’s tea.”

“That’s a biscuit.”

“That biscuit’s been there for three days.”

Now, the point isn’t that these are physically separate machines inside your skull.

They’re analytically distinct principles of cognition.

And notice the limitation.

You know one thing at a time, or some particular collection of things.

You’re not apprehending the universe through unlimited divine knowledge.

Your own power of knowing appears restricted to particular contents.

Right.

We’ve got agency.

We’ve got knowledge.

What’s missing?

Ah.

Desire.

Because apparently the little bloke is about to become dissatisfied with absolutely everything.

14. Rāga — the cosmic invention of wanting stuff
Now we come to:

Rāga-tattva.

Attachment.

Desire.

The principle through which a limited agent is drawn toward particular objects and experiences.

Remember our earlier distinction?

Mala involves the fundamental appearance of incompleteness.

But rāga is already concerned with particular attractions.

The individual doesn’t merely experience:

“Something’s missing.”

Now it’s:

“I want THAT.”

And Abhinavagupta makes a subtle argument.

Rāga cannot simply be reduced to the absence of detachment.

Avairāgya.

Because people can lack detachment and nevertheless not be attracted to every particular object.

And conversely, someone possessing detachment toward one domain may still exhibit attraction elsewhere.

Even a person who’s eaten their fill may retain an underlying disposition toward food.

So attachment cannot simply be explained as:

“Well, you haven’t become detached yet.”

No.

Rāga is a positive principle of attraction within the structure of limited experience.

Now, imagine you’ve just eaten an enormous meal.

Absolutely stuffed.

You couldn’t possibly manage another bite.

Then someone brings out a magnificent dessert.

Suddenly:

“Well, perhaps there’s a little room.”

There he is.

Rāga.

The little bastard.

But within this philosophical system, it’s much more fundamental than liking pudding.

It’s what makes particular objects appear desirable to a subject who experiences itself as incomplete.

And now we’ve got an individual who can act, can know, and wants things.

What’s missing?

A schedule.

15. Kāla — now you’ve got deadlines
Kāla-tattva.

Time.

Remember Chapter Six?

We spent an extraordinary amount of time discussing time.

Rather ironic.

Now we’re considering it as a limiting principle.

And its function is to determine action through temporal restriction.

The individual isn’t merely thinking:

“I can do something.”

Now the individual experiences:

“I can do this now.”

Or:

“I could do that yesterday.”

“Perhaps I’ll do it tomorrow.”

And suddenly you’ve got the entire nightmare of temporal existence.

Past.

Present.

Future.

Things that are no longer available.

Things that haven’t happened.

Things you wish would last.

Things you wish would end.

And within this system, that limitation is connected with kāla.

Now, supreme consciousness is not intrinsically restricted by temporal succession.

Tattvasvarūpa.

And here’s a final thought.

You think:

“I’m this little human being sitting in the universe.”

Abhinavagupta says:

That very thought is the result of an extraordinary series of limiting manifestations.

The actual consciousness through which you think it?

That’s not confined to the little identity you’ve constructed.

And the whole hierarchy we’ve just described?

It is an expression of the freedom of that supreme reality.

Śiva freely manifests the entire universe, including the limited individual who looks around and wonders where Śiva has gone.

The absolute bloody cheek of it.

End of Chapter Eight — Tattvasvarūpaprakāśanam.
