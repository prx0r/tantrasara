# Tantrasāra — Chapter 5

Tantrasāra — Chapter 5
पञ्चममाह्निकम् — Āṇavaprakāśanam

The Method of the Little Bloke: or, congratulations, you’re literally infinite consciousness, but apparently we’re going to need some breathing exercises to convince you.

Right then.

Last chapter, we discussed Śāktopāya.

The method of Śakti.

Beautiful method.

You take your conceptual understanding, refine it through correct reasoning, and gradually all those ridiculous ideas about being a separate little creature start falling apart.

Very sophisticated.

But there’s a problem.

Some of you are absolutely hopeless at it.

You sit there thinking:

“I am infinite consciousness.”

Then:

“But how can I be infinite consciousness if I’m sitting in a chair?”

Then:

“Who is sitting in the chair?”

Then:

“What’s a chair?”

And before you know it, you’ve spent three hours arguing with yourself about furniture.

So clearly, we’re going to need something else.

Welcome to Āṇavopāya.

The method of the aṇu.

The apparently limited individual.

And don’t get offended by the name.

Aṇu means something minute, atomic, tiny.

In this context, it means consciousness appearing in the restricted condition of individuality.

So yes, you’re infinite Śiva.

But you’re experiencing yourself as a tiny bloke with a body and a mind.

And that’s precisely where we’re going to begin.

1. When thinking needs a bit of help
Now, remember what we said about vikalpa.

Conceptual understanding.

In Śāktopāya, correct understanding can refine itself without depending on another practical method.

Through the grace of śuddhavidyā, pure knowledge, thought becomes the expression of the supreme Lord’s own power.

Brilliant.

But suppose your understanding can’t refine itself quite so easily.

Suppose it needs assistance.

Something concrete.

Something you can actually work with.

Then we use supports belonging to your apparently limited condition.

We’ve got four principal domains, expressed through five forms of practice.

Buddhi — the intellect and imaginative faculty, used in dhyāna, meditation.
Prāṇa — vital energy, approached through uccāra and the subtler practice of varṇa, phonemic resonance.
Deha — the body, approached through karaṇa, particular bodily configurations.
Bāhya — external objects, such as ritual vessels, altars and liṅgas, used in worship.
And that’s āṇavopāya.

We’re using limited things to bring about recognition of the unlimited.

Now, you might object.

“Abhinava, mate, didn’t you spend the entire last chapter telling us that Śiva can’t be produced by practices?”

Indeed I did.

And that hasn’t changed.

We’re not making Śiva.

We’re refining the limited individual’s capacity to recognise Śiva.

The method changes. The reality being recognised doesn’t.

And now, let me teach you a particular meditation.

One that I received from my own revered teacher, Śambhunātha.

This one’s absolutely magnificent.

2. The fire-wheel of Mahābhairava
Right.

Let’s recall what we’ve established.

The highest principle is svaprakāśa.

Self-luminous consciousness.

And it pervades all the tattvas.

Every level of manifestation.

From the highest divine principles right down to physical matter.

Now, in this meditation, you’re going to contemplate that supreme reality within the awareness of your own Heart.

Nija-hṛdaya-bodha.

And I don’t mean merely imagining something inside your anatomical heart.

I mean the centre of awareness.

The Heart in which experience manifests.

Now, within that Heart, we’re going to bring together three things.

Agni

Fire — Vahni

Pramātṛ: the knower, the subject of experience.

सूर्य
Sūrya

Sun — Arka

Pramāṇa: the process or means of knowing.

चन्द्र
Candra

Moon — Soma

Prameya: the object that is known.

Now this is extremely important.

Ordinarily, you experience these as separate.

There’s you.

There’s the act of knowing.

And there’s the thing you’re knowing.

For example:

You see a tree.

You’re the observer.

Seeing is the process.

The tree is the object.

Three things.

Simple enough.

But now imagine all three brought together in the Heart of consciousness.

Saṅghaṭṭa.

Their conjunction.

Their coming together.

And as contemplation intensifies, a fire arises.

Not an ordinary fire.

Mahābhairavāgni.

The fire of Great Bhairava.

The wind of meditation has kindled it.

Dhyāna-vāta.

And this fire takes the form of a wheel.

Cakra.

A magnificent wheel surrounded by twelve flames.

Remember those twelve divine powers we discussed in the previous chapter?

The Kālīs?

Well, here they are again.

Twelve flames.

Twelve rays of divine power.

The entire wheel is an expression of Mahābhairava.

And now we’re going to do something absolutely extraordinary.

3. Send the fire-wheel into the world
Imagine you’re looking at something.

A flower, perhaps.

Very nice flower.

Lovely petals.

Beautiful colour.

Nothing particularly unusual.

Now, according to the meditation, the fiery wheel issues forth through the appropriate opening of one of your senses.

If you’re seeing, through the eye.

If you’re hearing, through the ear.

And so forth.

It comes to rest upon the object.

Now the ordinary bloke thinks:

“There’s me, and there’s a flower.”

But the yogin is going to investigate that distinction through the wheel of Bhairava.

And the object is going to undergo four transformations.

First:

Sṛṣṭi — Manifestation.

The moon.

Soma.

The object is filled through the lunar power of emanation.

Its appearing is contemplated as the expression of consciousness.

Not some foreign object mysteriously existing outside awareness.

The flower is manifest.

Second:

Sthiti — Maintenance.

The sun.

Arka.

The flower shines in the radiance of the sustaining power.

It’s present.

It’s being experienced.

Its manifest appearance endures.

Third:

Saṃhāra — Dissolution.

Fire.

Vahni.

Now the wheel dissolves the object.

The differentiated flower is withdrawn into the consciousness from which it appeared.

Gone.

Not because we’ve physically incinerated someone’s garden.

Please don’t set the flowers on fire.

We’re discussing contemplative dissolution.

The flower’s identity as an independently existing object is being relinquished.

And fourth:

Anuttara — The unsurpassable.

The object is contemplated as having attained identity with the highest reality.

No separation.

No independent object standing over against consciousness.

Only the supreme reality within which that object appeared.

Now, listen carefully.

The wheel has become complete through its identity with the totality of external objects.

Samasta-bāhya-vastv-abheda-paripūrṇa.

Filled with nonseparation from everything apparently external.

And you might think:

“Right, lovely. Flower dissolved. Job done.”

Not quite.

Because there’s something you haven’t dealt with.

4. You’ve still got the flower in your head
Ah, yes.

Vāsanā.

The residual impression.

You’ve dissolved the apparent external object, but you still remember it.

You can still imagine the petals.

The colour.

The shape.

And the mind can bring the thing back.

It’s like deciding you’ve finished thinking about someone.

Then lying in bed two hours later having an imaginary argument with them.

The original person isn’t even there.

But the mental impression is having an absolute field day.

So Abhinavagupta says:

Apply the same contemplation to the remaining impressions.

Bring them into the wheel.

The inner residue too undergoes manifestation, maintenance, dissolution and return to the unsurpassable.

Now the process is complete.

The apparent external object and its internal impression have both been recognised within consciousness.

And as the yogin repeatedly contemplates this, something begins to become clear.

The entire sequence of creation, continuation and dissolution consists, in its essential reality, of consciousness.

Svasaṃvin-mātra-paramārtha.

And the freedom through which these things occur?

That belongs to consciousness itself.

Svātantrya.

So the point isn’t to become a talented imaginary pyromaniac.

It’s to recognise that the very power by which things appear, persist and disappear is the power of your own essential awareness.

And when this is recognised fully, Abhinavagupta makes an enormous claim.

Sadya eva bhairavībhāvaḥ.

The state of Bhairava arises immediately.

And through repeated practice, he says, the various desired siddhis or attainments may also be accomplished.

Now we’re getting into traditional tantric claims about extraordinary powers, not something I’m asking you to accept as an established scientific fact.

But the central insight is clear.

The fire-wheel is a means of recognising the unity of the knower, knowing and known.

And Abhinavagupta gives us three summary verses.

Their sense is this:

Bring the self-luminous triad into the blissful Heart.

Contemplate the all-pervading Lord of the wheel, radiating the twelve great powers through the openings of the senses, manifesting and withdrawing external appearances.

Then contemplate every external and internal object as absorbed into that wheel, resting in the Self.

Thus the Self becomes manifest in its fullness.

Right.

That’s dhyāna.

Meditation.

Now we’re going to discuss something even more intimate.

Something you’ve been doing since the day you were born.

Breathing.

And apparently you’ve been doing it without understanding the cosmic implications.

Typical.

5. The five winds of life
Now we’re getting into uccāra.

The elevation of vital energy.

And before you start doing some heroic breathing exercise and passing out on the floor, understand that we’re examining a traditional contemplative process involving prāṇa, awareness and repose.

This isn’t a competition to see how long you can hold your breath.

Right.

We’ve got five forms of vital energy.

Prāṇa.

Apāna.

Samāna.

Udāna.

Vyāna.

And these aren’t simply five different names for oxygen.

Within tantric physiology, they’re five functions of the life-force.

Five movements through which embodied consciousness manifests its activity.

And here’s the extraordinary thing.

Each movement can reveal a different mode of viśrānti.

Repose.

Resting in awareness.

Now watch.

First: Śūnya — the void of the Heart
Before the outward movement begins, the yogin rests in the Heart.

Hṛdaya.

And there, one encounters:

Śūnya.

Openness.

Emptiness.

The absence of a particular object occupying attention.

Now don’t imagine this means you’ve entered some dreadful realm of nothingness.

We’re talking about a condition of repose.

The place from which the movement of prāṇa can be contemplated.

The yogin rests there.

And then the outward movement begins.

Second: Prāṇa — consciousness goes out
The vital energy moves outward.

Attention extends toward an external object.

And consciousness appears to reach out into the world.

You see something.

You hear something.

You encounter something.

Now ordinarily, you think:

“I’m here, and the thing is over there.”

But through this practice, you’re examining how the movement toward the apparent object unfolds within awareness itself.

It’s not simply air leaving your lungs.

It’s a contemplative account of the outward movement of experience.

And through that movement, a particular repose becomes available.

But then comes the return.

Third: Apāna — the moon draws everything inward
Now, apāna.

Associated here with the lunar movement.

Candra.

The inward filling.

And what happens?

The practitioner apprehends the external object within the fullness of consciousness.

The apparent outside is gathered inward.

And then something remarkable occurs.

Sarvātmatā.

The condition of being the Self of all.

One no longer experiences some independently existing other thing that must be acquired to achieve completeness.

Anya-nirākāṅkṣa.

Freedom from longing for something other.

Now that’s an interesting expression.

Because ordinarily you spend your entire life imagining that completeness is somewhere else.

“I’ll be complete when I get this.”

“I’ll be happy when I become that.”

“I just need one more thing.”

And the tradition says:

Mate.

The very reality in which the object appears is already complete.

What are you looking for outside it?

Fourth: Samāna — everything comes together
Now we get samāna.

The equalising vital energy.

And with its arising, the practitioner experiences:

Saṅghaṭṭa-viśrānti.

Repose in conjunction.

The coming together of movements previously experienced as distinct.

Outward and inward.

Subject and object.

The apparent division begins to settle.

Now remember the fire-wheel?

Remember how we brought the knower, knowing and known into conjunction?

We’re approaching that same recognition through another support.

Not imaginative visualisation this time.

Vital energy.

And now something rather dramatic happens.

Fifth: Udāna — the fire devours the whole operation
Here comes udāna.

The ascending vital energy.

And Abhinavagupta associates it with fire.

Udāna-vahni.

Now this fire doesn’t merely dissolve a particular object.

It consumes the very divisions through which cognition occurs.

The knower.

The known.

The operations by which the known is apprehended.

Mātṛ-meyādi-kalanā.

All swallowed.

And don’t imagine that this means the universe must physically vanish.

The point is the dissolution of these divisions as independently existing realities.

The subject is not standing apart from consciousness.

The object is not standing apart from consciousness.

The very act of knowing is not standing apart from consciousness.

The fire of udāna devours their supposed separateness.

And then the fire itself subsides.

What remains?

Sixth: Vyāna — consciousness everywhere
Vyāna.

The pervasive vital energy.

Now the limitations through which consciousness appeared restricted no longer define the experience.

Sarvāvaccheda-vandhya.

Free from all delimitation.

And Abhinavagupta uses a lovely verb here.

Sphurati.

It flashes.

It shines.

It vibrates.

Consciousness shines forth unrestricted by those divisions.

Not sitting in one corner of the body like a little spectator.

Not trapped in some tiny mental chamber.

But apprehended as the all-pervasive reality.

The movement began in the Heart, extended toward the world, returned inward, united its divisions, dissolved them, and culminated in unrestricted awareness.

And now you might ask:

“Abhinava, mate, what does any of this actually feel like?”

Excellent question.

Because apparently there are six kinds of bliss involved.

And I know what you’re thinking.

Six?

Bloody hell.

We’re going to need a bigger vocabulary.

6. The six levels of bliss
Here they are.

Nijānanda.

The bliss of one’s own nature.

Associated with the original repose in the Heart’s openness.

Nija means one’s own, intrinsic, belonging to oneself.

So nijānanda is the bliss inherent in one’s own being.

No external object required.

Not a pleasure supplied from somewhere else.

The bliss of what you are.

Nirānanda.

Now, this one’s interesting because the prefix nir- ordinarily suggests absence or freedom from.

So you might think:

“Hang on, is this the bliss of having no bliss?”

Don’t be ridiculous.

We’re not constructing an ordinary English compound.

This is a technical name for the repose associated with the outward prāṇa movement.

The character of bliss is changing as consciousness moves into manifestation.

Parānanda.

The bliss of the other, or the bliss associated with what appears beyond oneself.

This belongs to the movement of apāna.

And here’s the paradox.

The apparent other is being drawn into the fullness of one’s own consciousness.

So what initially appeared external becomes apprehended within the Self.

Brahmānanda.

The bliss of expansive fullness.

Associated here with samāna and the conjunction of previously differentiated movements.

The word brahman carries the sense of vastness or expansion in its etymological background.

So there’s a magnificent sense of fullness here.

But don’t confuse this technical stage with the unrestricted absolute itself.

We’re still discussing a particular repose within this practice.

Mahānanda.

Great bliss.

Mahā — great.

Ānanda — bliss.

Associated with udāna and its dissolving fire.

The distinctions of knower and known are being consumed.

And the resulting repose is described as a great bliss.

Cidānanda.

The bliss of consciousness.

Cit — consciousness.

Ānanda — bliss.

Associated with vyāna.

Here the pervasive nature of consciousness shines forth.

Now, that’s six.

And you’re probably thinking:

“Lovely, so I’ve just got to collect all six and I’ve completed spiritual enlightenment.”

No.

This isn’t a loyalty card.

Because there’s something that unites all six.

And it’s not merely a seventh stage added to the end.

7. Jagadānanda — the bliss that never comes and goes
Jagadānanda.

The bliss of the entire universe.

Jagat — world, universe.

Ānanda — bliss.

And here’s why this is so important.

All those previous states involve particular movements or forms of repose.

They arise in relation to the stages of uccāra.

But jagadānanda is described as:

Udayāstamaya-vihīna.

Free from rising and setting.

Think about that.

No beginning.

No disappearance.

Not something that arrives when you’ve performed your breathing exercises correctly and leaves because someone slams a door.

It’s the single connecting reality of those different reposes.

The underlying repose of consciousness itself.

Antar-viśrānti.

And now we’ve arrived at the actual purpose of the practice.

Because through resting in these stages individually, in combinations, or together, the practitioner discovers something beyond identification with the particular body, breath and other limited supports.

Viśrānti-tattva.

The reality of repose itself.

The practice began with prāṇa.

But prāṇa was never the final destination.

The movements of vital energy serve to refine understanding until the practitioner recognises the consciousness in which those movements occur.

And that, says Abhinavagupta, is also the secret of the seed syllables associated with creation and dissolution.

We’ll come back to those shortly.

But first, he’s got something else to explain.

Apparently, as this practice deepens, some rather strange experiences can occur.

Now, I want you to understand something.

These are traditional descriptions of states associated with contemplative absorption.

They’re not a checklist of symptoms you ought to deliberately induce.

And experiencing trembling or dizziness by itself certainly doesn’t establish spiritual attainment.

Right.

First state.

Ānanda.

Bliss.

This arises from touching an aspect of supreme fullness.

Pūrṇatā.

A glimpse.

A contact.

Not the entirety of unrestricted consciousness exhaustively comprehended.

Udbhava.

Arising. Emergence. A kind of elevation.

Bodilessness.

Then:

Kampa.

Trembling.

The body-identification loosens further as the practitioner’s own power becomes manifest.

And the traditional description associates this with a kind of shaking.

Then:

Nidrā.

Sleep.

But don’t go thinking:

“Brilliant, I always fall asleep during meditation. Clearly I’m a master.”

Not quite.

Here the term belongs to a sequence of contemplative states and refers to the dissolution of outward-directed awareness.

It’s not simply ordinary drowsiness.

And finally:

Ghūrṇi.

Whirling.

Reeling.

A profound disorientation associated with mahāvyāpti.

Great pervasion.

Now here’s the extraordinary philosophical point behind that last one.

Ordinarily, you identify the Self with things that are not the supreme Self.

Body.

Breath.

Mind.

Limited individuality.

That identification begins to dissolve.

But then you might make the opposite mistake.

You might think:

“Right, I’m pure consciousness, and the universe is something separate from me.”

And Abhinavagupta says:

No.

You’ve still got a distinction left.

Because the true Self is sarvamaya.

Of the nature of everything.

So even the idea that some part of reality is excluded from the Self must dissolve.

First, you cease identifying yourself exclusively with what is limited. Then you cease excluding the universe from what you truly are.

And that’s the significance of the great pervasion.

Now these five states are also associated with the five levels of awareness extending from waking consciousness through to turyātīta.

Beyond the fourth.

And they correspond to entry into five subtle centres.

The triangle.

Trikoṇa.

The bulb or root.

Kanda.

The Heart.

Hṛdaya.

The palate.

Tālu.

And the upper kuṇḍalinī centre.

Ūrdhva-kuṇḍalinī.

Again, we’re working within a specifically tantric subtle-body system.

Not describing five anatomical structures that you could ask a surgeon to locate.

But the pattern is important.

Different levels of experience.

Different degrees of inward penetration.

And through all of them, the same underlying consciousness.

Now Abhinavagupta introduces something rather peculiar.

Three liṅgas.

And, yes, he’s about to become quite secretive.

Now, in the repose of uccāra, we’ve been discussing spanda.

The dynamic pulsation of consciousness.

And Abhinavagupta distinguishes three conditions.

First, there’s the supreme pulsation in which all knowable objects have been dissolved.

Nothing differentiated remains manifest as an object.

Second, there’s a condition in which an object is just beginning to emerge.

Unmiṣat-vedya.

The knowable opening into manifestation.

Third, there’s the condition in which the object has emerged.

Unmiṣita-vedya.

Manifest.

And these correspond to three liṅgas.

Not merely external stone emblems of Śiva.

Subtle focal principles within the contemplative system.

And Abhinavagupta says he’ll explain these properly on another occasion.

Yoginī-hṛdaya.

The Heart of the Yoginī.

The Heart of the Goddess.

And what is its essential characteristic?

Spanda.

Pulsation.

But specifically, pulsation manifesting through:

Saṃkoca and vikāsa.

Contraction and expansion.

The contraction of consciousness.

Well, here it appears together with its complementary movement.

Expansion.

The two are apprehended through the emergence of the divine pair.

Yāmala.

Śiva and Śakti.

Their union.

And through that union comes repose in the power of emission.

Visarga-kalā.

The very power through which consciousness pours forth manifestation.

Enough.

He’s just casually introduced the Heart of the Yoginī, described the dynamic union of Śiva and Śakti, connected it with the power of manifestation, and then essentially gone:

“Right, that’s enough of that.”

Mate.

You’ve got an entire audience here.

Very well.

10. The summary of uccāra
Now he gives us a few summary verses.

And the essential sequence is beautifully clear.

First, rest in your own awareness.

Svabodha.

Then contemplate the object.

Prameya.

Let the object be filled through that awareness.

Rest in that fullness.

And when fullness has been recognised, dissolve the distinction between knower and known.

Mātṛ-meya-vibhāga.

Then rest in pervasion.

Vyāpti.

The entire world of experience, from waking onward, is understood as resting in these modes of consciousness.

And through sustained practice, the practitioner approaches the abode of the very self-awareness through which manifestation and withdrawal take place.

Sṛṣṭi-saṃhṛd-vimarśa-dhāman.

The locus of consciousness’s own creative and dissolving self-apprehension.

Now that is a phrase worth remembering.

Not becoming a better breather.

Not collecting unusual bodily sensations.

Not becoming obsessed with inner lights and mystical fireworks.

But recognising the self-aware freedom in which experience is created and withdrawn.

And with that, we’re finished with uccāra.

But we’re not finished with prāṇa.

Because now comes its subtler expression.

Sound.

11. Varṇa — the secret vibration of Sanskrit
Right.

The entire cosmic alphabet?

All those consonants?

Well, they’re back.

Varṇa-vidhi.

The method of phonemes.

And Abhinavagupta begins by describing a subtle resonance arising within uccāra itself.

Dhvani.

Sound.

A subtle vibration, almost an imitation of something unmanifest.

Avyakta.

And this varṇa has its principal expressions in two important seed-mantras.

One associated with creation.

Sṛṣṭi-bīja.

The other associated with dissolution.

Saṃhāra-bīja.

Now, these sounds possess a special role because they express fundamental movements of consciousness.

Para-saṃvitti.

Supreme consciousness.

Now, here’s where Abhinavagupta makes a fascinating distinction between ordinary language and mantra.

12. Why a mantra doesn’t need to mean anything
Let’s take an ordinary word.

“Elephant.”

Now, when I say elephant, you immediately imagine a gigantic grey animal.

Big ears.

Trunk.

The whole business.

Why?

Because you’ve learned that the word refers to a particular creature.

The connection depends on linguistic convention.

Samaya.

But look what happens.

I say “elephant,” and you’ve produced an entire imagined object.

You can practically see the thing.

And if I say:

“Imagine an elephant riding a bicycle.”

Congratulations.

You’ve now got an elephant on a bicycle.

Absolutely ridiculous.

But your imagination manages it.

Even ordinary words can evoke their referents within awareness.

Now, in this tantric account, mantra sounds possess another kind of efficacy.

Their capacity to awaken contact with consciousness doesn’t depend merely upon conventional semantic meaning.

The phoneme can be contemplated as vibration itself.

Saṃvit-spanda-sparśa.

Contact with the pulsation of consciousness.

Abhinavagupta discusses phonemes with or without their accompanying vowels.

They may be internally articulated or remembered.

And the essential point is that their subtle resonance can disclose a mode of awareness that doesn’t require an ordinary object of reference.

Ordinary language directs your attention toward something.

“Tree.”

“Mountain.”

“Elephant.”

“Tea.”

Each word has an object or meaning.

But here the emphasis is on the manifestation of awareness through the very movement of sound.

Not merely:

“What does this syllable mean?”

What is the awareness through which the syllable appears at all?

And how might its vibration reveal that awareness?

That’s the principle behind this practice.

Now, the chapter becomes deliberately cryptic.

Again.

14. Some people see colours
Now, according to certain teachers, varṇa is understood differently.

Because the Sanskrit word varṇa can also mean colour.

And in their explanation, inner colour experiences arise from the subtle apprehension of the phoneme.

White.

Sita.

Yellow.

Pīta.

And other colours.

These appear within contemplative awareness, and their manifestation is said to lead the practitioner toward recognition of consciousness.

Now isn’t that fascinating?

One group emphasises subtle phonemic resonance.

Another emphasises an internally manifest colour experience.

But both are being interpreted through the same fundamental principle.

The expressive power of consciousness.

And now Abhinavagupta gives another summary verse.

Its meaning concerns the innate pulsation of awareness, independent of ordinary word-meaning, the lunar and solar movements of vital energy, and the attainment of balanced repose.

In the practice of bīja and piṇḍa forms of mantra, consciousness is apprehended in its fullness.

And that concludes the method of varṇa.

15. What about the body and actual ritual?
Ah, yes.

You might have noticed something.

At the beginning of this chapter, I told you there were practices involving imagination, breath, sound, the body and external ritual objects.

And we’ve spent practically the entire chapter on the first three.

What about the rest?

Well, Abhinavagupta says:

Karaṇaṃ tu mudrāprakāśane vakṣyāmaḥ.

I’ll discuss bodily configurations when I explain mudrā.

So that’s being postponed.

And the external ritual supports — vessels, altars, liṅgas and so forth — will also be explained in their appropriate places later.

This chapter establishes the basic classification but concentrates on dhyāna, uccāra and varṇa.

He isn’t forgetting the remaining methods.

He’s arranging the teaching.

And now, after all these magnificent descriptions of fire-wheels, vital energies, subtle bliss, inner centres and mystical phonemes, we arrive at the concluding verse.

And it brings the entire argument back to something surprisingly simple.

16. The final verse — stop ranking the methods as if they’re competing
Here is the actual Sanskrit:

SANSKRIT — CHAPTER FIVE, CONCLUDING VERSE

विकल्पः कस्यापि स्वयमनुपयन्पूर्णमयतामुपायात्संस्कारं व्रजति स उपायोऽत्र बहुधा।धियि प्राणे देहे तदनु बहिरित्याणवतया स निर्णीतो नैषां परफलविधौ कापि हि भिदा॥
Vikalpaḥ kasyāpi svayam anupayan pūrṇamayatām upāyāt saṃskāraṃ vrajati sa upāyo’tra bahudhā.

Dhiyi prāṇe dehe tadanu bahir ity āṇavatayā sa nirṇīto naiṣāṃ paraphalavidhau kāpi hi bhidā.

Now let me tell you what this means.

Some individuals can attain fullness of understanding spontaneously.

Without needing another method.

Their conceptual understanding becomes complete through its own refinement.

Lovely.

For others, understanding needs assistance.

And that assistance takes many forms.

Through the intellect.

Dhī.

Through vital energy.

Prāṇa.

Through the body.

Deha.

And through external objects.

Bahiḥ.

These are classified under āṇavopāya because they rely upon limited supports.

But here’s the final point.

In relation to the supreme result, there is no difference between these methods.

Now that’s remarkable.

Because you might have spent the entire book thinking:

“Well, anupāya is obviously the best.”

“Śāmbhavopāya must be better than Śāktopāya.”

“And āṇavopāya sounds a bit rubbish, doesn’t it?”

After all, it’s the method for the limited individual.

Surely that’s the bottom of the pile?

And Abhinavagupta says:

You’re missing the point.

The methods differ according to the capacities and conditions of their practitioners.

But the ultimate realisation?

That’s not divided.

You don’t get a slightly worse Śiva because you needed breathwork.

You don’t get a budget version of liberation because you started with meditation.

And the person who recognises through a limited support is not thereby recognising a lesser supreme consciousness.

The fruit is one.

Para-phala.

The supreme result.

And what is that?

The recognition of the unrestricted consciousness that is your own essential nature.

Now consider the whole journey.

You began with a limited understanding.

You thought:

“I am this body.”

“I am this mind.”

“I am this individual.”

So you used the body.

You used the mind.

You used the breath.

You used sound.

You used visualisation.

You examined objects appearing in awareness.

You watched the movements through which they arose and disappeared.

And gradually, the very things you thought confined you became the means by which the limitation of your understanding was overcome.

The body wasn’t the enemy.

Breathing wasn’t the enemy.

Thinking wasn’t the enemy.

The world wasn’t the enemy.

They were all available as expressions of the power of consciousness.

And through those expressions, the practitioner could recognise their ground.

Śiva was never absent from the limited individual.

The limited individual was Śiva appearing under limitation.

And through āṇavopāya, that very limitation becomes the doorway to recognition.

So there you are.

You’re sitting in your chair.

Breathing.

Looking at a flower.

Hearing a sound.

And apparently, the entire bloody universe is manifesting through the freedom of consciousness in every one of those experiences.

You thought you needed to escape your embodied condition to find Śiva.

And here’s Abhinavagupta telling you that your embodied condition can itself become the method.

Absolutely magnificent.

End of Chapter Five — Āṇavopāya.
