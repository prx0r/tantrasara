This passage describes an animal-sacrifice rite, not merely a symbolic offering of an imaginary animal.

And it’s not necessary to endorse animal killing to study this passage faithfully.

The traditions incorporated transgressive rituals.

12. Vāsanā — why your intention changes what the mantra does
Now, this is one of the most important sentences in the chapter.

Now, we’ve encountered vāsanā before.

A latent tendency.

Something left behind by previous experience.

But here the operative meaning is closer to the particular contemplative intention or conception adopted in the rite.

And Abhinavagupta explains why.

Mantrāṇāṃ vāsanānuguṇyena tattatkāryakāritvāt.

Because mantras accomplish their respective functions in accordance with the particular contemplative orientation.

Now, that’s absolutely extraordinary.

Think about it.

You might use a mantra in one ritual aimed at liberation.

And the same principal divine power might be invoked within another ritual directed toward enjoyment.

But the intended result differs.

So what determines the distinction?

The contemplative orientation.

The specific understanding through which the ritual is being performed.

Now, don’t reduce this to:

“If you think hard enough about getting a new car, the mantra will magically produce one.”

That’s not the argument.

We’re working within a formal tantric system in which mantra, intention, deity, ritual action and the qualifications of the recipient are understood together.

But the philosophical principle is profound.

A mantra isn’t treated as an independently functioning machine that produces the same outcome regardless of the consciousness employing it.

The meaning and orientation of the ritual matter.

Now consider the larger significance.

We’ve spent thirteen chapters explaining how the power of consciousness manifests different realities through different modes of self-apprehension.

The guru doesn’t merely pronounce some sounds.

The guru understands their divine power and directs the contemplation toward the appropriate result.

What is being purified?

What is being retained?

What form of divine identification is being cultivated?

The answers depend upon the intended initiation.

The guru has first contemplated the cosmic path within their own body.

And now the same process becomes established in relation to the disciple.

The disciple’s bodily consciousness is brought into non-difference with the guru’s own consciousness.

And then something remarkable happens.

The guru enters into unity with the disciple’s Self.

Not merely intellectual agreement.

Not merely sympathy.

A contemplative identification.

And this is where Abhinavagupta’s Sanskrit becomes absolutely beautiful.

14. The ocean of infinite bliss
Now, listen carefully.

The guru reaches the condition in which the disciple’s embodied consciousness has been brought into unity with their own.

And where does the guru rest?

In:

Anantānanda-saras.

The lake of infinite bliss.

Let’s break that down.

Ananta — endless, without limit.

Ānanda — bliss.

Saras — lake, pool or expanse of water.

The lake of limitless bliss.

Now that’s a magnificent place to perform an initiation, isn’t it?

Not a lake somewhere in the Himalayas.

Not a physical body of water.

The fullness of consciousness itself.

And how does Abhinavagupta describe its nature?

Svātantryaiśvarya-sāra.

Whose essence is the majesty of absolute freedom.

Svātantrya — unrestricted freedom.

Aiśvarya — lordship, sovereignty, divine majesty.

Sāra — essence.

Now remember.

We’ve spent the entire Tantrasāra discussing Śiva’s freedom.

The power through which he manifests the universe.

The power through which he conceals himself.

And the power through which recognition occurs.

And here the guru and disciple are being brought into conscious unity within that very sovereignty.

But the description continues.

This reality is full of:

Icchā.

Will.

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Kriyā.

Action.

And it is the Lord of the entire circle of deities.

Samasta-devatā-cakreśvara.

The sovereign of every divine power in the ritual.

And it’s filled with all the cosmic pathways.

Samasta-adhva-bharita.

Every world.

Every tattva.

Every kalā.

Every mantra.

Every phoneme.

Everything.

Now, here’s where it gets even more beautiful.

The entire universe is apprehended in a condition where nothing remains but pure consciousness.

Cinmātra-avaśeṣa.

Cit — consciousness.

Mātra — alone, nothing but.

Avaśeṣa — what remains.

Consciousness alone remains.

Now don’t imagine this requires physically annihilating the universe.

The worlds and principles are being contemplated in their ultimate conscious nature, without independent separation.

And then comes the decisive moment.

The guru becomes one with the disciple’s Self and rests in that condition.

Śiṣyātmanā saha ekībhūto viśrāntim āsādayet.

Having become one with the disciple’s Self, the guru attains repose.

And the result?

Parameśvarābhinnaḥ.

Non-different from Parameśvara.

Now that’s absolutely magnificent.

Think about what we’re describing.

The guru has contemplated the entire universe.

Installed it within the disciple.

Purified its differentiated manifestations through the nondual awareness of Śiva.

And entered the ocean of infinite bliss in which the guru’s consciousness and the disciple’s consciousness are apprehended as one.

There’s no ultimate second consciousness standing outside Śiva.

And that is the metaphysical centre of the initiation.

Not the flowers.

Not the vessels.

Not the impressive ceremony.

The recognition of non-difference between guru, disciple and the supreme consciousness.

And Abhinavagupta hasn’t finished.

Because apparently the disciple might still want a few things.

15. What if the disciple still wants enjoyment?
Right.

Suppose the disciple is a:

Bhogecchu.

Someone desiring experience.

Now, the previous contemplation has brought the disciple into the supreme condition.

But the particular initiation is also intended to provide a designated form of enjoyment.

So Abhinavagupta says the guru should join the disciple with the relevant tattva.

Yojayet.

Bring into conjunction.

Where?

Wherever the desired enjoyment belongs.

Yatraiva tattve bhogecchā.

Now, this is important.

Remember the enormous hierarchy of thirty-six tattvas?

Different worlds.

Different divine powers.

Different kinds of experience.

The disciple may desire a particular attainment associated with a particular level.

And the guru’s contemplation directs the disciple toward that level.

Either collectively or individually.

Samasta-vyastatayā.

Now, here’s the difference.

The liberation-oriented contemplation concerns the supreme reality of consciousness.

The enjoyment-oriented addition concerns a particular manifestation within the cosmic hierarchy.

And Abhinavagupta is preserving that distinction.

Because a desire for a particular experience isn’t identical with the recognition of unrestricted fullness.

So the ritual accommodates the recipient’s aim without pretending the two aims are interchangeable.

Now, we’ve reached something rather extraordinary.

The disciple has been brought into supreme consciousness.

The bonds have been purified.

But there’s one little problem.

The disciple is still supposed to have a life.

And the ritual has just contemplated the dissolution of the entire differentiated structure.

So what happens now?

Well.

Apparently we’re going to give the disciple a new body.

Not literally reconstruct their flesh.

A ritually purified body.

And the Sanskrit is magnificent.

16. Give the disciple a body made of pure tattvas
Now Abhinavagupta says:

Śeṣa-vṛttaye.

For the continuation of the remaining life or activity.

The guru contemplates that a body has suddenly emerged for the disciple from the essential nature of Parameśvara.

Parameśvara-svabhāvāt.

And what is this body made of?

Śuddha-tattva-maya.

Consisting of pure principles.

Śuddha — pure.

Tattva — principle.

Maya — consisting of, made of.

A body composed of pure tattvas.

And how does it emerge?

Jhaṭiti prasṛtam.

Suddenly issued forth.

Now, think about what’s happened.

Before the initiation, the disciple experienced themselves as a limited individual.

Body.

Prāṇa.

Mind.

Karma.

Bonds.

The entire structure of conditioned existence.

Then the guru contemplated the purification of those bonds through the six cosmic pathways.

The disciple was united with the supreme reality.

And now, for the continuation of embodied life, the body is re-established in a purified form.

Not because the physical body has magically vanished and been replaced by a new organism.

But because the disciple is ritually reconstituted in relation to the pure divine principles.

The body that was previously apprehended through bondage becomes a body apprehended through Śiva.

And Abhinavagupta concludes this passage with a powerful description.

Samasta-pāśa-viyojikā dīkṣā.

An initiation that separates the disciple from all bonds.

Samasta — all.

Pāśa — bond or fetter.

Viyojikā — that which separates or disconnects.

Dīkṣā — initiation.

The initiation that releases all the bonds.

Now, that is the real result being claimed.

The ceremony isn’t merely conferring a title.

It’s traditionally understood to release the disciple from the entire network of bonds sustaining limited existence.

And now, after that extraordinary contemplation, we return to something rather familiar.

The disciple honours the guru.

Dakṣiṇā.

The customary offering.

But the ritual isn’t quite finished.

Because we still need to carry out the corresponding purification in the sacred fire.

And the final homa is exceptionally precise.

17. Three offerings for every tattva
Right.

We’ve reached the fire rite.

Agni.

And the guru invokes:

Śrī Parā Mantra.

The principal mantra of the supreme goddess.

Now, here’s the actual structure of the formula, with the individual disciple and tattva supplied in the relevant places.

ATTESTED FORMULA FRAMEWORK — CHAPTER 14.25

अमुकस्य अमुकं तत्त्वं शोधयामि … स्वाहा
Amukasya amukaṃ tattvaṃ śodhayāmi … svāhā.

The Sanskrit text introduces this with the Śrī Parā mantra. The wording above represents its variable ritual declaration; it is not a complete independently usable mantra.

Now let’s translate.

Amukasya — of such-and-such a person.

Amukaṃ tattvam — such-and-such a tattva.

Śodhayāmi — I purify.

Svāhā — the traditional offering exclamation.

So, in plain English:

“I purify this particular tattva of this particular disciple.”

And how many offerings?

Three.

Tisra āhutayaḥ.

Three oblations for every tattva.

Now remember how many tattvas we’ve got.

Thirty-six.

And the sequence extends all the way up to:

Śiva-tattva.

The entire hierarchy is purified.

Now, here’s something extraordinary.

The guru doesn’t merely say:

“Right, you’re pure now.”

The purification is ritually enacted through the complete structure of the tattvas.

Each principle is brought within the mantric operation.

And the fire becomes the means through which that purification is expressed.

At the end comes:

Pūrṇāhuti.

The complete oblation.

And it ends with:

Vauṣaṭ.

The ritual exclamation.

Now, Abhinavagupta refers to the previously described process of conjunction.

Yojanikā.

The operation by which the disciple is joined with the appropriate divine condition.

And there’s another distinction for the enjoyment-seeker.

One additional complete oblation is used to join the disciple with the desired place of enjoyment.

Another concerns the manifestation of a body composed of pure principles.

So the ritual provides for both the intended experience and the continuation of the disciple’s existence in a purified form.

Then, once again, the guru is honoured with the appropriate offering.

Dakṣiṇā.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Ity eṣā putrakadīkṣā.

Thus is the putraka initiation.

Now, that would be a perfectly good place to finish.

But he adds one final sentence.

And it’s absolutely essential.

Because it tells us precisely how this initiation is understood to affect karma.

18. The astonishing exception — your present karma remains
Now, listen carefully.

Abhinavagupta says:

ATTESTED FINAL PROSE SENTENCE — 14.29

यत्र वर्तमानमेकं वर्जयित्वा भूतं भविष्यच्च कर्म शुध्यति।
Yatra vartamānam ekaṃ varjayitvā bhūtaṃ bhaviṣyac ca karma śudhyati.

Now this is particularly important.

Let’s break it down.

Yatra — in which.

Vartamānam — present, presently operative.

Ekam — alone.

Varjayitvā — having excluded.

Bhūtam — past.

Bhaviṣyat — future.

Karma — karma.

Śudhyati — is purified.

So the meaning is:

In this initiation, past and future karma are purified, with the present karma alone excluded.

Now, that’s extraordinary.

Because someone might imagine liberation means that every consequence of embodied existence instantly disappears.

The body vanishes.

All experiences stop.

Nothing remains.

But Abhinavagupta explicitly preserves the presently operative karma.

Why?

The broader Śaiva initiation system distinguishes the karmic activity sustaining present embodiment from the bonds that perpetuate further conditioned existence.

This helps explain why the disciple can continue living after initiation.

The initiated person may continue to have a body.

Experience pleasure and pain.

Become hungry.

Grow older.

Perform actions.

And eventually die.

But according to the traditional efficacy claimed for the rite, the relevant past and future karmic bonds have been purified.

The present embodiment continues under the exception just stated.

Now, notice the distinction.

Liberation doesn’t necessarily mean the immediate termination of all embodied appearance.

We’ve been discussing this throughout the Tantrasāra.

The supreme consciousness doesn’t need to destroy the world in order to recognise itself.

And the initiated disciple doesn’t necessarily have to abandon the physical body immediately for the ritual to be considered liberating.

The body may continue.

But the karmic structure of bondage has been transformed.

And that is why the final sentence matters so much.

It prevents us from making the simplistic claim:

“All karma disappears immediately.”

No.

Abhinavagupta makes a distinction.

Past and future are purified.

The presently operative karma is excepted.

Now, the 1918 Sanskrit edition represented by GRETIL ends Chapter Fourteen at precisely this sentence.

But another Sanskrit witness preserves two beautiful closing verses.

And they’re genuinely worth reading.

19. The attested closing verses — the guru and disciple become one

FIRST STANZA

अन्तः समस्ताध्वमयीं स्वसत्तांबहिश्च सन्धाय विभेदशून्यः।शिष्यस्य धीप्राणतनूर्निजासुतास्वेकतां सङ्गमयेत्प्रबुद्धः॥
Antaḥ samastādhvamayīṃ svasattāṃ

bahiś ca sandhāya vibhedaśūnyaḥ.

Śiṣyasya dhīprāṇatanūr nijāsu

tāsv ekatāṃ saṅgamayet prabuddhaḥ.

Right.

Let’s unpack it.

Antaḥ — within.

Samasta-adhva-mayīṃ — consisting of all the cosmic pathways.

Sva-sattām — one’s own being.

And:

Bahiḥ — externally.

So first, the awakened guru establishes the recognition of their own being, within and without, as comprising the entire cosmic path.

Then:

Vibheda-śūnyaḥ.

Free from division.

Now here’s the crucial instruction.

The guru brings:

Dhī — intellect.

Prāṇa — vital energy.

Tanu — body.

The disciple’s intellect, prāṇa and body are joined with the guru’s corresponding faculties.

And the awakened one brings them into unity.

Ekatā.

Now isn’t that beautiful?

The teacher isn’t merely pronouncing some sacred sounds over an otherwise independent person.

The guru is contemplatively uniting the disciple’s body, life-force and intellect with their own, within the all-encompassing conscious reality.

Now the second stanza tells us what happens through that unity.

SECOND STANZA

शिष्यैकभावं झटिति प्रपद्यतस्मिन्महानन्दविबोधपूर्णे।यावत्स विश्राम्यति तावदेवशिवात्मभावं पशुरभ्युपैति॥
Śiṣyaikabhāvaṃ jhaṭiti prapadya

tasmin mahānandavibodhapūrṇe.

Yāvat sa viśrāmyati tāvad eva

śivātmabhāvaṃ paśur abhyupaiti.

Now.

This is absolutely magnificent.

Śiṣya-eka-bhāvaṃ jhaṭiti prapadya.

Having suddenly attained unity with the disciple.

Jhaṭiti — suddenly.

Eka-bhāva — the condition of oneness.

Then:

Mahānanda-vibodha-pūrṇa.

Filled with great bliss and awakened consciousness.

And as long as the guru rests in that condition of unity?

Yāvat sa viśrāmyati.

The bound individual attains:

Śivātmabhāva.

The condition of having Śiva as the Self.

And what is the word for that previously bound individual?

Paśu.

The bound being.

So the verse says that through this repose in awakened unity, the paśu attains the nature of Śiva.

Now that is an extraordinary conclusion.

Because the ceremony began with a difference.

Guru and disciple.

Initiator and initiated.

Knower and recipient.

One possessing the appropriate knowledge.

Another seeking liberation from bondage.

And the ritual progressively brings all those distinctions into conscious unity.

The guru contemplates the six cosmic pathways.

The disciple receives them.

They’re purified through the nondual power of Śiva.

The guru’s consciousness and the disciple’s consciousness are brought into unity.

And within that unity, the apparent bound individual attains Śiva-nature.

The paśu becomes Śiva, not by acquiring a foreign consciousness, but through the removal of the bonds that obscure its own essential nature.

And that’s the entire philosophical purpose of putraka initiation.

End of Chapter Fourteen — Putrakadīkṣā
Now, consider the whole journey.

You began with a disciple.

A limited individual.

Someone who had already undergone the preliminary initiation and accepted the religious observances.

Then the guru prepared the great maṇḍala.

Installed the entire universe upon the cosmic trident.

Worshipped Parā, Parāparā and Aparā.

United the five ritual supports.

Performed the required offerings, including the historically attested animal rites.

And brought the disciple into the consecrated space.

Then the whole sixfold cosmic path was installed in the disciple’s body.

The appropriate karmic bonds were identified according to the goal of the initiation.

The entire path was purified through the contemplation of Śiva’s nonduality.

And then the guru entered into unity with the disciple’s consciousness.

Not as two independently existing selves trying desperately to bridge some metaphysical distance.

But as the recognition that the ultimate reality of both is one conscious freedom.

Svātantrya.

Now here’s the extraordinary thing.

Remember what we said in Chapter One?

There are two kinds of ignorance.

Bauddha-ajñāna.

Ignorance belonging to the understanding.

And:

Pauruṣa-ajñāna.

The deeper condition of contracted individuality.

Well, we’ve spent thirteen chapters studying the methods through which ignorance can be overcome.

And here the deeper process of initiation is being enacted.

The guru ritually removes the bonds sustaining the limited condition.

And the disciple is brought into recognition of Śiva.

This is why initiation is not simply another philosophical lecture.

It is understood as an effective transformation of the individual’s condition of bondage.

Now, that’s the traditional claim.

Whether and how such rites produce those results is a matter of religious doctrine and experience, not something we can independently establish simply by reading the Sanskrit.

But the internal philosophy is remarkably coherent.

The ritual’s power is not supposed to come from something outside consciousness.

The deity, the guru, the disciple, the mantra, the fire and the cosmic pathways all belong to the manifestation of Śiva.

And initiation brings them into conscious unity.

Now consider the irony.

The disciple begins as a little individual thinking:

“I’m bound.”

“I need to become free.”

“I need someone to take me to Śiva.”

And the guru brings them through the entire universe, from the lowest tattva to the highest, until nothing remains as an independent reality standing outside consciousness.

And at the end?

The disciple attains the nature of Śiva.

The very reality they were seeking.

The entire bloody universe was installed in you so that you could recognise there was never an ultimate separation between you and Śiva in the first place.

Absolute magnificence.
