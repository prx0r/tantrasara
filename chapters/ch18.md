# Tantrasāra — Chapter 18

अष्टादशमाह्निकम् — Abhiṣekaprakāśanam

The Consecration of the Guru: or, why having a magnificent beard, a thousand disciples and a certificate saying “Enlightened Master” doesn’t mean you know a bloody thing.


Right then.

Last chapter, we had a rather interesting problem.

Someone had previously entered another religious tradition, taken vows, perhaps served a guru, and then encountered the supreme teaching of Trika.

So what did we do?

We gave them a fast.

Had them relinquish their previous vow into water.

Performed a hundred fire offerings.

And politely informed Vrateśvara, the Lord of their previous religious commitment, that his services were no longer required.

Absolutely magnificent.

Now the person was free to receive the appropriate initiation.

Wonderful.

But we’ve been discussing initiation for quite some time now.

Dīkṣā.

And throughout these chapters, there’s been one rather important character overseeing the whole operation.

The guru.

The person giving the initiation.

The one who understands the mantras.

Knows the tattvas.

Conducts the rituals.

Purifies the six cosmic pathways.

And, according to the tradition, brings the disciple into the consciousness of Śiva.

Quite a lot of responsibility, isn’t it?

But here’s a question.

Who exactly is qualified to become that guru?

Because surely you can’t simply find some bloke wearing a rudrākṣa necklace, pour a little water over his head, give him the title of Ācārya and announce:

“Congratulations, mate. You’re now authorised to liberate other people.”

Well.

Abhinavagupta has something rather important to say about that.

And let me tell you something.

He’s absolutely ruthless.

Because this chapter is going to establish that genuine knowledge is more fundamental than ritual consecration itself.

And then he’s going to introduce a rather extraordinary paradox.

If you’re genuinely qualified to liberate people, refusing to do so can itself become a form of bondage.

Yes.

Apparently achieving profound spiritual knowledge doesn’t necessarily mean you can simply retire to a mountain and tell everyone else to bugger off.

Right.

Let’s get into it.

1. What is Abhiṣeka?
First:

Abhiṣeka.

अभिषेक

Consecration.

Anointing.

The ritual installation of someone into an authorised spiritual function.

Now, in ordinary religious contexts, abhiṣeka often involves pouring consecrated water or other substances over a deity or person.

But here the meaning is particularly important.

We’re discussing the consecration of a practitioner for one of two roles.

First:

Sādhaka.

साधक

An accomplished or authorised practitioner who undertakes the appropriate mantra discipline, often directed toward particular attainments.

Second:

Guru.

गुरु

A spiritual teacher capable of guiding and, within this initiatory system, bestowing grace upon others.

Now, notice something.

These aren’t quite the same roles.

A sādhaka may be principally concerned with their own accomplishment.

A guru takes responsibility for the spiritual transformation of others.

And Abhinavagupta introduces both together.

Here’s the actual opening sentence:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.1

स्वभ्यस्तज्ञानिनं साधकत्वे गुरुत्वे वाभिषिञ्चेत्।
Svabhyastajñāninaṃ sādhakatve gurutve vābhiṣiñcet.

Right.

Let’s break the thing down.

Su-abhyasta — well practised, thoroughly cultivated.

Jñānin — someone possessing knowledge.

Svabhyastajñāninam — the person whose knowledge has been thoroughly cultivated.

Then:

Sādhakatve — for the condition or office of a sādhaka.

Gurutve — for the condition or office of a guru.

Vā — or.

Abhiṣiñcet — one should consecrate.

So:

A person whose knowledge has been thoroughly cultivated should be consecrated as a sādhaka or guru.

Now, notice where the sentence begins.

Not:

“The person who’s completed the most elaborate ceremony.”

Not:

“The person with the longest lineage document.”

Not even:

“The person who can perform the most extraordinary yogic feat.”

It begins with:

Svabhyasta-jñāna.

Thoroughly cultivated knowledge.

Now, that is a magnificent distinction.

Because Abhinavagupta isn’t merely asking whether you’ve read the right scriptures.

He’s asking whether their teaching has genuinely become established.

Whether the knowledge has been practised.

Cultivated.

Assimilated.

Whether you actually understand what you’ve been studying.

And remember what we’ve been studying.

The nature of consciousness.

Cit.

Its freedom.

Svātantrya.

The relationship between Śiva and Śakti.

The difference between pure I-consciousness and the contracted ego.

The structure of the tattvas.

The means of recognition.

The relation between bondage and liberation.

All the bloody lot.

And now someone wants to teach this to others.

Well.

They’d better know what they’re talking about.

But Abhinavagupta goes much further.

Because he says something that, on first hearing, sounds almost absurd.

2. You can lack every other qualification and still possess the essential one
Here’s the next line:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.1

यतः सर्वलक्षणहीनोऽपि ज्ञानवानेव साधकत्वेऽनुग्रहकरणे चाधिकृतः।
Yataḥ sarvalakṣaṇahīno’pi jñānavān eva sādhakatve’nugrahakaraṇe cādhikṛtaḥ.

Now.

This is absolutely magnificent.

Sarva — all.

Lakṣaṇa — characteristic, distinguishing mark or qualification.

Hīna — deprived of, lacking.

Api — even.

So:

Even someone lacking all the other characteristics.

Then:

Jñānavān — possessing knowledge.

Eva — alone, precisely.

Adhikṛtaḥ — qualified, authorised.

And for what?

Sādhakatva — the sādhaka’s function.

Anugraha-karaṇa — the activity of bestowing grace.

Now consider the claim.

Even someone who lacks the other characteristic qualifications may be entitled to these functions if they genuinely possess knowledge.

That’s an extraordinary hierarchy of qualifications.

Knowledge comes first.

Not appearance.

Not reputation.

Not social prestige.

Not an impressive collection of religious accessories.

Now imagine two candidates.

The first has spent forty years studying and practising.

They understand the scriptures.

They can explain the relationship between the tattvas.

They possess genuine insight into the nature of consciousness.

But they don’t look particularly impressive.

No enormous beard.

No elaborate ceremonial clothing.

No magnificent title.

Perhaps they don’t even own a proper chair.

And then there’s the second candidate.

Oh, this one’s spectacular.

He arrives wearing seven kilograms of rudrākṣa.

He’s got a golden throne.

Three assistants.

A very serious expression.

He’s got a website explaining that he’s the direct embodiment of Mahābhairava.

And he’s recently been awarded a certificate declaring him a Supreme Universal Cosmic Guru.

Absolutely magnificent production.

Only one slight problem.

He doesn’t possess the knowledge.

Which one’s qualified?

Abhinavagupta doesn’t hesitate.

The one with knowledge.

And here’s the really brutal part.

He immediately says:

Na anyaḥ abhiṣikto’pi.

Not the other person, even if consecrated.

Now read that carefully.

Even if they’ve received abhiṣeka.

Even if the ceremony was properly conducted.

Even if all the sacred water was poured.

Even if there were flowers everywhere.

Even if the fire offerings were absolutely flawless.

If the relevant knowledge is absent, the consecration doesn’t supply the essential qualification.

Now, that is devastating.

Because he’s not merely distinguishing someone who’s been initiated from someone who hasn’t.

He’s distinguishing ritual status from actual spiritual competence.

And ritual status does not replace competence.

Of course, we should distinguish Abhinavagupta’s claim from an endorsement of anyone who simply announces that they’re knowledgeable.

The chapter gives a principle of qualification, not a magical test for identifying a fully realised teacher.

But within the teaching itself, the order is unmistakable.

First genuine knowledge. Then the appropriate consecration.

Not the other way around.

And now you might think:

“Wonderful. So the person who genuinely knows can simply do whatever they like?”

Ah.

No.

Because with knowledge comes responsibility.

And Abhinavagupta is about to make that responsibility sound rather terrifying.

3. Knowing enough to liberate someone can become an obligation
Right.

Suppose you’re a qualified guru.

You possess the required knowledge.

You’ve been consecrated.

And you’re authorised to initiate others.

But one morning you decide:

“Actually, I can’t be bothered.”

“Too much trouble.”

“All these disciples asking questions.”

“Honestly, I just want to sit around drinking tea.”

Now, Abhinavagupta says something rather unexpected.

Refusing to perform the function for which you’re responsible can create a religious fault.

Pratyavāya.

Let’s understand the word.

Pratyavāya refers to a fault, adverse consequence or transgression, especially one arising through the omission of something obligatory.

So we’re not simply talking about doing something deliberately harmful.

We’re talking about failing to perform a duty.

And in this context, the duty concerns:

Dīkṣādi.

Initiation and related spiritual activities.

Now, here’s the subtle distinction.

Abhinavagupta says:

स्वाधिकारसमर्पणे गुरुर्दीक्षादि अकुर्वन्नपि न प्रत्यवैति।

Svādhikārasamarpaṇe gurur dīkṣādi akurvann api na pratyavaiti.

If the guru has transferred their own authority, then even if they no longer perform initiation and related activities, they don’t incur the fault.

Now, that’s very interesting.

Because the guru can delegate their function.

Adhikāra-samarpaṇa.

The handing over of authority.

Let’s break it down.

Adhikāra — qualification, authority, jurisdiction or responsibility.

Samarpaṇa — handing over, entrusting, transferring.

And:

Sva — one’s own.

So:

The entrusting of one’s own authority to another.

Now imagine a genuine teacher.

They’ve taught for many years.

They’ve trained an appropriate successor.

The successor possesses the relevant knowledge.

The responsibility is formally transferred.

And the original guru steps back.

Absolutely fine.

No fault arises merely because they stop conducting initiations.

But what happens before that authority has been transferred?

Well.

Now we have a problem.

Because the obligation remains.

And Abhinavagupta describes this through a very interesting expression.

Adhikāra-bandha.

The bond of authority.

Now isn’t that extraordinary?

We’ve spent the whole Tantrasāra discussing the bonds of limited existence.

Pāśa.

Karma.

Mala.

Māyā.

All these things that restrict consciousness.

And now, apparently, even the possession of an authorised religious function can become a bond.

Adhikāra-bandha.

Bondage through one’s office or responsibility.

Now, think about it.

You might possess profound knowledge.

You might have genuine understanding of the nature of Śiva.

But you also occupy a particular role within the manifested religious order.

And that role carries obligations.

If you simply abandon them without properly entrusting them to someone else, the obligation isn’t fulfilled.

So Abhinavagupta says that failing to perform initiation can constitute bondage through the omission of duty.

And here’s the particularly fascinating part.

He connects this authority with:

Vidyeśa-pada.

The state or position of a Lord of Knowledge.

Vidyā — knowledge.

Īśa — lord.

Pada — position, state or station.

Now, remember Chapter Nine?

The different classes of knowing subject.

The divine mantra lords.

The hierarchy of beings qualified for different forms of spiritual agency.

Well, the guru’s function is not being treated merely as an ordinary job.

It’s linked with the divine hierarchy of knowledge and initiation.

And that’s why the responsibility is so serious.

But here’s the philosophical paradox.

The same person who understands that the ultimate Self is unrestricted consciousness may still possess a specific ritual responsibility within manifestation.

Now that’s a subtle point.

Nonduality doesn’t automatically abolish every distinction concerning conduct.

The absolute remains free.

But within the ritual order, different individuals possess different qualifications and duties.

And Abhinavagupta insists those duties matter.

Now, don’t mistake this traditional claim for a modern rule that a teacher must work indefinitely, ignore their own limits or continue serving a harmful community.

It concerns a specific office and its proper transfer within the Śaiva initiatory system.

But the principle is striking.

Knowledge isn’t merely a personal possession. If you’re entrusted with the capacity to help others, that capacity carries responsibility.

And now comes the practical question.

Suppose you’ve identified a genuinely knowledgeable candidate.

You’ve consecrated them.

They’ve received the appropriate authority.

Can they immediately start initiating people?

Not quite.

Because apparently the new guru has six months of work to do.

Every single day.

And Abhinavagupta is very explicit.

4. The six-month discipline — congratulations, Guruji, you’re on probation
Right.

We’ve got our knowledgeable candidate.

They’ve received abhiṣeka.

They possess the essential qualification.

Lovely.

But before exercising their newly consecrated authority, Abhinavagupta prescribes a particular discipline.

And here it is:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.2

मन्त्रदेवतातादात्म्यसिद्धये षाण्मासिकं प्रत्यहं जपहोमविशेषपूजाचरणेन विद्याव्रतं कुर्यात्।
Mantradevatātādātmyasiddhaye ṣāṇmāsikaṃ pratyahaṃ japahomaviśeṣapūjācaraṇena vidyāvrataṃ kuryāt.

Now.

Let’s unpack this magnificent little sentence.

Mantra-devatā-tādātmya-siddhi.

The attainment of identity with the mantra-deity.

And how is that accomplished?

Through a:

Vidyāvrata.

विद्याव्रत

A vow or religious discipline associated with sacred knowledge.

For how long?

Ṣāṇmāsika.

Six months.

And how often?

Pratyaham.

Every single day.

Not whenever you fancy.

Not every other Tuesday.

Not when Mercury is in retrograde and you’ve got a particularly nice candle.

Every day.

And what does the discipline involve?

Three things.

जप
Japa

Mantra repetition

The regular recitation or contemplative repetition of the relevant mantra.

होम
Homa

Fire oblation

The prescribed sacrificial offering through the consecrated fire.

पूजा
Pūjā

Special worship

The particular divine worship associated with the mantra and its deity.

Now imagine our newly consecrated guru.

Very pleased with himself.

“Wonderful. I’ve been given authority.”

“Excellent.”

“Can I start initiating people tomorrow?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“You’ve got six months of daily discipline.”

“Six months?”

“Yes.”

“Every day?”

“Every bloody day.”

“Can I take Sundays off?”

“Abhinavagupta hasn’t mentioned Sundays.”

“But surely I’ve already got the knowledge?”

Ah.

Now that’s the interesting question.

Why does someone who already possesses knowledge need six months of mantra discipline?

Because knowledge and the fully established ritual identification with a particular mantra-deity are not being treated as identical accomplishments.

Remember our opening distinction?

Knowledge qualifies someone for consecration.

But the newly consecrated practitioner must still undertake the prescribed process through which the mantra-deity becomes their established identity for the ritual function.

And that’s a crucial nuance.

Abhinavagupta isn’t saying the six-month discipline manufactures the essential knowledge.

That would contradict what he said in the first sentence.

He’s saying it establishes:

Tādātmya.

Identity.

And specifically:

Mantra-devatā-tādātmya.

Identity with the deity of the mantra.

Now, that’s beautiful.

Because we’ve been discussing mantra in extraordinary detail throughout the Tantrasāra.

Remember Chapter Three?

The Sanskrit phonemes as expressions of divine power.

Chapter Five?

Mantra and the pulsation of consciousness.

Chapter Thirteen?

The installation of Mālinī, and worship in which the whole ritual field becomes an expression of Śiva.

And now the future guru’s discipline is explicitly intended to establish identity with the mantra-deity.

Not merely intellectual knowledge of the mantra.

Not merely the ability to pronounce its syllables.

Not merely familiarity with its ritual instructions.

Tanmayībhāva.

Becoming of that very nature.

And here’s the important distinction.

The ritual practitioner isn’t supposed to remain merely an ordinary limited individual attempting to persuade a foreign deity to produce a result.

The divine power must become established as their own conscious identity.

Within this tradition, that’s what makes the consecrated function spiritually meaningful.

Now, don’t confuse this with a scientifically demonstrated claim that six months of chanting causes a supernatural transformation.

We’re describing the prescribed religious discipline and its intended attainment.

But the difference between knowing something conceptually and becoming firmly established in it is an intelligible one.

Imagine someone who understands a piece of music perfectly well.

They can analyse its structure.

Explain its harmony.

Discuss its rhythm.

But that isn’t quite the same as having practised until its performance becomes natural.

The musician’s understanding becomes embodied through repeated practice.

That’s an analogy, not Abhinavagupta’s example.

But it gives you a sense of the distinction.

The candidate has knowledge.

Now the ritual identity must become established.

And when it does?

A new level of authority comes into effect.

5. The moment the mantra-deity becomes your own nature
Here comes the next Sanskrit:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.2

तदनन्तरं लब्धतन्मयीभावो दीक्षादावधिकृतः।
Tadanantaraṃ labdhatanmayībhāvo dīkṣādāv adhikṛtaḥ.

Right.

Let’s unpack it.

Tad-anantaram.

Thereafter.

Labdha.

Having obtained or attained.

Tanmayībhāva.

The state of becoming of that nature.

Dīkṣādi.

Initiation and related rites.

Adhikṛta.

Authorised or qualified.

So:

Thereafter, having attained identity with that divine reality, the practitioner is authorised to perform initiation and related activities.

Now that’s important.

Because we have a precise sequence.

First, genuine knowledge.

Then consecration.

Then the six-month discipline.

Then the attainment of identity with the mantra-deity.

And then the exercise of the initiatory authority.

Now, there’s a little complication here.

The first sentence said that knowledge itself is the essential qualification for the capacity to bestow grace.

And now we’ve got an additional ritual requirement.

Isn’t that contradictory?

No.

Because the chapter distinguishes the fundamental qualification from the formal assumption and exercise of a particular ritual office.

The person without knowledge cannot acquire the essential spiritual competence merely by undergoing abhiṣeka.

But the person with knowledge, once consecrated, is directed to complete the prescribed discipline before performing the corresponding rituals.

So the two teachings complement each other.

Knowledge without the formal ritual may possess the essential liberating power.

Ritual consecration without knowledge cannot replace that power.

And the properly consecrated practitioner is expected to establish the mantra-deity identity before exercising the formal initiatory function.

Now, that’s rather sophisticated.

Because Abhinavagupta isn’t simply dismissing ritual.

He’s spent far too many chapters explaining it to do that.

But he also refuses to let ritual credentials stand in place of actual understanding.

Both matter.

At different levels.

And now we reach something extraordinarily important.

Because even once the guru is properly qualified, they cannot simply initiate anybody they fancy.

In fact, Abhinavagupta gives two rules.

And together they’re a remarkably elegant little statement of responsibility.

6. Don’t initiate the unqualified — and don’t refuse the qualified
Here’s the Sanskrit:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.2

तत्र नायोग्यान्दीक्षेत न च योग्यं परिहरेत्।
Tatra nāyogyān dīkṣeta na ca yogyaṃ pariharet.

Now.

First:

Na ayogyān dīkṣeta.

Do not initiate those who are unqualified.

And second:

Na ca yogyaṃ pariharet.

Nor should one turn away someone who is qualified.

Now that’s excellent.

Because we’ve got two opposite errors.

And the genuine guru is supposed to avoid both.

First, indiscriminate initiation.

You meet someone.

They’ve got absolutely no interest in the teaching.

They don’t understand the basic commitments.

They’re not prepared for the discipline.

They’ve perhaps heard that receiving a certain mantra will give them extraordinary powers.

And they announce:

“Right, Guruji.”

“Initiate me.”

“Immediately.”

Well.

No.

The guru has to consider:

Yogyatā.

Fitness.

Qualification.

Suitability for the intended initiation.

And remember, this depends upon the kind of initiation.

We’ve encountered several forms.

The samayī initiation.

The putraka initiation.

The seedless initiation.

The deathbed rites.

Different qualifications.

Different aims.

Different responsibilities.

So the guru must distinguish them.

They cannot simply distribute every advanced rite to anyone who asks.

But now here’s the opposite error.

A genuinely qualified person appears.

Perhaps they possess strong śaktipāta.

Perhaps they’re sincerely seeking liberation.

Perhaps they have cultivated the relevant understanding.

And the guru says:

“Sorry, mate.”

“Can’t be bothered.”

“Too busy.”

“Perhaps next year.”

Now Abhinavagupta says that’s also wrong.

Because:

Na ca yogyaṃ pariharet.

Do not reject the qualified candidate.

Now look at the symmetry.

The first rule protects the initiation from being given where it is inappropriate.

The second protects the qualified candidate from arbitrary refusal.

And together they express the guru’s responsibility.

Not indiscriminate generosity.

Not indiscriminate restriction.

Discrimination governed by genuine knowledge.

Now, that is exactly what you’d expect from a philosopher who has spent so much time discussing:

Viveka.

Discernment.

And:

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

The guru isn’t merely dispensing divine experiences according to personal preference.

The decision must concern the actual qualification of the recipient.

And here’s another important point.

Being qualified isn’t necessarily the same as being socially impressive.

Remember the opening statement.

Someone who possesses the essential knowledge may lack many conventional characteristics.

So the guru’s judgment cannot simply be reduced to wealth, background, reputation or outward appearance.

It must concern the relevant spiritual fitness.

Now, Abhinavagupta doesn’t give a comprehensive checklist for determining that fitness in this tiny chapter.

And we’re not going to invent one.

But he makes something clear.

The authority to initiate includes the duty to discriminate properly.

And now comes the next rule.

And it’s even more subtle.

Because suppose someone’s already been initiated.

Surely they’re now qualified to receive all the knowledge?

Not necessarily.

7. Even an initiated disciple has to be examined before receiving further knowledge
Here’s the Sanskrit:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.2

दीक्षितमपि ज्ञानदाने परीक्षेत।
Dīkṣitam api jñānadāne parīkṣeta.

Now.

Dīkṣita.

Someone initiated.

Api.

Even.

Jñāna-dāna.

The giving of knowledge.

Parīkṣeta.

One should examine or test.

So:

Even someone who has already been initiated should be examined before being given knowledge.

Now, this is very important.

Because initiation is not being treated as an automatic entitlement to every level of instruction.

Someone can be initiated.

And yet still require examination before receiving particular teachings.

Now, that should sound familiar.

Remember Chapter Thirteen?

The samayī was authorised for mantra practice, daily worship, listening and study.

But not automatically for every advanced rite.

The tradition distinguishes levels of qualification.

And the guru must understand those distinctions.

Now imagine someone approaching the teacher.

“Guruji.”

“Yes?”

“I’m initiated.”

“Very good.”

“So I want all the secret teachings.”

“All of them?”

“Yes.”

“Immediately?”

“Yes.”

“Have you properly understood what you’ve already received?”

“Well, not really.”

“Have you practised?”

“Occasionally.”

“Can you explain the difference between pure I-consciousness and ahaṃkāra?”

“Not exactly.”

“Can you distinguish kalā from kāla?”

“Hang on, which one’s time again?”

“And you’d like the entire esoteric initiation?”

“Yes.”

“Right.”

“Perhaps we ought to sort out the bloody basics first.”

Now, that’s an invented conversation.

But it captures the distinction.

Formal initiation and actual readiness for further knowledge aren’t identical.

And this brings us back to something Abhinavagupta has insisted upon throughout the work.

Knowledge is not merely a collection of words.

It’s something to be understood.

Cultivated.

And ultimately realised.

So the teacher should examine the student’s fitness for the knowledge being offered.

Not simply assume that previous participation in a ceremony has settled the matter.

But Abhinavagupta now introduces one final type of student.

And this one’s rather troublesome.

Because apparently some people obtain sacred knowledge through deception.

8. The disciple who gets the teachings through deception
Here’s the Sanskrit:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT — 18.2

छद्मगृहीतज्ञानमपि ज्ञात्वोपेक्षेत।
Chadmagṛhītajñānam api jñātvā upekṣeta.

Now.

This is a fascinating little phrase.

Chadma.

Deceit.

Disguise.

Fraudulent means.

Gṛhīta.

Taken, received or acquired.

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Jñātvā.

Having discovered or recognised.

Upekṣeta.

One should disregard, leave aside or refrain from attending to.

So the sense is:

Having recognised that someone has acquired knowledge by deceptive means, the guru should disregard them.

Now, there’s a distinction worth making.

The phrase concerns knowledge obtained through deceit. It isn’t simply a general instruction to reject people who make innocent mistakes.

Imagine someone who falsely claims to possess the necessary qualification.

They manipulate the circumstances.

They deceive the teacher.

Perhaps conceal their true condition.

And obtain instruction they weren’t entitled to receive within the system.

Abhinavagupta says that when the deception becomes known, the guru should refrain from further engagement of the relevant kind.

Upekṣā.

Disregard.

Now, the text doesn’t spell out every possible form the deception might take.

So we shouldn’t invent a detailed fraud investigation procedure.

But here’s the underlying principle.

The transmission of knowledge depends upon a relationship of genuine qualification, not merely the acquisition of information by whatever means are available.

Now, that is an interesting distinction.

Because in modern life you can obtain almost any Sanskrit text online.

You can download the Tantrāloka.

Read its chapters.

Study its mantras.

Look at the ritual procedures.

And none of that necessarily establishes the full initiation or qualification recognised by the historical tradition.

Knowing the words isn’t identical with possessing the corresponding understanding, authority or ritual relationship.

But another distinction also matters.

Reading a publicly accessible philosophical text isn’t itself evidence of wrongdoing.

The chapter’s concern is specifically deceptive acquisition in a context where qualification governs the transmission of knowledge.

And Abhinavagupta’s response is restrained in an interesting way.

He doesn’t say the teacher should punish the person.

Or curse them.

Or perform a terrifying ritual involving Kālarātrī.

He says:

Upekṣeta.

Disregard.

Leave them aside.

Now, that brings us to the final line of prose.

And after all this extraordinary seriousness about knowledge, responsibility, authority and spiritual qualification, Abhinavagupta makes one last practical observation.

Apparently the consecration should be conducted according to the resources available.

A rather sensible conclusion.

9. Don’t bankrupt yourself trying to become a guru
The chapter’s final prose reads:

ATTESTED FINAL PROSE — 18.2

अत्र चाभिषेकविभवेन देवपूजादिकम्।
Atra cābhiṣekavibhavena devapūjādikam.

Now let’s understand this.

Abhiṣeka.

Consecration.

Vibhava.

Resources, means, splendour or available wealth.

Deva-pūjā.

Worship of the deity.

Ādi.

And the related activities.

So the worship accompanying the consecration should be performed according to the available resources or scale of the ceremony.

Now, remember Chapter Fourteen?

Abhinavagupta warned against undertaking the great maṇḍala ritual without the resources required for it.

Here the principle is expressed rather more compactly.

The specific ceremonial arrangements correspond to the means available.

Now, isn’t that rather sensible?

Because we’ve been discussing the essential qualification for guruhood.

And what’s the essential qualification?

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Not the amount of money spent upon the consecration.

So imagine two ceremonies.

One involves magnificent vessels.

Flowers everywhere.

A beautiful maṇḍala.

Elaborate offerings.

A great assembly of practitioners.

Very impressive.

The other is more modest.

Fewer resources.

Less ceremony.

But the person being consecrated possesses the necessary knowledge.

Which feature is decisive?

Not the expense.

Because we’ve already established that even someone possessing every outward characteristic, including consecration, lacks the essential authority if they don’t possess knowledge.

Now, don’t mistake this for Abhinavagupta saying ritual preparation is irrelevant.

He gives the instruction precisely because the abhiṣeka remains a proper ceremonial act.

But its magnificence must not be confused with spiritual attainment.

Money can furnish the ceremony. It cannot purchase the knowledge that makes the candidate qualified.

And that is a rather excellent place to finish the prose.

Because we’ve now completed both passages of Chapter Eighteen.

That’s actually the entire chapter in the GRETIL Sanskrit transcription.

No missing catalogue of worlds.

No elaborate additional ritual hidden between the lines.

Just a remarkably concentrated statement of the qualifications, duties and discipline of the consecrated practitioner.

But another Sanskrit witness preserves a concluding verse.

And this one is worth learning.

Because it sums up the entire chapter in four short lines.

Let’s unpack the whole thing.

Svabhyasta-jñānatayā.

Again, Abhinavagupta places knowledge first.

Not consecration.

Not ritual splendour.

Knowledge.

Then:

Svārtha-parārtha.

Now this is beautiful.

Sva — one’s own.

Artha — purpose, goal or benefit.

So:

One’s own purpose and the purpose of others.

Now, this is one of the most important distinctions in the entire chapter.

Because the practitioner isn’t simply being qualified to accomplish something privately.

They’re being considered in relation to two capacities.

The fulfilment of their own aim.

And the ability to assist others in attaining theirs.

Then:

Possessing or bearing the corresponding authority.

Sādhaka-gurutā-yogaḥ.

That consecration should indeed be performed for such a person.

Now, here’s my slang rendering.

Right.

Suppose you’ve got someone who actually knows the teaching.

Not someone who’s simply memorised the terminology.

Not someone who’s acquired an enormous spiritual reputation.

But someone whose understanding has been properly cultivated.

Svabhyastajñāna.

And this person is qualified for two things.

First:

Their own spiritual undertaking.

Svārtha.

Second:

The benefit of others.

Parārtha.

And through that qualification, they may undertake the role of sādhaka or guru.

Now.

That’s the person you consecrate.

Not merely someone who’s applied for the position.

Not merely someone who looks the part.

Not merely someone who’s willing to pay for the ceremony.

Someone possessing the actual knowledge and the corresponding capacity.

And once they’ve received the appropriate consecration?

They undertake the prescribed discipline.

They establish identification with the mantra-deity.

They initiate the qualified.

They refuse initiation where it is inappropriate.

They examine students before imparting further knowledge.

And they don’t use their spiritual office as an excuse to behave arbitrarily.

Because the authority isn’t merely an honour.

It’s a responsibility.

And responsibility without genuine knowledge is a bloody dangerous thing.

11. The real distinction — being liberated and being authorised to teach aren’t identical
Now, let’s finish with something particularly important.

Because this chapter contains a distinction that is easy to miss.

Throughout the Tantrasāra, Abhinavagupta has been explaining liberation.

Mokṣa.

Recognition of the essential nature of consciousness.

Pratyabhijñā.

The overcoming of the contracted condition in which the Self appears limited.

But now we’re discussing something different.

Adhikāra.

Authority.

Qualification for a particular religious function.

And those two things are related without being identical.

You can possess genuine spiritual understanding without occupying the formal office of an initiating guru.

And you can occupy the outward office without possessing the essential spiritual understanding.

That’s precisely why the chapter begins by putting knowledge above consecration.

Now, remember Chapter Eleven.

The prātibha-guru.

The spontaneously awakened teacher whose liberating knowledge arises without depending upon an external guru or scripture.

That person was understood to possess a genuine capacity to communicate recognition.

But here we are discussing the formal consecration of a practitioner into the sādhaka or guru function within the ritual order.

So we shouldn’t collapse all the distinctions.

Liberating knowledge concerns the realisation of consciousness.

Consecration establishes a formal role within the tradition.

The six-month discipline is prescribed to establish the relevant identity with the mantra-deity.

Authority to initiate is exercised according to the recipient’s qualification.

These are connected but distinct matters.

Now, why is that so important?

Because religious traditions can easily confuse status with understanding.

Someone receives a title.

A robe.

A special name.

Perhaps an elaborate public ceremony.

And everyone begins treating them as an unquestionable authority.

But Abhinavagupta’s opening statement is devastating to that confusion.

Na anyaḥ abhiṣikto’pi.

Not another person, even if consecrated.

Without the knowledge, the essential qualification is absent.

And here’s the even more interesting part.

He doesn’t let the knowledgeable person simply avoid responsibility either.

If you possess the relevant authority, then exercising it appropriately becomes an obligation.

And if you want to relinquish the function, the authority can be transferred.

So genuine knowledge is neither a decorative personal accomplishment nor a licence for arbitrary power.

It gives rise to a particular responsibility within the religious order.

Now, that is a remarkably mature conception of spiritual teaching.

And it also raises an important question for anyone investigating a living tradition.

How do you distinguish someone who genuinely understands the teaching from someone who merely possesses the title?

Abhinavagupta doesn’t provide a simple infallible test here.

And modern students should not treat ritual office, mystical claims or institutional prestige as proof of someone’s understanding or ethical reliability.

But his fundamental principle gives us a powerful starting point.

Look for knowledge.

The capacity to explain.

The capacity to distinguish.

The ability to guide without confusing one’s own personal status with the truth being communicated.

And above all, a recognition that the ultimate reality isn’t the private property of the teacher.

Because what is the guru actually teaching?

That the essential nature of consciousness is Śiva.

Not:

“I’m the special person who possesses Śiva, and everyone else has to rent access through me.”

Absolutely bloody not.

The whole point of the tradition is the recognition of the supreme Self.

And the genuine guru’s responsibility is to help communicate that recognition.

Not to manufacture permanent dependence upon their personality.

Now, Abhinavagupta’s historical system places substantial authority in the hands of the realised guru, and we shouldn’t pretend he was writing a modern handbook of egalitarian pedagogy.

But the distinction between actual knowledge and mere ceremonial status is explicit.

And it runs through the entire chapter.

So after seventeen chapters of metaphysics, mantras, cosmic diagrams, initiations, divine powers and extraordinary ritual operations, Abhinavagupta gives us this wonderfully concentrated teaching.

If you want to become a spiritual authority, first understand what you’re supposed to be teaching.

Then cultivate it.

Become established in it.

Accept the appropriate responsibility.

And don’t initiate people simply because you can.

Initiate them when they’re qualified.

And don’t refuse someone who’s genuinely ready.

Because the purpose of the whole thing isn’t your title.

It’s their recognition.

Pratyabhijñā.

And the very consciousness they’re being guided toward?

Paramaśiva.

The same reality that constitutes the essential nature of the guru and disciple alike.

And that’s the final joke.

You’ve spent years becoming qualified to teach someone about the supreme Self.

You’ve studied the scriptures.

Undergone initiation.

Received consecration.

Completed six months of daily practice.

And finally someone comes to you asking:

“Guruji, what is the supreme reality?”

And after all that preparation, the whole point is to help them recognise the consciousness through which they’re already asking the question.

You’ve done six months of bloody paperwork to teach someone that Śiva was never absent in the first place.

Absolute magnificence.

End of Chapter Eighteen — Abhiṣekaprakāśanam.
