Ekasyaiva ṣaḍvidhatvaṃ yuktam.

It’s appropriate that one and the same reality should be sixfold.

Now that’s a beautiful sentence.

Because we’ve spent ten chapters examining an absolutely enormous array of distinctions.

And here Abhinavagupta is saying that the very division of manifestation into six cosmic pathways doesn’t compromise its fundamental unity.

You can examine reality as worlds.

Or principles.

Or great divisions.

Or meaningful expressions.

Or mantras.

Or phonemes.

But all six are ways in which one reality manifests itself.

And it’s that one reality that finally rests in its own nature.

Now, isn’t that extraordinary?

The entire universe becomes intelligible through a structured series of distinctions.

Yet the distinctions themselves don’t establish six independent worlds.

They articulate one manifestation.

And that manifestation isn’t independent of the conscious freedom of Śiva.

So now the whole cosmological part of our discussion has acquired a new purpose.

The six adhvans aren’t merely abstract classifications.

They’re going to become essential in the practices of purification and initiation.

Adhvaśuddhi.

And when we come to the initiation chapters, you’ll see why it was so important to establish the cosmic pathways first.

Because in those rituals, the practitioner is not merely receiving some arbitrary ceremonial blessing.

The entire structure of differentiated existence becomes relevant to the process of liberation.

But before we get to that, Abhinavagupta has one final thing.

And this time, he’s actually provided a neat little summary.

Three verses.

Real ones.

In the Āryā metre.

Apparently even Abhinavagupta understands that memorising this entire bloody system is a rather tall order.

14. The three concluding Āryā verses
These three verses occur at the close of Chapter 10 in the Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism edition. They are also independently attested in Tantrāloka 11.51–53. They summarise the numbers of worlds, principles, phonemes, mantras and padas associated with the kalās. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism +3

VERSE ONE

पदमन्त्रवर्णमेकं पुरषोडशकं धरेति च निवृत्तिः।

तत्त्वार्णमग्निनयनं रसशरपुरमस्त्रमन्त्रपदमन्या॥१॥

Padamantravarṇam ekaṃ puraṣoḍaśakaṃ dhareti ca nivṛttiḥ.

Tattvārṇam agninayanaṃ rasaśarap uram astramantrapadam anyā.

Right.

Let’s decode the bloody thing.

Nivṛtti has one pada, one mantra, one phoneme and sixteen worlds, with earth as its single tattva.

Then Pratiṣṭhā gets twenty-three tattvas, twenty-three phonemes, fifty-six worlds, five mantras and five padas.

Now, why does he say agni-nayana?

Because he’s encoding numbers through symbolic words.

Agni — fire — represents three.

Nayana — eye — represents two.

And in this mnemonic arrangement, they give twenty-three.

Then rasa — six — and śara — five — give fifty-six.

So Abhinavagupta is using Sanskrit numerical wordplay to pack enormous amounts of information into a tiny metrical space.

Rather clever.

It’s like creating a mnemonic spreadsheet.

Except your spreadsheet is poetry.

VERSE TWO

मुनितत्त्वार्णं द्विकपदमन्त्रं वस्वक्षिभुवनमपरकला।

अग्न्यर्णतत्त्वमेककपदमन्त्रं सैन्यभुवनमिति तुर्या॥२॥

Munitattvārṇaṃ dvikapadamantraṃ vasvakṣibhuvanam aparakalā.

Agnyarṇatattvam ekakapadamantraṃ sainyabhuvanam iti turyā.

Now the third division, Vidyā.

Seven tattvas.

Seven phonemes.

Two mantras.

Two padas.

Twenty-eight worlds.

And the fourth, Śāntā, is summarised in this mnemonic as three tattvas, three phonemes, one mantra, one pada and eighteen worlds.

There’s a textual subtlety here: the prose explicitly assigns four tattvas to Śāntā, from Śuddhavidyā through Śakti, whereas the closing mnemonic says three. The discrepancy appears in the transmitted text and should not be silently harmonised.

But the basic structure is clear.

The verse gives the counts of the cosmic and linguistic constituents assigned to the two divisions.

VERSE THREE

षोडश वर्णाः पदमन्त्रतत्त्वमेकं च शान्त्यतीतेयम्।

अभिनवगुप्तेनार्यात्रयमुक्तं सङ्ग्रहाय शिष्येभ्यः॥३॥

Ṣoḍaśa varṇāḥ padamantratattvam ekaṃ ca śāntyatīteyam.

Abhinavaguptenāryātrayam uktaṃ saṅgrahāya śiṣyebhyaḥ.

Now the fifth.

Śāntātītā.

Sixteen phonemes.

One pada.

One mantra.

One tattva.

And then comes the closing statement.

And this is lovely.

Abhinavaguptena āryātrayam uktam saṅgrahāya śiṣyebhyaḥ.

These three Āryā verses have been spoken by Abhinavagupta as a summary for his disciples.

Yes.

He’s actually named himself.

Essentially:

“Right, I’ve written you three little verses so you can remember all of this.”

Thanks, Abhinava.

Very considerate.

Could’ve perhaps done it about six chapters earlier, but I appreciate the gesture.

End of Chapter Ten — Kalādyadhvaprakāśanam
And now we’ve arrived at an important turning point in the Tantrasāra.

We’ve established the fundamental nature of consciousness.

We’ve discussed the different methods of recognition.

We’ve examined the structure of time.

The structure of space.

The thirty-six tattvas.

The different classes of knowing subject.

And now the five kalās and six cosmic pathways.

But here’s the question that’s been hanging over the entire discussion.

If all this is already Śiva, why isn’t everyone liberated?

And more specifically:

Why does recognition arise in one person and not another?

What actually brings about the descent of Śakti?

Śaktipāta.

Is it karma?

Is it correct understanding?

Is it the maturation of impurity?

Is it because you’ve done enough meditation?

Enough worship?

Enough good deeds?

Or is there something much more radical going on?

Because the next chapter is going to examine the cause of spiritual awakening.

And Abhinavagupta is about to challenge some very popular explanations.

Including the idea that liberation must be earned through karma.

Apparently the supreme Lord doesn’t need you to collect spiritual loyalty points before he’ll reveal himself.

Which raises a rather serious philosophical problem.

Why does grace fall where it does?

And that’s where we’re going next.
