द्वाविंशमाह्निकम् — Kulayāgaprakāśanam

“Have a drink, get laid, and congratulations, you’ve achieved enlightenment.”

Absolutely bloody not.

Consciousness’s reflexive apprehension of itself.

The Kaula ritual isn’t supposed to replace the recognition of Śiva with pleasure. It’s supposed to recognise the supreme consciousness through which pleasure, embodiment, manifestation and their dissolution occur.

1. This teaching isn’t intended for everyone
Abhinavagupta opens with a qualification.

We’ve already explained the various kinds of worship.

Now the entire system will be presented through:

Śrīmat-kaulika-prakriyā.

The venerable Kaula procedure.

But for whom?

The Sanskrit says:

Samunmiṣat-tādṛśa-dṛḍha-vāsanārūḍhān adhikāriṇaḥ.

Now, that’s quite a compound.

Let’s unpack it.

Samunmiṣat — opening forth, beginning to blossom.

Dṛḍha — firm, deeply established.

Vāsanā — latent disposition or ingrained tendency.

Ārūḍha — having entered into or become established in.

Adhikārin — a person qualified for a particular teaching or practice.

So this instruction concerns practitioners whose dispositions have become firmly oriented toward this particular form of worship.

Now, why does that matter?

Because one of the most consistent teachings of the Tantrasāra is that different people require different means.

Remember Chapter Two?

Anupāya.

For someone possessing sufficiently intense śaktipāta, recognition may occur without an elaborate method.

And Chapter Five?

Āṇavopāya.

The person who needs bodily, breathing or mantric supports.

And Chapter Eleven?

Different degrees of grace corresponding to different capacities.

Well, the same principle governs Kaula worship.

The method isn’t being presented as compulsory for every spiritual aspirant.

And Abhinavagupta isn’t about to announce that everyone must abandon ordinary meditation and rush into a complicated tantric sexual ceremony.

He explicitly begins with qualification.

Now, that should already make you suspicious of anyone selling a weekend course titled:

BECOME A KAULA MASTER IN FORTY-EIGHT HOURS.

Apparently all you need is £999, a white robe and a willingness to say Anuttara with a very serious expression.

No, mate.

The actual text presupposes a qualified practitioner and an established religious context.

And it starts with two rather provocative verses from an earlier authority associated with the Yogasañcāra.

The verse is deliberately compressed. The Kaula ritual vocabulary commonly includes substances and practices named with initial ma, such as madya (wine), māṃsa (meat), and maithuna (sexual union). We shouldn’t pretend this short verse supplies a perfectly explicit three-item glossary, but the phonemic allusion is unmistakable.

Now imagine our very respectable religious scholar.

“Abhinava, I’ve spent my entire life preserving purity.”

“Excellent.”

“I avoid forbidden substances.”

“Very good.”

“I’ve studied the Vedas.”

Quite so.

“Good God.”

Precisely the sort of reaction this esoteric vocabulary was designed to challenge.

But now comes the really cheeky part.

Abrahmacāriṇaḥ.

Those who are not brahmacārins.

Ordinarily, brahmacarya can refer to the conduct of a religious student and, in many contexts, sexual restraint or celibacy.

But here the Kaula verse reworks the term around brahman and ānanda.

The people who abandon the relevant manifestations of bliss are described as deprived of that bliss.

Now that’s quite the inversion.

A conventional ascetic might say:

“You attain purity by renouncing all these things.”

And the Kaula text replies:

“Well, what if you’re rejecting manifestations of the very blissful reality you claim to seek?”

That’s the provocation.

Not that everybody ought to pursue sensual gratification.

Rather, the Kaula tradition refuses to treat embodied experience as automatically excluded from divine consciousness.

Then the second verse introduces:

Ānanda-kṛtrima-āhāra.

An artificial or prepared nourishment associated with bliss.

Within this context, the expression refers to ritual wine.

And the verse threatens dire postmortem consequences for those who fail to observe the prescribed arrangement.

That’s historical tantric religious rhetoric, not a reason to drink alcohol or an established fact about hell.

The important thing is what Abhinavagupta does next.

He doesn’t stop to deliver an enormous sermon about the three M’s.

He immediately classifies the entire ritual.

Six kinds of Kulayāga.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to show how the cosmic structure can be understood through twenty-sevenfold classifications at several levels.

Remember the thirty-six tattvas?

9. Saṃpuṭībhāva — consciousness contains the universe, and the universe contains consciousness
Right.

Here’s the philosophical heart of the external rite.

Abhinavagupta says:

Saṃvidi viśvaṃ paśyet.

See the universe within consciousness.

Fair enough.

That’s been our teaching from the beginning.

The whole universe appears within the luminosity of awareness.

But then:

Tad api ca saṃvinmayam.

And that universe itself consists of consciousness.

Now, that’s the important second half.

Because if you merely imagine consciousness as a vast empty container holding lots of otherwise independent objects, you haven’t yet understood the full Trika position.

The universe doesn’t simply sit inside consciousness as a collection of foreign objects.

It is itself a manifestation of consciousness.

And Abhinavagupta explains the relationship through:

Saṃpuṭībhāva.

सम्पुटीभाव

Mutual enclosing.

Let’s unpack that.

Saṃpuṭa — an enclosure, something folded together or enclosing from both sides.

Bhāva — condition or state.

The mutual enclosing condition.

And Abhinavagupta gives two directions.

First:

Saṃvida uditaṃ tatraiva paryavasitaṃ viśvam.

The universe arises from consciousness and comes to rest within it.

That’s one direction.

Consciousness contains the universe.

But second:

Vedyāc ca saṃvid udeti tatraiva ca viśrāmyati.

Consciousness arises in relation to the knowable and rests there.

That’s the complementary direction.

Now, don’t interpret that as saying an independently existing object manufactures consciousness.

The whole argument presupposes the nondual reality of awareness.

The point is that consciousness also manifests through its objectivity.

Through the knowable universe.

So there are two interrelated apprehensions.

Everything appears in consciousness.

Everything that appears is consciousness’s manifestation.

And this is the double enclosure.

Saṃpuṭībhāva-dvaya.

Now imagine holding a mirror.

You see a flower reflected in it.

The reflection is within the mirror.

But the visible reflection isn’t something alien to the mirror’s capacity to manifest appearances.

And here the image becomes even more radical.

The whole object-world is apprehended as an expression of conscious luminosity.

Now, this is one of the deepest insights in the chapter.

Because it prevents the practitioner from making a very common mistake.

Treating spiritual realisation as withdrawing from the world into some separate internal place called consciousness.

As though the world were outside it.

And you had to escape from the world to reach the divine.

But Abhinavagupta says:

The world arises through consciousness, rests within consciousness and is itself an expression of consciousness.

So even the external Kaula ritual can become an act of nondual recognition.

And he supports this through a brief scriptural phrase:

Sṛṣṭiṃ tu saṃpuṭīkṛtya.

Having enclosed the manifestation.

Wine.

Flowers.

Thus is the external sacrifice.

Now isn’t that remarkable?

Because we started with an altar and a vessel.

And by the end, we’ve reached the double relation between universe and consciousness.

The whole external rite becomes a demonstration of nonduality.

But now Abhinavagupta says:

Now, worship in Śakti.

And this is where things become rather more intimate.

The second kind of Kaula worship.

Ātmaka — having the nature of.

Śakti possesses the character of the vīra.

Prollāsa.

Exuberant unfolding.

Prabodha.

Awakening.

Sṛṣṭi.

Manifestation or creation.

The participants are being contemplated as embodiments of the divine polarity through which manifestation takes place.

The divine ordering or determination that renders the ritual intelligible within its own system.

Now here’s a crucial distinction.

The female participant isn’t merely a convenient object through which a male practitioner acquires spiritual power.

The metaphysical argument insists upon reciprocal participation in divine identity.

That doesn’t eliminate the real possibility of inequality or coercion in historical or modern ritual communities. A philosophical claim of nonduality is not a substitute for consent, bodily autonomy or safety.

But it is important to understand the intended reciprocal structure.

Śakti is divine. The vīra is divine. Their mutuality expresses a power belonging to the one consciousness.

And:

Anāhata.

Unstruck.

Now, the precise enumeration is compressed across the verses, but the concluding text identifies:

Aṣṭavidha Nāda-Bhairava.

The eightfold Bhairava of sound.

And here’s the important distinction.

Anāhata means unstruck.

Not produced by the ordinary collision of objects.

So we’re discussing a traditional yogic conception of subtle sound.

Not simply ordinary acoustic vibration.

And Abhinavagupta connects the sequence with:

Mantravīrya.

The potency of mantra.

Now, that’s remarkable.

Because remember Chapter Three?

The ultimate phonemic powers emerge from the divine self-apprehension of consciousness.

And audible Sanskrit is only one level of that manifestation.

Here, too, we’re moving toward the subtle source of sound.

From articulated resonance toward its unmanifest ground.

And Abhinavagupta gives several pairs or circles in which this is contemplated.

The movements of prāṇa and apāna.

Gamana and āgamana.

Going and coming.

Their points of repose.

The two ears.

The two eyes.

The relevant paired bodily supports.

And the condition in which the movements come together and cease.

Now, here’s the subtle point.

Sound isn’t being treated merely as something produced by a mouth and received by an ear.

The practitioner is being directed toward the relationship between the manifestation of sound and the conscious awareness through which it appears.

And Abhinavagupta describes a subtle sound associated with the Heart and throat.

And then:

Kṣobha-vigama.

The cessation of agitation.

Kṣobha — stirring, disturbance, excitation.

Vigama — disappearance, cessation.

At this cessation, the differentiated sounds subside.

And the supreme form of Nāda-Bhairava is apprehended.

Now, that is particularly beautiful.

Because the whole sequence concerns the emergence and withdrawal of manifest sound.

And where does it end?

Not merely in physical silence.

But in the luminous consciousness through which both sound and silence appear.

And Abhinavagupta connects this with:

Jyotis.

Light.

And:

Dhvani.

Sound.

The luminous and sonorous expressions of the same divine reality.

And he names the result:

Paramā māntrī vyāptiḥ.

The supreme pervasion of mantra.

Now, that’s an absolutely magnificent phrase.

Because the mantra’s true pervasion isn’t simply the sound travelling through the air.

It’s the recognition of its divine power throughout experience.

And here’s where Abhinavagupta delivers one of the most important claims in the entire chapter.

22. Jīvanmukti — liberation in every action
The text says:

Evaṃ karmaṇi karmaṇi viduṣaḥ syāj jīvato muktiḥ.

Now, let’s break that down.

Evaṃ — thus, in this way.

Karmaṇi karmaṇi — in each and every action.

Viduṣaḥ — of the one who knows.

Jīvataḥ — while living.

Muktiḥ — liberation.

So:

In every action, the knower may be liberated while still alive.

Now, that is magnificent.

Because we’ve spent so many chapters discussing liberation after initiation, after death, through ritual purification and through divine grace.

And now Abhinavagupta returns to:

Jīvanmukti.

जीवन्मुक्ति

Liberation while living.

Not merely the promise of a future heavenly state.

Not merely the dissolution of the body.

But liberation within embodied existence.

And the extraordinary claim is that this can be expressed:

Karmaṇi karmaṇi.

In each action.

Now, consider what that means.

Walking.

Speaking.

Listening.

Eating.

Thinking.

Laughing.

Experiencing pleasure.

Experiencing discomfort.

The entire movement of embodied life.

And within each of these, the knower recognises the conscious reality through which the experience occurs.

Now, this is the real culmination of the Kaula teaching.

Not simply that you can perform an unusual ceremony.

But that the relationship between experience and consciousness can be transformed throughout life.

And here’s the distinction.

The ordinary limited individual experiences actions as belonging to a small independent self.

“I am doing this.”

“I am experiencing that.”

“This is mine.”

“That is outside me.”

But the realised practitioner apprehends the same manifestations within the freedom of one consciousness.

Śiva acting through Śakti.

And the apparent individual no longer needs to interpret the entire field as something fundamentally alien.

Now, that’s the teaching.

But Abhinavagupta isn’t finished.

Because we’ve reached a particularly esoteric set of verses concerning the integration of fire, wind, the trident and the senses.

And one of these verses contains something rather dangerous.

So we need to distinguish its symbolic and historical meaning carefully.

23. The last esoteric seals — fire, wind, the senses and a dangerous leap
The next verses concern:

Parakula-vijñāna.

The knowledge of the supreme Kula.

And the practitioner is called:

Garbha.

A womb or receptacle of that knowledge.

Now, isn’t that a beautiful reversal?

Throughout the chapter we’ve discussed manifestation emerging from the divine womb of Śakti.

And now the knower becomes a receptacle for the higher knowledge.

The verse then speaks of the relation between:

Śūnya.

Void.

And:

Aśūnya.

Nonvoid.

And the establishment of:

Anala and anila.

Fire and wind.

Within a single axis.

Ekadaṇḍa.

Then comes:

Samarasīkaraṇa.

समरसीकरण

Making of one flavour.

Let’s unpack it.

Sama — equal, same.

Rasa — flavour, essence, relish.

Kṛ — to make.

So:

Bringing things into one homogeneous essence or flavour.

Now, that’s a magnificent word.

Because it’s not simply saying that different things happen to exist together.

It’s describing their integration into one experiential reality.

And the text applies this to:

Śūla.

The trident.

Remember the three powers?

The triadic manifestation.

The entire relationship between consciousness and its powers?

Well, the trident is brought into samarasa.

One flavour.

And Abhinavagupta gives the image:

Rase rasam iva sthitam.

Like one liquid essence resting within another of the same kind.

Now, that’s gorgeous.

Because once the distinction between two substances has vanished in a homogeneous mixture, you can’t simply point to one portion and announce:

“That’s the absolutely separate essence.”

The whole arrangement is of one nature.

And that’s the intended contemplative relation between the powers of consciousness.

Now comes:

Tyaktāśaṅka.

Having abandoned doubt.

And:

Nirācāra.

Free from the constrictions of fixed conduct.

Within this esoteric context, the practitioner is being described in relation to the transcendence of ordinary limiting distinctions.

But that isn’t a universal permission to behave recklessly or violate other people’s boundaries.

And then an extraordinary phrase appears:

Nāham asmīti bhāvayan.

Contemplating, “I am not.”

Now, this is very important.

Because it could easily be misunderstood.

Abhinavagupta hasn’t suddenly abandoned the doctrine of self-aware consciousness and decided to become a philosopher of absolute personal nonexistence.

The phrase operates within a contemplative context in which the limited identification of “I” is relinquished.

The small, contracted ego isn’t the ultimate Self.

And this is exactly what we’ve been investigating since Chapter One.

Ahaṃkāra is not the same as the supreme aham.

The ego-sense is a limited formation.

But the essential I-consciousness is the self-aware luminosity of Śiva.

So the apparent negation must be understood within the movement beyond contracted identification.

Not as a proof that consciousness doesn’t exist.

And then the text directs attention toward the divine powers within the body.

Dehasthā devatāḥ.

The deities established in the body.

Even delight and agitation are apprehended within:

Cidghana.

The compact fullness of consciousness.

Now, that’s a beautiful expression.

Cit — consciousness.

Ghana — dense, compact, solid mass.

So:

A solid mass of conscious reality.

Not a physically solid substance, of course.

The term expresses undivided fullness.

Then Abhinavagupta mentions the divine groups located in the circles of the ears, eyes, mouth and nose.

And the recognition of:

Grahītṛ.

The apprehender.

The subject of experience.

And the culminating result is associated with:

Khecarī.

The divine power moving through the expanse of consciousness.

Now, one final verse describes a dramatic and dangerous physical leap associated with Vyomacarīmudrā. Its literal wording involves throwing the body toward a distant precipice or chasm, rather than merely sitting in meditation.

That is an attested part of the chapter, but it is not a safe practice to attempt, and nothing in the source establishes that invoking a mudrā protects a person from falling injuries or death.

The relevant philosophical theme is the traditional association of vyoma, space, with unrestricted consciousness. The text also contains this hazardous physical image; we shouldn’t erase it or turn it into an actionable exercise.

And with that, Abhinavagupta closes the long metrical section:

Ity eṣa yāmalayāgaḥ.

Thus is the worship of the pair.

Now, that was quite a journey, wasn’t it?

We began with Śakti and her inseparability from the possessor of Śakti.

Then the divine pair.

The inner and outer circles.

The return of the subordinate goddesses into the central consciousness.

The peaceful and manifested conditions.

The supreme Kaula beyond both.

The threefold visarga.

The generative lotus.

The Sun, Moon and fire.

The emergence of subtle sound.

Khecarī.

The sensory powers.

And the extraordinary claim that the knower can be liberated in each and every action.

Now we return to prose.

And the fifth sacrifice.

Prāṇayāga.

24. Prāṇayāga — when the entire universe becomes the movement of life
Right.

Remember the six forms?

We’ve completed:

External worship.
Worship in Śakti.
Worship in the body.
Worship in the divine pair.
Now comes the fifth.

Prāṇayāga.

प्राणयाग

The sacrifice in vital energy.

And here’s something important.

The Sanskrit description is incredibly short.

Abhinavagupta doesn’t introduce another enormous independent breathing system.

He says that the prāṇa already contemplated as pervading the universe is strengthened through offerings.

Upodbalana.

Reinforcement, strengthening, supporting.

And what supports it?

The previously described pervasion of consciousness.

Saṃvid-vyāpti.

And offerings involving food, fragrance, incense and other ritual substances.

Now, here’s the crucial distinction.

The offerings aren’t merely being presented to a physical breath.

The vital power is being contemplated as pervaded by consciousness and encompassing the universe.

Viśvamaya-prāṇa.

Prāṇa consisting of or pervading the universe.

Now, remember Chapter Five.

Prāṇa wasn’t merely the oxygen moving through the lungs.

Within the tantric subtle-body account, it was associated with a far more extensive system of vital powers and their relation to conscious manifestation.

And here the same principle operates.

The breath and vital activity become supports for recognising the whole cosmic manifestation within consciousness.

And the offerings strengthen that orientation.

Now, imagine our disciple.

“Right, Abhinava.”

“Yes?”

“We’ve gone through the altar.”

“Yes.”

“Śakti.”

“Yes.”

“The body.”

“Yes.”

“The divine pair.”

“Indeed.”

“And now prāṇa.”

“Correct.”

“So do we need another fifty pages of instructions?”

“Not this time.”

“Thank God.”

Because the essential method has already been described.

The fifth rite extends that same nondual pervasion into vital energy.

And that prepares the sixth and highest.

Saṃvidyāga.

The sacrifice in consciousness itself.

25. Saṃvidyāga — the highest sacrifice requires no separate object
Now comes the final of the six.

Saṃvidyāga.

संविद्याग

Saṃvid — consciousness.

Yāga — worship, sacrifice, sacred offering.

So:

The sacrifice in consciousness.

And Abhinavagupta defines it with extraordinary concision.

Viśrānti-rūḍhiḥ.

The firm establishment of repose.

Viśrānti — resting, repose.

Rūḍhi — becoming established, firmly rooted.

Now, that’s magnificent.

Because after all these elaborate ceremonies, the highest sacrifice is simply the establishment of repose in consciousness.

But we need to be careful about that word.

Viśrānti doesn’t merely mean relaxation.

It doesn’t mean becoming drowsy.

Or falling asleep.

Or having a particularly pleasant afternoon.

It means resting in the essential reality of awareness.

And remember what we’ve discovered about that awareness.

It isn’t simply the absence of activity.

It’s the self-aware freedom of Śiva.

The reality beyond the division between peaceful and manifested states.

So the highest sacrifice is not an action through which you manufacture consciousness.

It’s the firm recognition of consciousness as the essential reality of the entire sacrificial process.

Now think about how extraordinary that is.

You began with an altar.

A physical support.

Something outside the body.

Then a living embodiment of Śakti.

Then your own body.

Then the divine pair.

Then vital energy.

And finally consciousness itself.

Each step became more interior.

But the point wasn’t simply moving from outside to inside.

It was the progressive overcoming of the assumption that the divine reality is restricted to a particular object or event.

And in the sixth sacrifice, there is no separate object that needs to become divine.

The essential reality is recognised directly.

Saṃvid.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Prāg eva nirūpitaḥ.

It has already been explained earlier.

Of course it has.

Because we’ve spent twenty-one chapters doing precisely that.

Remember Chapter Two?

Anupāya.

The condition beyond means.

And Chapter Four?

The power of correct contemplative understanding.

And Chapter Twelve?

The highest bathing is immersion in Bhairava.

And Chapter Thirteen?

True worship is the offering of the universe into the fullness of awareness.

All those teachings converge here.

The highest Kulayāga is the recognition that the entire universe, the ritual, the worshipper and the deity are nothing but the manifestation of one consciousness.

Now, that’s absolutely magnificent.

But the chapter isn’t quite finished.

Because there is a consequence for initiation.

And then one final surprise.

Apparently the six sacrifices aren’t the complete story.

There’s a seventh.

And Abhinavagupta saves it for the very end of the book.

26. The initiation by a glance — when the Heart is free from doubt
Right.

Suppose someone has performed one of these Kaula sacrifices.

And the result is a condition in which their Heart is purified through freedom from uncertainty.

Nirvicikitsatā-pavitrita-hṛdaya.

Now, that’s a magnificent compound.

Let’s unpack it.

Nir — without.

Vicikitsā — hesitation, uncertainty, doubt.

Pavitrita — purified.

Hṛdaya — Heart.

So:

A Heart purified through the absence of doubt.

Now, remember Chapter Twenty-One.

We were discussing:

Prasiddhi.

Firmly established conviction.

And the way a teaching becomes rooted in the Heart.

Here the same theme returns in ritual form.

The disciple has entered into a state of conviction concerning the divine reality.

And what may the guru do?

Dīkṣā.

Initiation.

But here’s the remarkable thing.

The initiation can occur through:

Avalokana.

A glance.

Now, that should sound familiar.

Remember Chapter Eleven?

We discussed the genuine guru’s ability to bestow initiation through knowledge, speech, vision and other means.

Well, here’s another example.

The guru first shows the disciple the relevant Kaula rite.

And then, through the previously described contemplative pervasion:

Vyāpti.

And:

Anusandhāna.

Sustained conscious connection or recollection.

The guru performs initiation by the glance.

Now, that’s extraordinary.

Because after all these elaborate initiatory systems, the text permits a comparatively direct operation for someone whose Heart has genuinely attained the relevant purity.

And it doesn’t depend upon the usual sequence of sesame and ghee oblations as a necessary preliminary in this case.

Now, remember:

Tila.

Sesame.

Ājya.

Clarified butter.

We encountered their oblations in the earlier initiations.

But here the method is explicitly distinguished.

The guru’s contemplative apprehension and glance become decisive.

Now, we shouldn’t present this as proof that looking at someone can reliably alter their consciousness or religious status.

It’s a traditional claim about initiation through realised divine awareness.

But the inner structure is consistent with everything we’ve learned.

The closer the practitioner is to recognition, the less elaborate the external means may need to be.

And for other rites?

Abhinavagupta says the earlier procedures still apply.

Parokṣadīkṣā.

Initiation in absence.

And the other occasional rites.

But now with the Kaula sacrifice occupying the principal position.

Etad-yāga-pradhānatā.

The predominance of this sacrifice.

So the previous ritual framework isn’t simply abolished.

It’s reorganised around the Kaula understanding.

And now comes the final sentence.

The end of Chapter Twenty-Two.

And, in the checked editions, the end of the Tantrasāra itself.

And after all the complexity, the conclusion is astonishingly simple.

27. The seventh sacrifice — the guru’s own body
Now listen carefully.

We’ve had six sacrifices.

External.

Śakti.

Body.

The pair.

Prāṇa.

Consciousness.

But Abhinavagupta now says:

Guruśarīre saptamaḥ kulayāgaḥ.

गुरुशरीरे सप्तमः कुलयागः।

The seventh Kaula sacrifice is in the body of the guru.

And what is its status?

Sarvottamaḥ.

The highest of all.

Now that’s quite an ending.

Because we might have expected the sixth sacrifice in consciousness to be the absolute conclusion.

And indeed, it’s explicitly principal for the liberation-seeker.

But the final sentence adds a seventh form centred upon the guru.

Why?

Because the guru, within this initiatory tradition, is understood as the living embodiment of the knowledge through which the divine reality is transmitted.

And Abhinavagupta says that the seventh sacrifice, performed once together with the previously described worship, fulfils everything.

Sakṛd eva kṛtaḥ sarvaṃ pūrayati.

Now, let’s break that down.

Sakṛt — once.

Eva — indeed, only.

Kṛtaḥ — performed.

Sarvam — everything.

Pūrayati — fills, completes, fulfils.

So:

Performed just once together with the earlier sacrifice, it fulfils the whole.

Now, that is a remarkable claim.

Because the guru is no longer merely one participant standing beside the sacred ritual.

Their very body becomes the support of the highest Kaula worship.

And this takes us straight back to Chapter Eighteen.

Remember what Abhinavagupta said about qualification?

Genuine knowledge is primary.

Not merely the status of guru.

Not merely consecration.

Not merely the impressive religious title.

The guru must actually possess the liberating understanding.

And within that conception, the guru’s body becomes significant as an embodiment of the supreme knowledge.

Now, that doesn’t mean worshipping an ordinary teacher’s personality as though they possessed independent divine authority.

Nor does it legitimise exploitation or unquestionable obedience.

The ritual claim presupposes the spiritual qualification described throughout the work.

And the philosophical meaning is the same one we’ve encountered from the very beginning.

The guru, the disciple, the Goddess and the entire manifestation are ultimately non-different from Parameśvara.

The guru’s body becomes a culminating ritual support for that recognition.

And then comes the final phrase.

ACTUAL ENDING OF TANTRASĀRA CHAPTER 22

गुरुशरीरे सप्तमः कुलयागः सर्वोत्तमः सोऽपि प्राग्यागसाहित्येन सकृदेव कृतः सर्वं पूरयतीति शिवम्॥
Guruśarīre saptamaḥ kulayāgaḥ sarvottamaḥ so’pi prāgyāgasāhityena sakṛd eva kṛtaḥ sarvaṃ pūrayatīti śivam.

And what are the final words?

Iti śivam.

इति शिवम्।

Thus, auspiciousness.

Thus, Śiva.

And that’s it.

There is no separate concluding metrical stanza after this sentence in the two checked witnesses. The chapter concludes in prose, followed by the authorial colophon.

Not another elaborate ritual.

Not another description of the tattvas.

Not another metaphysical argument.

Simply:

Śivam.

28. The end of the Tantrasāra — the whole bloody thing was Śiva
And now.

Consider what we’ve just read.

Twenty-two chapters.

We began with consciousness.

The supreme light.

Prakāśa.

And its power of self-apprehension.

Vimarśa.

We established its freedom.

Svātantrya.

Its capacity to manifest everything.

Then the apparent limitation of the individual.

The emergence of Māyā.

The tattvas.

The worlds.

The cosmic pathways.

The phonemes.

The mantras.

The different conditions of subjectivity.

And the way the one consciousness can appear as innumerable limited beings.

Then we discussed the methods of recognition.

Anupāya.

Śāmbhavopāya.

Śāktopāya.

Āṇavopāya.

And the various forms of divine immersion.

Samāveśa.

Then the descent of grace.

Śaktipāta.

The initiation of the disciple.

The purification of the cosmic pathways.

The sacred baths.

The ritual worship.

The consecration of gurus.

The rites associated with death.

The continuing conduct of initiated practitioners.

And the authority of the scriptures.

All of it.

And finally, here in Chapter Twenty-Two, we reach the Kaula sacrifice.

A progressive movement through increasingly interior forms of worship.

From the external support.

To Śakti.

To one’s own body.

To the divine pair.

To prāṇa.

And finally:

Saṃvid.

Consciousness itself.

And what was the supreme teaching?

That the divine reality isn’t confined to any particular manifestation.

Not merely the stillness.

Not merely the activity.

Not merely the bliss.

Not merely the absence of bliss.

Not merely the world.

Not merely withdrawal from the world.

But the unrestricted consciousness that is the reality of both.

Anuttara.

The unsurpassable.

And now we can finally understand why Abhinavagupta insisted upon the Kaula character of the ritual.

Because the whole point is the conscious integration of what appears differentiated.

The body.

The senses.

The vital power.

The Goddess.

The creative emission.

The universe.

The ritual participants.

And the awareness through which everything becomes manifest.

Nothing independent remains outside the ultimate reality.

And here’s the extraordinary irony.

The highest sacrifice doesn’t require sacrificing consciousness to attain something beyond it.

It’s consciousness recognising its own nature.

And the external rites become meaningful insofar as they support that recognition.

Now, think back to the beginning of the book.

Chapter One.

Remember the opening philosophical claim?

Jñānaṃ mokṣakāraṇam.

Knowledge is the cause of liberation.

And what was the ignorance that had to be overcome?

The contracted apprehension through which consciousness seemed limited.

And now the whole thing has returned to exactly that problem.

The apparent individual.

Paśu.

Believes itself separate.

The world appears other.

The deity appears distant.

The guru appears to possess some power that the disciple lacks.

The rituals appear to be complicated external mechanisms for acquiring something new.

But through the course of the teaching, Abhinavagupta has progressively undermined those assumptions.

The individual is a manifestation of Śiva.

The world is a manifestation of Śiva.

The deity is a manifestation of Śiva.

The guru’s liberating power is attributed to Śiva.

And the awareness through which all of these are apprehended?

Śiva.

So the ultimate problem wasn’t that the Supreme Lord had disappeared.

Or that consciousness lacked some foreign property.

Or that the universe had somehow escaped beyond divine freedom.

The problem was the limited apprehension of what was already present.

And the whole Tantrasāra has been a progressive clarification of that recognition.

Now, here’s the thing.

Abhinavagupta doesn’t end by saying:

“Reject the universe.”

Or:

“Reject the body.”

Or:

“Reject the senses.”

Or:

“Reject every experience.”

He concludes with a ritual tradition in which all of these become possible supports for recognition.

But neither does he conclude:

“Do whatever you like and call it Śiva.”

He has spent whole chapters distinguishing qualification, knowledge, appropriate conduct, initiation and the actual purpose of worship.

The recognition of nonduality is not the same thing as indiscriminate indulgence.

And that distinction is absolutely essential.

Because the Kaula path is not simply a theology of pleasure.

It’s a theology of conscious freedom.

Pleasure can appear.

Pain can appear.

Repose can appear.

Activity can appear.

The body can act.

The senses can move.

And all of these are expressions of the one consciousness whose nature is unrestricted.

Now, what does that mean for liberation?

Jīvanmukti.

Liberation while living.

Not merely the promise of attaining some other place after death.

But the recognition of the divine reality throughout embodied existence.

Karmaṇi karmaṇi.

In each action.

And that’s the extraordinary culmination.

Not that you have to spend your whole life performing increasingly elaborate rituals.

But that the ultimate nature of the ritual can be recognised within the very awareness through which life is experienced.

And then, after all those chapters, Abhinavagupta adds the seventh sacrifice.

The guru’s body.

The living embodiment of the transmission.

The recognition communicated through a qualified teacher.

And finally:

Iti śivam.

Thus, Śiva.

Now imagine the disciple closing the book.

Twenty-two chapters.

Thousands of Sanskrit words.

Extraordinary metaphysics.

Detailed rituals.

Cosmic hierarchies.

The most beautiful conceptions of divine consciousness.

And the disciple turns to Abhinavagupta.

“Right.”

“Yes?”

“I’ve finished.”

“Excellent.”

“So what’s the final teaching?”

And Abhinavagupta gives a little smile.

“You were never ultimately separate from the reality you’ve been studying.”

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“So what was all this bloody business about?”

“Understanding that.”

“Twenty-two chapters?”

“Twenty-two chapters.”

“All those tattvas?”

“Yes.”

“The six cosmic pathways?”

“Yes.”

“The mantras?”

“Yes.”

“The initiations?”

“Yes.”

“The sacred baths, the tridents, the Kālīs, the festivals, the divine couple and the entire Kaula ritual?”

“All of it.”

“And the result?”

“Recognition.”

Pratyabhijñā.

The recognition of one’s own essential nature.

And here’s the final joke.

You thought you were a little individual reading an enormous book to discover the nature of Śiva.

And Abhinavagupta has spent the entire book explaining that the reality through which the reading, the understanding, the doubt and the recognition occur is itself the supreme consciousness.

So, after all this time, who’s reading?

Who’s understanding?

Who’s questioning?

And who’s being recognised?

The whole bloody business is Śiva.

Iti śivam.

End of Chapter Twenty-Two — Kulayāgaprakāśanam.

End of Abhinavagupta’s Tantrasāra.
