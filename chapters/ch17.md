# Tantrasāra — Chapter 17

सप्तदशमाह्निकम् — Saptadaśam Āhnikam

Liṅgoddhāra: or, how to leave your old religion, get the previous gods to release your contract, and discover that Śiva doesn’t actually require a bloody transfer certificate.


Right then.

Last chapter was quite extraordinary, wasn’t it?

We discovered that apparently being dead is no excuse for missing your initiation.

A guru could, according to the tradition, contemplate an absent disciple, construct an enormous fiery net through the cosmic pathways, draw their consciousness into a ritual representation, and perform the appropriate liberation ceremony.

Absolutely magnificent.

But now we’ve got a rather different problem.

Because suppose you’re not dead.

You’re perfectly alive.

You’ve got two functioning legs.

A reasonable understanding of Sanskrit.

Perhaps a particularly impressive meditation cushion.

And you’ve finally discovered the teachings of Trika.

Wonderful.

Except there’s one slight complication.

You’ve already been initiated into another religion.

Perhaps you’re a Vaiṣṇava.

Perhaps you’ve taken vows within another Śaiva tradition.

Perhaps you’ve spent years following a particular guru, and now you’ve encountered Abhinavagupta’s teachings and decided that the supreme nondual reality of Bhairava is what you want to investigate.

What happens?

Do you simply abandon everything and begin chanting new mantras?

Do your previous vows still bind you?

Can you receive a higher initiation while remaining committed to an earlier religious discipline?

And what about the deity associated with your previous vows?

Is that deity going to be terribly annoyed?

Well.

Abhinavagupta has a ceremony for this.

Of course he does.

Liṅgoddhāra.

लिङ्गोद्धार

The removal or lifting away of the previous religious mark.

Now, before anyone gets concerned, liṅga here does not mean someone’s sexual anatomy.

Nor is the guru running around removing Śiva-liṅgas from people’s houses.

The relevant meaning is a sign or mark of religious affiliation.

And uddhāra means lifting out, removing or releasing.

So the purpose is to free an aspirant from the marks and obligations of an earlier religious commitment, allowing the appropriate initiation into the higher system as Abhinavagupta understands it.

And you’ll notice something immediately.

We’re not discussing how to convert somebody by winning a philosophical argument.

This is a ritual for a person in whom:

Śaktipāta has already occurred.

The supreme Power has begun directing their attention toward the new teaching.

That’s absolutely fundamental.

So let’s begin.

1. Who needs the previous religious mark removed?
Abhinavagupta opens with a rather extraordinary compound.

Vaiṣṇavādi-dakṣiṇa-tantrānteṣu śāsaneṣu.

Bloody hell.

Straight in, no warm-up.

Let’s unpack it.

Vaiṣṇava — belonging to Viṣṇu or a Vaiṣṇava tradition.

Ādi — beginning with.

Dakṣiṇa-tantra — the Dakṣiṇa or right-hand stream of tantric revelation.

Anta — ending with.

Śāsana — a system of teaching, religious doctrine or authoritative discipline.

So he’s referring to religious systems ranging from Vaiṣṇava teachings through the Dakṣiṇa tantras.

Now, a historical clarification.

Dakṣiṇa here belongs to the traditional classification of tantric scriptural streams. It shouldn’t simply be equated with the modern popular distinction between respectable right-hand Tantra and transgressive left-hand Tantra.

Abhinavagupta is describing a hierarchy of religious revelations.

And in that hierarchy he regards Trika as belonging to the highest teaching.

Uttama-śāsana.

Now, that is his doctrinal judgment.

We’re not going to pretend he considers every religious tradition equally authoritative.

He doesn’t.

He’s quite unapologetic about it.

And then he gives the first category of people who may require this rite.

Those established in the earlier systems.

Ye sthitāḥ.

And those who have accepted their vows.

Tad-gṛhīta-vratāḥ.

Gṛhīta — taken, accepted.

Vrata — vow or observance.

So these are people who have already bound themselves through religious commitments.

And that’s important.

Because a vow isn’t merely an interesting opinion.

Within this ritual culture, taking a vrata establishes a religious obligation.

A relationship with a deity.

A pattern of conduct.

An authorised form of worship.

Perhaps a specific mantra.

And the ceremony we’re about to investigate is designed to release that prior commitment.

Now, Abhinavagupta gives a second category.

And this one’s even more interesting.

Someone already established in the highest teaching but serving an unauthorised guru of a lower system.

Uttamaśāsanasthā api anadhikṛtādhar aśāsanagurūpasevinaḥ.

Let’s break down the central words.

Uttama — highest.

Adhara — lower.

Anadhikṛta — not properly qualified or authorised.

Guru-upasevin — someone attending upon or serving a teacher.

So imagine you’ve already entered the Trika tradition.

You know the philosophical hierarchy.

You’ve studied the teachings.

But you’re now following someone Abhinavagupta considers unqualified to act as your spiritual authority.

And he says you may also require this purification.

Now, notice what he isn’t saying.

He isn’t saying anyone who has studied two religious traditions must automatically undergo this rite.

The passage concerns previous vows or guru relationships that constitute an impediment within his initiatory hierarchy.

And we also shouldn’t infer that an actual guru becomes illegitimate simply because another religious group declares its doctrine superior.

That’s the historical sectarian framework speaking.

But within Abhinavagupta’s system, the problem is real.

A previous religious commitment may be understood as binding the aspirant to an order of practice that doesn’t culminate in the recognition being sought.

And now we reach the decisive condition.

2. You don’t just decide to change religions because you’re bored
The Sanskrit says:

Yadā te śaktipātena pārameśvareṇa unmukhīkriyante.

When they are turned toward the supreme teaching through the descent of Power from Parameśvara.

Now that’s beautiful.

Śaktipāta — descent of divine Power.

Pārameśvara — belonging to the Supreme Lord.

Unmukhīkriyante — they are made to turn toward, become inclined or face something.

Let’s look at that last word.

Unmukha.

Ud — upward, outward or forth.

Mukha — face.

The sense is turning one’s face toward something.

And isn’t that a magnificent description of spiritual attraction?

You’re going about your life.

Following a particular system.

Perhaps perfectly satisfied with it.

And then something happens.

You encounter a teaching.

Maybe a sentence.

Perhaps a passage from the Śiva Sūtras.

Or a verse from Utpaladeva.

Or one of Abhinavagupta’s arguments about consciousness.

And suddenly your attention turns toward it.

You can’t quite explain why.

You want to understand.

Not merely because someone has advertised it particularly well.

But because, within the tradition’s explanation, the divine Power is drawing you toward recognition.

Now, Chapter Eleven was extremely emphatic about this.

Śaktipāta isn’t ultimately manufactured by karma, merit or religious effort.

It’s an expression of Śiva’s own freedom.

Svātantrya.

And here the same teaching governs eligibility.

The ritual is prescribed when the aspirant has become oriented toward the new teaching through divine grace.

So the essential condition isn’t:

“I’ve decided Viṣṇu is terribly unfashionable this year.”

Or:

“I heard Kashmir Śaivism has some absolutely magnificent Sanskrit terminology.”

Or:

“I’d like a more impressive spiritual title.”

No.

The relevant movement is toward the higher recognition as this tradition conceives it.

And then Abhinavagupta says:

Tadā teṣām ayaṃ vidhiḥ.

Then this is their procedure.

Right.

Time to perform the ceremony.

And the first thing you’ve got to do is stop eating.

3. First, a fast
Kṛtopavāsam.

Having undertaken a fast.

Now, remember what we discussed about upavāsa?

The word is traditionally associated with dwelling near, although its normal ritual meaning is fasting.

Here it refers to the required fast before the ceremony.

The aspirant observes it, and on the following day:

Anyadine.

On another day, the next day.

The guru brings them before the Lord.

But first, the Lord is worshipped through:

Sādhāraṇa-mantra.

A general or common mantra.

Now this is a recurring feature of the chapter.

The rite doesn’t immediately invoke the aspirant’s former specific mantra.

Nor does it begin with the special mantra belonging to the new advanced initiation.

It employs a general formula.

Why is that appropriate?

Because we’re in a transitional condition.

The previous affiliation is being released.

The next full initiation has not yet occurred.

We’re between established religious commitments.

And the rite is explicitly preparing the candidate for the higher initiation.

So the general mantra serves the preliminary worship of Parameśvara.

Then the candidate is brought before the Lord.

But there’s a fascinating little clause here.

Tadīyāṃ ceṣṭāṃ śrāvitasya bhagavataḥ.

The Lord has been informed of the aspirant’s conduct or actions.

Let’s unpack that.

Tadīya — belonging to that person.

Ceṣṭā — conduct, activity, behaviour.

Śrāvita — caused to hear, informed.

Bhagavat — the Lord.

So the aspirant’s previous conduct is formally brought before Parameśvara.

Now imagine the scene.

The aspirant has fasted.

The ritual place is prepared.

The guru has worshipped Śiva.

And now the candidate is presented.

“Right, Lord.”

“We’ve got someone here.”

“Previously initiated elsewhere.”

“Has undertaken certain vows.”

“Now turned toward your supreme teaching.”

“And we’re here to release the previous obligations.”

Very formal.

Quite a ceremony.

But the significance is important.

The earlier commitment isn’t being treated as though it never existed.

It is acknowledged before the divine presence.

The ritual recognises that the candidate has a religious past.

And now that past affiliation is to be relinquished.

Which brings us to the most wonderfully concrete action in the entire chapter.

4. Take the old vow and throw it in the water
Here’s the Sanskrit:

ATTESTED PROSE — 17.1

Tatrāsya vrataṃ gṛhītvāmbhasi kṣipet.

There, having taken up the person’s vow, one should cast it into water.

Now that’s extraordinary.

Vrata — vow.

Gṛhītvā — having taken.

Ambhas — water.

Kṣipet — one should cast, throw or deposit.

Take the previous vow and put it into water.

Now, presumably you can’t physically pick up a vow like a rock.

It’s not a pair of sandals.

You can’t just reach into someone’s head, pull out a religious commitment and chuck it into a bucket.

So the action is understood through ritual representation or contemplative appropriation.

The Sanskrit does not specify exactly what physical object, if any, represents the vow.

And that’s worth being careful about.

We’re not going to invent a little paper certificate and claim Abhinavagupta tells you to burn it.

He doesn’t.

He says the vow is taken and cast into water.

Now, think about the symbolism.

A religious obligation has been accepted.

It has shaped the person’s identity and practice.

Within this tradition, it establishes a binding relationship with a particular order of religious authority.

And the rite symbolically removes it.

Not by pretending it never existed.

But by formally relinquishing it.

Now, here’s where the ceremony becomes almost comically simple.

The old religious commitment?

Into the water.

Splash.

Lovely.

But then comes another action.

Because apparently the candidate must also have a bath.

5. Then wash yourself
Tato’sau snāyāt.

Then the candidate should bathe.

Now, remember Chapter Twelve?

We spent an entire chapter discussing the meaning of purification.

And what did Abhinavagupta tell us?

Purity is immersion into the nature of Parameśvara.

Not merely washing dirt from the body.

The real impurity was the mistaken identification that one’s essential consciousness is something separate from Śiva.

And now, in this rite, bathing has a very particular function.

The aspirant has relinquished the old vow.

And undergoes purification before entering the new initiatory system.

The water marks the transition.

But here’s something important.

We shouldn’t pretend Abhinavagupta explicitly repeats the entire philosophical theory of Chapter Twelve here.

He doesn’t.

This chapter simply prescribes the bath.

The interpretation becomes clear when we read it within the larger ritual framework of the Tantrasāra.

So you’ve got the previous obligation relinquished.

Then the candidate bathes.

And after that?

Prokṣya.

The guru sprinkles the candidate with consecrated water.

Now, notice the distinction.

Snāna — bathing.

Prokṣaṇa — ritual sprinkling.

The first is the candidate’s bath.

The second is an act of consecration performed in the ritual sequence.

And then the candidate is prepared through two more things.

Caru.

Ritually prepared food, commonly cooked grain.

And:

Dantakāṣṭha.

A tooth-cleaning twig.

Yes.

We’re back to cleaning the teeth.

You can apparently transfer religious affiliations at the level of supreme metaphysics, but you’re still going to need a little stick to sort your mouth out.

And these are the same sorts of preliminaries we encountered in Chapter Thirteen.

We’re not going to recreate their entire description here.

Abhinavagupta is deliberately concise.

But the order matters.

Fast.

Present the aspirant before the Lord.

Relinquish the vow into water.

Bathe.

Sprinkle.

Consecrate with caru and the tooth-stick.

And now?

Blindfold the candidate.

Because we’re about to introduce them to the worship of Parameśvara.

6. Blindfolded again — the old identity goes, the new worship begins
Right.

Baddhanetra.

Blindfolded.

Remember Chapter Thirteen?

The disciple was brought into the consecrated space with their eyes covered.

And when the blindfold was removed, the tradition described the possibility of encountering the deity’s presence through faculties transformed by grace.

Well, we’re using the same ritual motif again.

The aspirant is brought inside with covered eyes.

And the guru causes them to perform:

Parameśvara-pūjā.

Worship of the Supreme Lord.

Using:

Sādhāraṇa-mantra.

The general mantra.

Now, here’s something worth noticing.

This is not yet the full initiation into the new tradition.

The previous vow is being relinquished, and the candidate is now worshipping Parameśvara through a general formula.

It’s a transitional act.

Imagine you’re changing from one religious discipline to another.

You’ve acknowledged the earlier commitment.

Released its vow.

Undergone purification.

And now the next act is not immediately to receive the most esoteric secret of Trika.

It’s to worship the Supreme Lord in a preparatory form.

Rather sensible.

But the ceremony isn’t finished.

Because vows aren’t merely ideas floating around inside the head.

Within this ritual system, they’re understood as establishing a real religious obligation.

So now Abhinavagupta moves into the fire rite.

And this is where the previous commitment receives its formal purification.

7. The fire must first become Śiva
Here’s the beginning of the next instruction:

Sādhāraṇa-mantreṇa śivīkṛte agnau.

In the fire that has been made Śiva through the general mantra.

Now, there’s an extraordinary verb here.

Śivīkṛta.

Śiva — the Supreme Lord.

Kṛta — made.

Made into Śiva.

Now, before we get carried away, the fire isn’t being presented as something that originally existed outside consciousness and must somehow be dragged into divinity.

Remember Chapter Thirteen.

We united the external ritual flame with:

Hṛdayāntar-bodhāgni.

The fire of awareness within the Heart.

And the fire became the consecrated expression of the divine power.

Here the same underlying ritual principle is operating.

The flame is made a suitable support for the action through mantra.

And within that consecrated fire, the guru performs:

Vrataśuddhi.

Purification of the vow.

Vrata — vow.

Śuddhi — purification.

Now think about the structure.

The vow was first relinquished in water.

But that isn’t the entire operation.

It must also undergo a formal purification in the fire.

Water and fire.

Two distinct stages.

The water receives the discarded commitment.

The fire purifies the outstanding religious obligation.

And here’s where the text gets very specific.

Because there’s a formula.

A name.

And precisely one hundred oblations.

8. The hundred offerings — religious paperwork by sacred fire
Abhinavagupta says:

Tan-mantra-sampuṭaṃ nāma kṛtvā.

The person’s name is incorporated with the mantra through a sampuṭa arrangement.

Now, sampuṭa suggests something enclosed or encased.

In ritual usage, it refers to a formula being enclosed by another mantric expression, or a name or formula being placed in such an enclosing arrangement.

The exact layout is compressed in this passage, so I’m not going to invent a fully expanded mantra that Abhinavagupta hasn’t written down.

But the central spoken words are explicit.

Prāyaścitta.

Expiation.

Atonement.

A ritual act addressing a transgression or religious fault.

Śodhayāmi.

From the verbal root śudh, to become pure, with the causative formation.

The previous religious obligation is being formally purified.

The traditional oblation exclamation.

One hundred.

“Bloody hell.”

Now, here’s something interesting.

You might imagine that, once the previous vow has been cast into water and purified in fire, the religious affiliation has simply disappeared.

Because apparently there’s a divine authority associated with the previous vows.

Personally.

10. Vrateśvara — the previous contract has a divine administrator

The Lord of the Vow.

13. The attested concluding verse — even someone who followed the wrong guru may be initiated
CLOSING VERSE ATTESTED IN THE DEVANĀGARĪ EDITION

अधरस्थोऽपि गाढेशशक्तिप्रेरितमानसः।संस्कृत्य दीक्ष्यो यश्च प्राङ्निरतोऽसद्गुरावभूत्॥

Saṃskṛtya dīkṣyo yaś ca prāṅ nirato’sadgurāv abhūt.

Right.

Let’s unpack the thing.

Adharasthaḥ api.

Even someone established in a lower system.

Adhara — lower.

Stha — situated or established.

Api — even.

Then:

Gāḍha-īśa-śakti-prerita-mānasaḥ.

Now that’s a magnificent compound.

Gāḍha — intense, powerful, deep.

Īśa — Lord.

Śakti — power.

Prerita — impelled, driven, directed.

Mānasa — mind.

So:

Someone whose mind has been powerfully impelled by the Lord’s Śakti.

Now that’s the essential qualification.

Not which religion they were previously born into.

Not which guru they used to follow.

Not how many years they spent in an earlier system.

But the present movement of divine Power.

Then:

Saṃskṛtya dīkṣyaḥ.

Having been ritually purified or prepared, they should be initiated.

And the final clause:

Yaś ca prāṅ nirato’sadgurāv abhūt.

Even someone who was previously devoted to an untrue or unqualified guru.

Now, that is particularly interesting.

Because you might imagine that someone who spent years following what Abhinavagupta regards as an unqualified teacher has permanently ruined their spiritual prospects.

But no.

The verse explicitly allows them to be initiated.

Once the appropriate śaktipāta has occurred and the ritual preparation has been performed, the previous affiliation does not permanently disqualify them.

Now that’s the whole chapter in one verse.

The candidate may have previously belonged to a lower system within Abhinavagupta’s hierarchy.

They may have followed a teacher he considers unqualified.

But if the Lord’s Power has genuinely turned them toward the supreme teaching, the appropriate release and initiation become possible.

Now, there are two things to distinguish here.

The sectarian claim is that Trika possesses the highest revelation and that other systems occupy lower positions.

That’s Abhinavagupta’s religious judgment, not an objective ranking established by this ritual.

But there’s also a more general philosophical implication.

A person’s previous commitments need not determine the entirety of their future spiritual life.

And that conclusion fits remarkably well with the earlier chapters.

Because śaktipāta isn’t ultimately determined by your karmic or institutional history.

It’s an expression of divine freedom.

Svātantrya.

And the previous religious mark can be relinquished.

End of Chapter Seventeen — Liṅgoddhāra
Now, consider what just happened.

This chapter was only six short passages of prose.

Rather a relief after the enormous initiatory instructions of Chapter Thirteen.

But it’s remarkably important.

Because we’ve encountered a situation that tests the relationship between spiritual freedom and religious obligation.

An individual has accepted vows.

Entered a tradition.

Followed a teacher.

Established a particular religious identity.

And then something changes.

A new spiritual attraction arises.

Within Abhinavagupta’s account, Śiva’s Power has turned the aspirant toward a different understanding.

But the previous vows still have ritual significance.

So the tradition provides a formal release.

And what’s particularly striking is that the release isn’t carried out by denying the previous commitment.

It’s acknowledged.

Presented before the Lord.

Relinquished.

Purified.

And its presiding divine authority is formally told that it no longer has a claim over the aspirant.

Then the new initiation can proceed.

Now, that is quite an elaborate solution to the problem of changing religious affiliations.

And the historical rules shouldn’t be treated as universally necessary for modern practitioners.

You don’t need a ritual to obtain permission to reconsider your beliefs, leave a community or stop following a teacher. Nor should any religious authority use vows to prevent someone from exercising their freedom of conscience.

But within Abhinavagupta’s world, vows are serious.

They establish obligations within a consecrated hierarchy.

And removing those obligations is itself a religious act.

Now consider the deeper irony.

We’ve spent sixteen chapters establishing that the entire universe is a manifestation of Śiva.

That every deity, every mantra, every individual and every possible act occurs within the one conscious reality.

And now someone says:

“Abhinava, I’ve been worshipping the wrong deity.”

Well, in the ultimate nondual sense, no deity could exist outside Śiva.

But at the level of differentiated religious practice, Abhinavagupta insists that not every doctrine or initiation is equivalent.

Ontological nonduality does not mean ritual and doctrinal equivalence.

And that’s a crucial distinction for understanding Trika.

Everything may be Śiva.

But not every way of understanding or worshipping Śiva is considered equally conducive to the highest recognition.

The hierarchy of teachings remains meaningful within manifestation.

Yet the ultimate Power through which someone turns toward recognition isn’t restricted by their earlier religious identity.

And that brings us back to the opening condition.

Śaktipāta.

The descent of divine Power.

A person may have spent years in a particular tradition.

They may have taken formal vows.

They may have followed teachers Abhinavagupta regards as limited.

But the emergence of that attraction toward the supreme recognition changes their relationship to the hierarchy.

And the rite enables the corresponding transition.

So there you are.

The old vows have gone into the water.

The candidate has bathed.

The fire has been consecrated.

The hundred oblations have been offered.

Vrateśvara has been politely informed that his services are no longer required.

And the disciple may now receive the appropriate initiation.

Quite an afternoon.

And after all that?

The ultimate reality hasn’t changed in the slightest.

Śiva was the reality of the aspirant before the change of affiliation.

Śiva is the reality of the aspirant afterward.

The ritual changes their religious commitments and initiatory qualification within the tradition.

It doesn’t manufacture a second supreme consciousness.

And that’s the final joke.

You spent an entire ceremony formally changing your relationship to the divine.

And the divine reality itself?

Never went anywhere.

The whole bloody business was Śiva from the beginning.

Absolutely magnificent.
