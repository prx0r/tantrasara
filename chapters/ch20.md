# Tantrasāra — Chapter 20

विंशमाह्निकम् — Viṃśam Āhnikam

Śeṣavartanaprakāśanam — The Rest of Your Bloody Life: or, what to do after you’ve been initiated, why your entire body is a temple, and how Abhinavagupta somehow turns daily worship into a six-part festival calendar, an esoteric drinking ceremony and a rather alarming set of rules for spiritual teachers.


Right then.

Twenty chapters.

Twenty bloody chapters.

We’ve investigated the essential nature of consciousness.

Cit.

Its freedom.

Svātantrya.

Its capacity to manifest the entire universe.

Śakti.

We’ve examined thirty-six tattvas, six cosmic pathways, innumerable divine worlds, the Sanskrit alphabet, the five great kalās and every conceivable distinction between bondage and liberation.

Then we discovered that apparently none of the divisions possesses an independent reality outside Śiva.

Wonderful.

And since then?

Well, we’ve had an extraordinarily busy time.

We’ve initiated disciples.

Consecrated gurus.

Built cosmic tridents.

Performed sacred baths.

Purified karmic bonds.

Investigated deathbed initiations.

Discovered that a deceased person can supposedly be initiated from a distance.

And, in the last chapter, learned that when a genuine master dies, their lineage may celebrate the day as a sacred festival.

Absolute magnificence.

Now, at this point, you’d be forgiven for thinking the whole business is finished.

You’ve received initiation.

Your bonds have been ritually purified.

The guru has bestowed the appropriate mantra.

And you’ve been introduced to the supreme reality of consciousness.

Congratulations, mate.

You’re Śiva.

Well.

You were already Śiva.

But you know what I mean.

So now what?

Do you spend the rest of your life sitting around looking enormously pleased with yourself?

Perhaps occasionally muttering ahaṃ śivaḥ while somebody brings you tea?

Do you abandon the rituals?

Stop studying?

Go off and live in a cave?

Well, not necessarily.

Because Abhinavagupta has an entirely new subject to discuss.

What do you actually do with the rest of your life?

And that’s the name of the chapter.

1. Śeṣavartana — what remains after initiation
Let’s break down the Sanskrit.

Śeṣa.

शेष

What remains.

The remainder.

Vartana.

वर्तन

Conduct.

Behaviour.

The manner of living.

So:

Śeṣavartana.

The conduct to be followed during the remainder of one’s life.

And Abhinavagupta begins:

तत्र या दीक्षा संस्कारसिद्ध्यै ज्ञानयोग्यान्प्रति या च तदशक्तान्प्रति मोक्षदीक्षा सबीजा तस्यां कृतायामाजीवं शेषवर्तनं गुरुरुपदिशेत्।

Tatra yā dīkṣā saṃskārasiddhyai jñānayogyān prati yā ca tadaśaktān prati mokṣadīkṣā sabījā tasyāṃ kṛtāyām ājīvaṃ śeṣavartanaṃ gurur upadiśet.

Now, that’s quite a mouthful.

But the argument is simple.

There are disciples capable of receiving the relevant knowledge.

Jñānayogya.

Qualified for knowledge.

And others who lack that particular capacity.

Tadaśakta.

Not capable of it.

For these different individuals, the initiatory system provides different functions, including the sabīja form of liberation-oriented initiation.

Sa-bīja.

With seed.

Now remember our earlier discussion of nirbīja?

Seedless initiation.

The form that can remove even the remaining bonds associated with prescribed observances.

Well, here we’re discussing the initiated person whose relationship to religious discipline continues.

And Abhinavagupta says:

The guru should instruct them concerning their conduct for the rest of their lives.

Ājīva.

For life.

Not until next Thursday.

Not for twenty-eight days.

Not until you’ve completed the introductory meditation course.

For the rest of your bloody life.

Now, that’s quite a commitment.

Imagine going to the guru.

“Right, I’ve received initiation.”

“Wonderful.”

“So I’m finished?”

“No.”

“How much practice remains?”

“The rest of your life.”

“Bloody hell.”

But this isn’t merely a demand for endless religious labour.

The whole point is to establish a continuing relationship with the recognition.

Remember something.

The ritual may have purified the fundamental bonds according to the tradition.

But the person’s embodied life continues.

They still act.

Think.

Experience.

Encounter situations.

And therefore their religious conduct becomes a continuing expression of their initiatory condition.

The question is no longer only:

How do I enter the state of Śiva?

It’s:

How does an initiated person live?

And Abhinavagupta gives three categories.

Naturally.

2. Three kinds of religious duty — the cosmic calendar begins
Right.

Here they are.

Nitya.

नित्य

Regular or obligatory.

Naimittika.

नैमित्तिक

Occasional, arising through particular circumstances.

Kāmya.

काम्य

Undertaken for a desired result.

Now, remember Chapter Eleven.

We distinguished people seeking liberation from those seeking particular enjoyments.

Bhoga.

Well, kāmya observances belong especially to the sādhaka pursuing particular attainments.

And Abhinavagupta says:

We’re not discussing those here.

So the chapter’s principal concern is with the first two categories.

Regular conduct.

And occasional religious observances.

But here’s where something subtle happens.

Because Abhinavagupta doesn’t define the essential regular practice merely as performing a fixed collection of outward actions.

He gives a much more important term.

Tanmayībhāva.

तन्मयीभाव

Now, this is a word worth learning properly.

Tat — that.

Maya — consisting of, made of, filled with.

Bhāva — being, state, condition.

The condition of being of that very nature.

And here, tat points toward the divine reality expressed through the mantra.

The state of identity with Śiva.

Now that’s extraordinary.

Because you might have expected:

“Your daily duty is to chant one thousand mantras, perform sixteen offerings, and wash your hands in precisely the correct fashion.”

But the real underlying requirement is establishing identity with the divine.

The rituals support that condition.

They don’t replace it.

And now Abhinavagupta introduces a technical debate.

Some teachers regard practices performed on regularly recurring dates as nitya, precisely because their time is fixed.

Svakāla-naiyatya.

Others distinguish genuinely occasional events from these predictable observances.

So a practice performed every morning may be regular.

A festival occurring at an established time may also be classified as obligatory.

But a spontaneous visit from the guru?

That’s an occasion.

It doesn’t necessarily happen every day.

Likewise, receiving sacred knowledge.

Or receiving a scripture.

Or the anniversary of an important religious event.

And Abhinavagupta preserves these distinctions rather than collapsing everything into a single word meaning “practice.”

Now, what are the necessary regular observances?

He names five principal forms.

Regular worship — niyata-pūjā.
Worship at the junctions of the day — sandhyopāsā.
Worship of the guru — gurupūjā.
Festival observance — parvapūjā.
The pavitraka rite of ritual renewal.
We’ll get to all of them.

Including the pavitraka.

Which, I warn you now, involves knots.

Quite a lot of knots.

Apparently all thirty-six tattvas are going to end up represented in a ceremonial cord.

But first, the occasional observances.

And this is a rather beautiful list.

Jñānalābha.

The attainment of knowledge.

Śāstralābha.

The acquisition of a scripture.

Guru-gṛhāgamana.

The guru’s arrival at one’s home.

And the arrival of the guru’s associated community.

Then important days connected with the teacher.

Birth.

Initiation or consecration.

Departure from the body.

Remember the last chapter?

The genuine guru’s death can be a sacred day for the lineage.

Well, here it appears as an occasion for special worship.

Then:

Laukikotsava.

A worldly or public festival.

Yes.

Even an ordinary festival can become an occasion for divine celebration.

And:

Śāstravyākhyā.

The explanation of scripture.

Specifically at its beginning, middle and conclusion.

Now, that’s rather interesting.

You’re reading a text.

Perhaps undertaking a serious course of study.

And Abhinavagupta says the stages of its exposition can be ritually significant.

Then:

Devatādarśana.

A vision or encounter with the deity.

Melaka.

An esoteric gathering or encounter, especially important in tantric contexts involving Siddhas and Yoginīs.

Svapnājñā.

A command received in a dream.

And:

Samayaniṣkṛtilābha.

The attainment of release or expiation concerning a religious observance.

All these constitute occasions for:

Viśeṣārcana.

Special worship.

Now isn’t that beautiful?

Because spiritual life isn’t being organised exclusively around the ordinary calendar.

It’s also organised around the actual unfolding of your relationship with the teaching.

You receive a scripture.

That’s significant.

You gain knowledge.

Significant.

Your guru visits.

Significant.

You encounter a particularly important experience.

Significant.

And each of these can become an occasion for worship.

Now imagine your friend coming over.

“What are we celebrating?”

“I’ve received a Sanskrit manuscript.”

“Right.”

“And it’s an absolutely magnificent one.”

“Wonderful.”

“Get the flowers.”

“Are we having a party?”

“Apparently.”

Now, I rather like that.

Because the tradition is treating intellectual and spiritual developments as events worthy of celebration.

But here’s the crucial point.

These aren’t merely social celebrations.

They’re understood as occasions when the radiance of consciousness becomes especially apparent.

We’ll return to that later.

First, we need to establish the daily practice.

And this is where the chapter becomes genuinely profound.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to describe one of the most beautiful forms of worship in the entire work.

4. The mantra must be received from the guru’s mouth
Right.

The disciple has received initiation.

Now the guru bestows the principal mantra.

Pradhāna-mantra.

But there are three important qualifications.

First:

Savīryaka.

सवीर्यक

Possessing potency.

Vīrya — power, vigour, efficacy.

Second:

Saṃvitti-sphuraṇa-sāra.

संवित्तिस्फुरणसार

Whose essence is the pulsation or flashing forth of consciousness.

Let’s break that down.

Saṃvitti — awareness, consciousness.

Sphuraṇa — flashing, pulsation, living manifestation.

Sāra — essence.

So the mantra isn’t being treated merely as a verbal password.

Its essential nature is connected with the living dynamism of awareness.

And third:

Alikhita.

अलिखित

Unwritten.

The mantra is transmitted through:

Vaktrāgama.

वक्त्रागम

The oral transmission, literally through the mouth.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Vaktrāgamenaiva arpayet.

It should be bestowed through oral transmission alone.

Now, that’s important.

Because this is a specific requirement of the initiated transmission.

It doesn’t mean every Sanskrit mantra in the world must be kept secret.

But for the principal mantra being discussed here, Abhinavagupta requires its transmission through the guru’s speech rather than writing it down.

And why is that philosophically interesting?

Because remember Chapter Three.

The powers of speech.

Parā.

Paśyantī.

Madhyamā.

Vaikharī.

We established that sound is not merely an arbitrary collection of letters.

The phonemic powers express the self-apprehension of consciousness.

And here the mantra is given through a living transmission.

The guru speaks.

The disciple receives.

But the essential thing isn’t merely the air vibrating between their mouths and ears.

It’s the communication of the mantra as a power of consciousness.

Saṃvitti-sphuraṇa.

Now, here’s something particularly important.

The disciple isn’t meant to become obsessed merely with possessing the syllables.

What must they attain?

Tanmayībhāva-siddhi.

The establishment of identity with the mantra’s divine nature.

And to accomplish this, the disciple practises at the:

Sandhyās.

The junctions.

The transitions of the day.

Traditionally morning, noon and evening.

But why those times?

Because the practice is meant to help establish the same recognition throughout the entire day.

Sarvakālam.

At all times.

Now, that’s a beautiful distinction.

You don’t meditate at sunrise simply so that Śiva can have fifteen minutes of your attention before you go back to forgetting him.

You practise at recurring moments so that the corresponding recognition becomes increasingly established.

The repeated practice creates or reinforces:

Saṃskāra.

A formative impression.

A disposition.

An established tendency.

And this is precisely what the initiated practitioner requires.

Not merely occasional philosophical excitement.

Not simply:

“Oh my God, everything is Śiva!”

Followed three hours later by complete absorption in irritation, attachment and distraction.

But an increasingly stable identification.

The recognition becomes familiar.

Natural.

Available.

And the means is daily practice.

Now, the disciple also worships Parameśvara every day.

Where?

Either upon a:

Sthaṇḍila.

A ritual altar or consecrated ground.

Or a:

Liṅga.

The sacred emblem or support of Śiva.

And here’s where things become genuinely gorgeous.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to turn the altar into a mirror.

5. The altar is a mirror — and the deity is your own Self
Right.

Imagine a beautiful altar.

Clean.

Prepared.

Perhaps adorned with flowers.

And Abhinavagupta says it is to be contemplated as:

Vimala-makura.

A spotless mirror.

Now, makura means mirror.

Vimala means stainless, spotless, without impurity.

And here’s the instruction.

Within that mirror-like altar, contemplate your own form as non-different from the entire circle of deities.

Svaṃ eva rūpam.

Your very own nature.

Yājya-devatā-cakra-abhinna.

Not different from the circle of deities being worshipped.

Now, that is absolutely magnificent.

Because imagine an ordinary act of worship.

You place a divine image before yourself.

You look at it.

You offer flowers.

And you think:

“There’s Śiva.”

“And here’s me.”

But Abhinavagupta says:

No.

Look at the deity as you would look at your own reflected face.

The form in the mirror isn’t some independently existing stranger.

It’s your own appearance.

And likewise the deity-circle is to be contemplated as non-different from your essential consciousness.

Now, this is the crucial point.

The practice doesn’t ask you to imagine that your ordinary limited personality has suddenly become an omnipotent cosmic ruler.

It asks you to recognise the divine consciousness underlying the apparent division between worshipper and worshipped.

Then comes the actual worship.

And Abhinavagupta gives a wonderfully rich collection of offerings.

Flowers.

Fragrances.

Consecrated wine.

Libations.

Food.

Incense.

Lamps.

Other offerings.

Hymns of praise.

Singing.

Instrumental music.

Dance.

And mantra repetition.

Now that’s a proper pūjā.

Not merely somebody sitting in silence looking very serious.

There’s music.

Poetry.

Fragrance.

Movement.

Taste.

Beauty.

And devotion.

Bhakti.

The entire sensuous and expressive world becomes part of worship.

Now, remember that this is a historical ritual system in which consecrated alcohol appears among the offerings. The passage is not a recommendation that drinking alcohol is necessary for contemplation or spiritual attainment.

But the larger point is remarkable.

Abhinavagupta isn’t demanding that all sensory richness be eliminated.

He is describing its integration into divine worship.

The flowers are not independent of consciousness.

The fragrance is not independent.

The music.

The dancer.

The deity.

The worshipper.

All are expressions of the one reality.

And through the ritual, the practitioner establishes the recognition of that non-difference.

What’s the stated goal?

Tanmayībhāvam aśaṅkitaṃ labdhum.

To attain identification with the deity free from doubt.

Aśaṅkita.

Unhesitating.

Free from uncertainty.

Now, imagine someone who has repeated a mantra for years.

But every time they practise, there’s a subtle division.

“Here am I, trying to reach Śiva.”

And Abhinavagupta says the whole practice is intended to overcome that division.

The mantra isn’t merely bringing you nearer to a distant object.

It’s a means through which the identity of consciousness becomes increasingly evident.

And then comes an extraordinary comparison.

6. Keep looking in the mirror until you recognise your own face
Abhinavagupta says:

Ādarśe hi svamukham aviratam avalokayataḥ tatsvarūpaniścitiḥ acireṇaiva bhavet.

Now, let’s break that down.

Ādarśa — mirror.

Sva-mukha — one’s own face.

Aviratam — continuously, without interruption.

Avalokayataḥ — of someone looking.

Svarūpa-niściti — certain recognition of its own form.

Acireṇa — before long.

So:

Someone who continually looks at their own face in a mirror soon becomes certain of its form.

Now, that is a beautiful analogy.

Because recognition becomes established through familiarity.

And the point isn’t merely visual memory.

It’s certainty about identity.

You look in the mirror.

And the image becomes unmistakably familiar.

You don’t have to ask every morning:

“Good God, who’s that enormous bloke staring back at me?”

You know.

Likewise, through the repeated contemplation of the deity as non-different from one’s Self, the intended identity becomes increasingly certain.

And here’s the extraordinary thing.

Abhinavagupta says:

Na cātra kaścit kramaḥ pradhānam ṛte tanmayībhāvāt.

No particular sequence is ultimately primary here, apart from the attainment of identity.

Now read that again.

We’ve spent seventeen chapters examining highly elaborate ritual sequences.

First do this.

Then that.

Then install the mantra.

Then offer the flowers.

Then contemplate the deity.

Then perform the mudrā.

Then make the oblation.

And now Abhinavagupta says:

The decisive thing is tanmayībhāva.

Identity with the divine nature.

Not the external sequence for its own sake.

Now, that doesn’t mean the initiated practitioner is free to disregard their formal commitments whenever they feel like it.

This chapter is specifically about observing them.

But Abhinavagupta is distinguishing the purpose of ritual from its particular arrangement.

The arrangement is the means.

The divine identification is the aim.

And then comes a line that might be the most beautiful prose in the entire chapter.

Because he invokes his own teachers.

Asmad-guravaḥ.

Our gurus.

And here’s what they taught.

7. The Heart of a person whose bonds have melted in devotion
Abhinavagupta describes someone who has entered:

Paramantra-tanmayībhāva.

Identity with the supreme mantra.

Whose stain of contracted individuality has ceased.

Nivṛtta-paśu-vāsanā-kalaṅka.

Let’s unpack that.

Paśu — the bound being.

Vāsanā — latent impressions or dispositions.

Kalaṅka — stain, blemish.

Nivṛtta — ceased, withdrawn, removed.

The stain of the latent tendencies of the bound individual has ceased.

Now, isn’t that an extraordinary way to describe liberation?

Not merely possessing a philosophical doctrine.

But the disappearance of the deeply rooted tendency to apprehend oneself as a restricted creature.

And then comes:

Bhakti-rasa-anuvedha.

The penetration of the flavour of devotion.

Bhakti — devotion.

Rasa — flavour, essence, aesthetic relish.

Anuvedha — penetration, pervasion.

And the result?

Vidruta-samasta-pāśa-jāla.

The entire net of bonds has melted.

Vidruta — melted, dissolved, liquefied.

Samasta — entire.

Pāśa — bond.

Jāla — net.

The whole network of bondage has melted under the penetrating flavour of devotion.

Now that is unbelievably beautiful.

Because remember our earlier discussion of grace.

We talked about the bonds of individual consciousness.

Mala.

Karma.

Māyā.

The conditions through which consciousness appears limited.

And here the language is no longer merely technical.

The bonds have melted.

Under the flavour of devotion.

Not merely been cut by an external instrument.

Not simply removed through an abstract intellectual operation.

The devotion itself penetrates and dissolves the contracted condition.

And what remains?

The Heart.

Hṛdaya.

And Abhinavagupta says that what resides in such a Heart is the supreme thing to be embraced.

Tad eva paramam upādeyam.

That alone is the highest thing to be adopted or attained.

Now, that’s magnificent.

Because the entire daily practice is directed toward establishing this condition.

The mantra becomes identified with consciousness.

The stain of limited individuality recedes.

The network of bonds melts in devotion.

And the Heart becomes filled with the supreme reality.

Not merely the physical organ pumping blood.

The spiritual Heart.

The living centre of self-awareness.

And apparently Abhinavagupta’s own gurus emphasised this.

Which brings us to two remarkable Sanskrit verses.

And they’re genuinely attested in the GRETIL text as well as the Devanāgarī edition.

8. The first hymn — worship with the vision of supreme nectar
ATTESTED SANSKRIT VERSE — 20.12–20.13

अधिशय्य पारमार्थिकभावप्रसरं प्रकाशमुल्लसति या।परमामृतदृक्त्वां तयार्चयन्ते रहस्यविदः॥
Adhiśayya pāramārthika-bhāva-prasaraṃ prakāśam ullasati yā.

Paramāmṛta-dṛk tvāṃ tayārcayante rahasyavidaḥ.

Now let’s understand what it says.

Pāramārthika — pertaining to ultimate reality.

Bhāva-prasara — the spreading or expansion of manifested realities.

Prakāśa — luminous awareness.

And:

Ullasati.

Shines forth.

Arises in splendour.

The verse describes a vision shining upon the luminosity in which real manifestation unfolds.

And this vision is:

Paramāmṛta-dṛk.

The vision of supreme nectar.

Parama — supreme.

Amṛta — deathless nectar.

Dṛk — vision, seeing, the seer or faculty of sight.

And who worships through it?

Rahasya-vidaḥ.

The knowers of the secret.

The initiates who understand the profound reality.

Now, this is a densely compressed verse, and the grammar is subtler than a simple English slogan.

But the contemplative meaning is clear.

The divine is worshipped through a vision of supreme nectar grounded in the luminous reality of manifestation.

Think about what we’re doing.

You aren’t simply directing attention toward an external object called God.

You’re recognising the consciousness through which every object appears.

And through that recognition, the act of seeing itself acquires a divine character.

The very vision becomes nectar.

Now, that’s a magnificent idea.

You worship Śiva through a vision whose nature is divine consciousness.

And then comes the second verse.

This one’s even better.

Because it contains an entire daily pūjā.

And the altar is the body.

The flowers are your own nature.

And the offering vessel?

Your Heart.

9. The second hymn — the body as the temple of Śiva
ATTESTED SANSKRIT VERSE — 20.14–20.15

कृत्वाधारधरां चमत्कृतिरसप्रोक्षाक्षणक्षालितामात्तैर्मानसतः स्वभावकुसुमैः स्वामोदसन्दोहिभिः।आनन्दामृतनिर्भरस्वहृदयानर्घार्घपात्रक्रमात्त्वां देव्या सह देहदेवसदने देवार्चयेऽहर्निशम्॥
Kṛtvādhāradharāṃ camatkṛti-rasa-prokṣākṣaṇa-kṣālitām āttair mānasataḥ svabhāva-kusumaiḥ svāmoda-sandohibhiḥ.

Ānandāmṛta-nirbhara-svahṛdayānarghārghapātra-kramāt tvāṃ devyā saha deha-deva-sadane devārcaye’harniśam.

Right.

Let’s unpack this magnificent bloody verse.

First:

Ādhāra-dharā.

The supporting earth.

And what happens to it?

It is washed through the sprinkling of:

Camatkṛti-rasa.

The flavour of wonder.

Now, camatkāra is one of the most beautiful concepts in Kashmir Śaivism.

Wonder.

Astonished relish.

The immediate wonder of conscious manifestation.

And here the supporting earth is washed with the very flavour of that wonder.

Now, that’s not ordinary water.

We’re describing an interior transformation of the ritual ground.

Then come:

Svabhāva-kusuma.

The flowers of one’s own essential nature.

Svabhāva — one’s own nature.

Kusuma — flower.

And those flowers have a beautiful fragrance.

Svāmoda.

They’re brought forth through the mind.

Mānasa.

Now, think about that.

Instead of merely picking flowers from a garden, the practitioner contemplates the flowers of their own nature.

And these are offered through:

Ānanda-amṛta-nirbhara-sva-hṛdaya.

The Heart filled to overflowing with the nectar of bliss.

And this Heart serves as:

Anargha-arghapātra.

The priceless offering vessel.

Let’s break that down.

Anargha — priceless, beyond valuation.

Argha — a respectful ritual offering.

Pātra — vessel.

So the priceless offering vessel is the Heart itself.

And where is the worship performed?

Deha-deva-sadana.

The divine dwelling of the body.

Deha — body.

Deva — god, divine.

Sadana — dwelling, residence.

And whom does the practitioner worship?

Tvāṃ devyā saha.

You, together with the Goddess.

Śiva and Śakti.

In the divine temple of the body.

When?

Aharniśam.

Day and night.

Now that’s absolutely magnificent.

The verse is essentially saying:

The earth of this sacred place is washed with the flavour of astonished consciousness.

The flowers are the fragrant powers of my own nature.

The offering vessel is the priceless Heart overflowing with the nectar of bliss.

And in this body, which is itself the dwelling of the divine, I worship you together with the Goddess, day and night.

Now.

If that isn’t one of the most beautiful forms of pūjā you’ve ever encountered, I don’t know what is.

Because the entire ritual has become an expression of consciousness.

The place.

The water.

The flowers.

The vessel.

The temple.

And the divine pair.

All understood through the Heart.

And here’s the extraordinary thing.

The verse isn’t simply rejecting external worship.

It is showing how the external ritual can be recognised in its interior, nondual significance.

You may still offer flowers.

You may still light incense.

You may still sing hymns.

But the highest understanding is that all those actions express the one divine reality.

And Abhinavagupta says the practitioner should internalise the meaning of both verses.

Then contemplate:

Devatā-cakra.

The circle of deities.

Now the daily ritual can be completed.

But there are a few technical details.

And, rather wonderfully, one of them concerns whether you should feed the leftover offerings to cats.

Yes.

Cats.

Apparently Abhinavagupta has an opinion.

10. Mudrā, mantra, dissolution — and what to do with the leftovers
Right.

We’ve contemplated the two verses.

The Heart is overflowing with bliss.

The body has become the temple.

The flowers express our own nature.

And the divine couple is worshipped within consciousness.

Then the pavitraka.

A ceremonial thread containing the entire hierarchy of cosmic manifestation.

Followed by the sacred exposition of scripture.

And the demand that a proper teacher reconstruct the opposing argument, explain it accurately and establish the intended conclusion without contradiction.

Then the expiation of broken observances.

And finally the worship of the guru.

The entire cosmic path becomes the teacher’s seat.

And the disciple offers not merely food and flowers, but their own Self.

Now, here’s the important thing.

The chapter doesn’t abandon the metaphysics we’ve spent nineteen chapters studying.

It puts that metaphysics into daily life.

The same consciousness.

The same Śakti.

The same freedom.

The same relationship between the limited subject and the supreme Self.

But now everything becomes an opportunity for recognition.

Eating.

Worshipping.

Studying.

Singing.

Receiving a scripture.

Seeing the guru.

Celebrating a festival.

Explaining philosophy.

Even the apparently mundane business of handling a ritual offering.

All can become expressions of the divine reality.

And the culmination is not merely accumulating an ever-growing collection of ceremonies.

It’s establishing:

Tanmayībhāva.

Identity with Śiva.

So imagine the disciple asking:

“Guruji, what should I do now that I’ve been initiated?”

And Abhinavagupta replies:

“Well, mate.”

“Worship every day.”

“Remember the mantra.”

“Practise at the sacred junctions.”

“See the deity as your own essential nature.”

“Celebrate the appropriate festivals.”

“Honour the guru.”

“Understand the scriptures.”

“Perform the relevant observances.”

“And through all of it, recognise that nothing is ultimately separate from Parameśvara.”

“Right.”

“For how long?”

“The rest of your bloody life.”

And here’s the final joke.

You thought initiation would end your spiritual practice.

But Abhinavagupta has explained that your spiritual practice can become the very manner in which you live.

The whole of life becomes worship.

Not because everything you do is automatically wise or liberating.

But because the appropriate recognition can transform your relationship to everything you do.

And in the highest condition?

You’re no longer worshipping Śiva as a foreign deity.

The worshipper, the worship and the worshipped are apprehended within one unrestricted consciousness.

The whole bloody business is Śiva.

Absolutely magnificent.
