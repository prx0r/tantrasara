# Tantrasāra — Chapter 16

षोडशमाह्निकम् — Parokṣadīkṣāprakāśanam

Initiation from a Distance: or, how Abhinavagupta decided that being dead, living in another country, or otherwise unavailable was no bloody excuse for missing your initiation.


Right then.

Last chapter was absolutely extraordinary.

We had the deathbed initiation.

Kālarātrī.

The Night of Time.

We had the severing of subtle bonds, the departure of the life-principle, the Brahmavidyā whispered into the ear, and the seedless initiation that supposedly destroys the very capacity for future karmic bondage.

Quite a lot for one chapter.

And now you might reasonably think:

“Right, Abhinava, that covers everyone, doesn’t it?”

Well.

Not quite.

Because there’s a problem.

Suppose someone desperately wants initiation.

They’ve served their guru.

Studied the teachings.

Perhaps even experienced śaktipāta.

The descent of divine power.

But they’re nowhere near their teacher.

Perhaps they’ve been exiled.

Perhaps they’re travelling.

Perhaps they’ve died before the initiation could take place.

What then?

Are we seriously going to tell them:

“Terribly sorry, mate.”

“You were supposed to attend in person.”

“Can’t perform the ceremony without your signature.”

“Please contact the initiations department during normal working hours.”

Absolutely bloody not.

Because the supreme Lord is not restricted by space.

We’ve spent an entire chapter proving that.

Remember Deśādhva?

The spatial path?

All the cosmic worlds, principles and realms exist within the power of consciousness.

And now we’re going to apply that teaching to something remarkably practical within the tradition.

Can a guru initiate someone who isn’t physically present?

Abhinavagupta says yes.

And he’s going to explain why.

Now, I want you to notice something.

This chapter is much shorter than the enormous ritual descriptions we’ve just read.

But philosophically it’s absolutely fascinating.

Because it forces us to examine what makes initiation effective.

Is it the physical presence of a disciple?

The maṇḍala?

The offerings?

The mantra?

The guru’s powers?

Or the consciousness through which all those things are manifested?

Right.

Let’s investigate.

1. Two kinds of absent disciple
Abhinavagupta begins with a wonderfully concise statement.

ATTESTED OPENING PROSE — 16.1

अथ परोक्षस्य दीक्षा द्विविधश्च सः — मृतो जीवंश्च।
Atha parokṣasya dīkṣā dvividhaś ca saḥ — mṛto jīvaṃś ca.

Now the initiation of the absent disciple.

And this disciple is of two kinds.

Mṛta.

Dead.

And:

Jīvan.

Living.

Now that’s rather extraordinary.

Because ordinarily, if someone tells you they cannot attend a ceremony, you might ask:

“Are they somewhere else?”

And the answer could be:

“Yes, they’re in another village.”

Fair enough.

Or:

“Yes, they’ve been dead for six months.”

Slightly more complicated.

But Abhinavagupta places both possibilities within the same category.

Parokṣa.

Let’s break that down.

Para — beyond, away from.

Akṣa — connected with the eye or perception.

Parokṣa — beyond immediate perception; not directly present.

So a parokṣa disciple is someone outside the guru’s immediate perceptual presence.

And here’s the remarkable thing.

The text isn’t merely concerned with the living disciple in another location.

It explicitly includes the deceased.

Now, we shouldn’t jump straight from that to assuming that rituals have experimentally verified effects upon deceased persons.

We’re examining what the Trika initiatory tradition claims is possible within its metaphysics.

And within that metaphysics, the separation of the physical body from the guru doesn’t necessarily establish an absolute separation of consciousness.

Because both the guru and the disciple are ultimately grounded in:

Paramaśiva.

Right.

But surely you can’t simply pick any dead person and perform the rite?

Excellent question.

Abhinavagupta has qualifications.

2. Who is actually eligible?
Now he gives the circumstances in which initiation of a deceased disciple is authorised.

And some of them are rather poignant.

First:

Kṛta-guru-seva.

Someone who served their guru.

Imagine a disciple.

They’ve found a genuine teacher.

They’re devoted to the teachings.

They’re attending upon the guru.

Preparing for initiation.

And then they die.

Before the ceremony can be completed.

Now, according to the tradition, the relationship already established through service and spiritual commitment makes them an appropriate candidate.

Death doesn’t simply invalidate that relationship.

Second:

Udvāsita.

Someone who has been expelled, banished or sent away.

Perhaps circumstances prevented their return.

Perhaps the disciple has become geographically inaccessible.

The text allows for initiation in such a case.

Third:

Abhicārādi-hata.

Someone killed through abhicāra and related causes.

Now, abhicāra is the term used for hostile or harmful ritual magic in traditional Indian literature.

Abhinavagupta is speaking from within a culture in which such rites were regarded as capable of causing harm.

We’re not treating alleged sorcery as an established cause of death.

But it matters that the Sanskrit includes this category.

Fourth:

Ḍimbāhata.

A difficult expression in this context, interpreted in available translations as death in childhood or through violent mishap.

The general point is premature or exceptional death, rather than someone completing the expected course of initiation.

Fifth:

Mṛtyu-kṣaṇa-udita-tathā-ruci.

A particularly beautiful expression.

Let’s unpack it.

Mṛtyu — death.

Kṣaṇa — moment.

Udita — arisen.

Ruci — inclination, desire, attraction.

Someone whose desire for initiation arises at the moment of death.

Now, isn’t that remarkable?

Imagine someone approaching the end of life.

Perhaps they’ve never seriously considered the teachings.

But suddenly, as their ordinary concerns begin to fall away, a powerful inclination toward liberation arises.

And Abhinavagupta says that such a person may be eligible.

Then finally:

Mukhāntara-āyāta-śaktipāta.

A descent of grace reported or known through another person’s communication.

Perhaps a third party informs the guru that the individual experienced śaktipāta.

And the appropriate initiation may be undertaken.

Now, Abhinavagupta closes the enumeration with:

Ity ājñā.

Thus is the command.

Meaning the scriptural authority for the rite.

Not simply:

“Well, I reckon it’d be nice to do something for them.”

This is a prescribed possibility within the tradition.

And here’s the broader implication.

The decisive religious question is not merely whether the individual can physically attend the ceremony, but whether the conditions of grace and qualification are present.

Now, there’s a practical difference between initiating someone alive and initiating someone dead.

And Abhinavagupta immediately addresses it.

3. The dead don’t need the usual preliminary retreat
Remember Chapter Thirteen?

Adhivāsana.

The preliminary consecration.

The elaborate preparation of the ritual ground, vessels, fire, offerings and disciple.

And remember how we spent ages arranging everything?

Well, Abhinavagupta says:

Atra ca mṛtadīkṣāyām adhivāsādi nopayujyate.

In initiation of the deceased, the preliminary consecration and related rites are not employed.

Now, that’s quite an administrative simplification.

“Right, we’ve got to initiate someone.”

“Excellent. Shall we begin the usual preliminary observances?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“He’s dead.”

“Ah.”

“Fair enough.”

But the reasoning isn’t merely convenience.

The preliminary consecration belongs to the preparation of a living candidate and their participation in the ritual.

The deceased candidate is in a different condition.

So the procedure must be adapted.

And that raises a really interesting issue.

What exactly makes a ritual effective when its normal physical conditions aren’t present?

This is where Abhinavagupta gives us an important hierarchy.

And it’s one of my favourite parts of the chapter.

Because he’s going to tell you what actually brings a deity’s presence into a ritual.

And apparently it’s not just having a particularly impressive collection of flowers.

4. What actually makes a deity present?
Right.

Suppose you’re preparing a maṇḍala.

And you want the presence of a particular mantra-deity.

Mantra-viśeṣa-sannidhi.

The presence or proximity of the particular divine power expressed by the mantra.

What produces that presence?

Abhinavagupta gives an ordered collection of contributing factors.

Kriyā — the appropriate ritual actions.
Upakaraṇa — excellent ritual materials, such as flowers.
Sthāna — a suitable sacred location, such as a pīṭha.
Maṇḍala — a consecrated diagram, such as the trident and lotus.
Ākṛti — a particular divine form used for contemplation.
Dīpta-mantra — the mantra itself in its luminous, awakened condition.
Yogin-sambandha — connection with a yogin dedicated to meditation.
Jñānin-sambandha — connection with a knower absorbed in single-minded devotion to the divine.
And he says these are causes of divine presence in their stated progression.

Yathottaram.

Now, notice the movement.

We begin with external activities.

Then physical materials.

Then sacred places.

Then ritual forms.

Then the mantra.

Then the meditating yogin.

And finally the awakened knower.

Isn’t that rather telling?

Because we’ve just spent several chapters discussing elaborate ritual preparations.

Flowers.

Altars.

Fire.

Vessels.

Sacred locations.

And Abhinavagupta is now directing attention toward the importance of realised consciousness.

The guru’s knowledge and divine absorption aren’t merely accessories to the ceremony.

They are central to its claimed efficacy.

Now, don’t misunderstand this as a simple equation in which one enlightened person always equals exactly seventeen ritual offerings.

The text is describing an ordered set of conditions that establish divine presence.

And then comes the beautiful line:

Samuditatve tu kā kathā syāt?

And if all these are present together, what more need be said?

Imagine you’ve assembled everything.

The proper ritual.

Excellent offerings.

An auspicious place.

The correct maṇḍala.

The contemplated deity.

A luminous mantra.

An accomplished yogin.

And a genuine knower absorbed in the divine.

Well.

According to Abhinavagupta, you’ve got quite an extraordinary conjunction.

And this is attributed to the supreme Lord’s own teaching.

But here’s the question.

If the guru can establish divine presence through consciousness and ritual, how does the guru bring an absent disciple into that field?

Ah.

Now we’re getting into something genuinely esoteric.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to construct a representation of the disciple.

And then describe an enormous fiery net extending through the subtle universe.

He calls it:

Mahājāla.

The Great Net.

And this time he explicitly credits a teacher.

Śrī Śambhunātha.

His revered guru.

Now that’s where things get very interesting.

5. The great fiery net — Śambhunātha’s extraordinary method
Right.

We’ve established the ritual field.

We’ve established that divine presence isn’t merely a question of arranging the right flowers.

And now Abhinavagupta introduces a method he attributes to the tradition of his own guru.

Śrī Śambhunātha.

Remember him?

The master whose teaching had such a profound influence upon Abhinavagupta.

Well, this particular method is called:

Mahājāla.

महाजाल

Mahā — great.

Jāla — net, mesh, network.

The Great Net.

Now, don’t imagine this is a fishing net.

We’re discussing a subtle yogic visualisation involving the entire network of cosmic manifestation.

First, the guru worships the deity.

Devaṃ pūjayitvā.

Then places before himself a representation of the absent disciple.

Tadākṛti.

The likeness of that individual.

And what is it made of?

Kuśa.

Sacred grass.

Or another suitable substance.

Now imagine someone walking into the ritual chamber.

“Guruji, who’s that?”

“The disciple.”

“That’s a bundle of grass.”

“Yes.”

“But the actual disciple is dead.”

“Yes.”

“And you’re going to initiate the grass?”

“No, you fool. The grass is a ritual representation of the disciple.”

“Ah.”

“Right.”

“Carry on.”

Now, here’s the crucial distinction.

The grass itself is not regarded as having somehow turned into the complete living person.

It’s serving as a focal point through which the absent individual is contemplated.

And Abhinavagupta says that the guru should apprehend this representation through the knowledge received from their own teacher.

Gurvāsādita-jñānopadeśa-krama.

The method of instruction and knowledge obtained from the guru.

This isn’t presented as something you improvise because you’ve had an interesting idea.

It’s a transmitted esoteric procedure.

And now comes the extraordinary visualisation.

The subtle power arises from:

Mūlādhāra.

The root support.

And extends through a vast structure of subtle channels.

Nāḍī.

Now, remember our earlier discussions of the subtle body?

Prāṇa.

The channels.

The cosmic pathways.

The upper centres.

Well, here we’re bringing all of that into a single remarkable image.

The subtle channels become an enormous staff.

Nāḍyadhva-daṇḍa.

A staff or axis of the pathways of the subtle channels.

And this staff extends upward.

Toward:

Nāsā-gagana.

The space associated with the nose.

Then the guru’s visualised power expands outward.

And apparently we’re going to cover the entire cosmic path in fire.

Yes.

The entire thing.

Again.

Abhinavagupta really does have a habit of setting the universe ablaze whenever he needs to get anything done.

Now, the Sanskrit describes a great network of flame-tips.

Enveloped in beautiful smoke.

And these flames spread across:

Adhva-cakra.

The wheel of the cosmic pathways.

What is their purpose?

To bring the desired individual into the ritual field.

Abhīṣṭa-jīvānayana.

The bringing of the intended living principle.

And Abhinavagupta gives this description in an actual metrical passage.

6. The first attested verse — fire through the entire subtle cosmos
ATTESTED METRICAL PASSAGE — 16.6–16.7

मूलाधारादुदेत्य प्रसृतसुविततानन्तनाड्यध्वदण्डं वीर्येणाक्रम्य नासागगनपरिगतं विक्षिपन्व्याप्तुमीष्टे।यावद्धूमाभिरामप्रचिततरशिखाजालकेनाध्वचक्रं सञ्छाद्याभीष्टजीवानयनमिति महाजालनामा प्रयोगः॥

Mūlādhārād udetya prasṛta-suvitatānanta-nāḍyadhva-daṇḍaṃ vīryeṇākramya nāsā-gagana-parigataṃ vikṣipan vyāptum īṣṭe.
Yāvad dhūmābhirāma-pracitatara-śikhā-jālakena adhva-cakraṃ sañchādya abhīṣṭa-jīvānayanam iti mahājāla-nāmā prayogaḥ.
Now, the Sanskrit is extremely compressed, so let’s unfold the meaning rather than pretending the entire thing is a straightforward modern instruction manual.

The contemplated power rises from the root.

It takes command of an enormously extended staff of subtle channels.

It reaches the space associated with the nose.

It projects outward.

And through a network of smoke-adorned flames, it encompasses the cosmic pathways.

The intended result is the drawing of the absent individual into the ritual’s contemplative field.

And that’s called:

The Great Net.

Now, imagine the visual scale of this.

Not merely a glowing triangle.

Not a little imaginary flame sitting between your eyebrows.

An entire network of divine radiance spreading through the cosmic pathways.

All those worlds.

All those tattvas.

Every level of manifest existence.

And the guru is contemplating their pervasion through one luminous structure.

But don’t miss the philosophical significance.

Remember what we said about deśa?

Space?

The supreme consciousness isn’t confined to a particular position within it.

The cosmic spatial manifestation itself arises through consciousness’s freedom.

And this rite is being explained within that worldview.

The guru doesn’t need to imagine an independently existing material cord stretching from Kashmir to some distant village.

The ritual is based upon the idea that the relevant cosmic pathways are already contained within the field of Śiva’s manifestation.

Now, whether this contemplation can actually reach the consciousness of a deceased person is a traditional religious claim, not an established capability.

But that’s the mechanism Abhinavagupta is describing.

And then comes a second attested verse.

This one’s especially interesting because it tells us exactly where the teaching came from.

7. The second verse — the great net of Śambhunātha
ATTESTED METRICAL PASSAGE — 16.8–16.9

एतेनाच्छादनीयं व्रजति परवशं सम्मुखीनत्वमादौ पश्चादानीयते चेत्सकलमथ ततोऽप्यध्वमध्याद्यथेष्टम्।आकृष्टावुद्धृतौ वा मृतजनविषये कर्षणीयेऽथ जीवे योगः श्रीशम्भुनाथागमपरिगमितो जालनामा मयोक्तः॥

Now the idea is this.

Through the net, the intended object of the rite is brought under its influence and made present within the contemplation.

The language includes:

Paravaśa.

Under another’s influence.

And:

Sammukhīnatva.

The condition of facing or becoming present before the practitioner.

The verse then speaks of drawing the intended being from within the cosmic pathways.

But here’s the really important statement.

Śrī-Śambhunātha-āgama-parigamita.

Received through the teaching tradition of venerable Śambhunātha.

And:

Jāla-nāmā mayoktaḥ.

The method named Net has been explained by me.

Now, that’s magnificent.

Because Abhinavagupta is not presenting himself as inventing the entire technique from nowhere.

He’s placing it within a lineage.

And that matters.

The authority of the teaching isn’t merely:

“Well, I had a very interesting meditation yesterday.”

It’s associated with his own guru’s transmission.

And we should also notice that the verse mentions both drawing in and raising up the individual.

Ākṛṣṭi.

Attraction or drawing.

Uddhṛti.

Lifting or rescuing.

And it explicitly concerns the deceased.

So this isn’t merely a metaphorical description of imagining someone who lives far away.

It’s a traditional claim about the ritual retrieval or liberation of a deceased being.

Now, the wording is powerful and, if treated as a proposed means of influencing living people without consent, ethically problematic. We’re examining its historical religious meaning, not endorsing coercive ritual practices.

But here’s a wonderfully interesting problem.

Because Abhinavagupta immediately anticipates an objection.

And it’s a very good one.

8. If the guru can do this, why can’t everyone perform magic instantly?
Right.

Here’s the objection.

“Abhinava, mate.”

Go on.

“If a yogin can produce this extraordinary result without years of ordinary practice, why can’t someone do the same thing in everyday life?”

Interesting.

Because we’ve got traditions of:

Ākarṣaṇa.

Attraction.

And other forms of ritual influence.

And some of these involve extensive practice.

Mantra repetitions.

Consecrations.

Specific ritual observances.

Sometimes years of effort.

So why can’t someone simply say:

“Right. I’ll just manifest the result through divine freedom.”

Excellent question.

And Abhinavagupta has a very precise answer.

Rāga-dveṣa.

Attachment and aversion.

Now, remember these.

Rāga — attraction, desire, clinging.

Dveṣa — aversion, hostility, rejection.

These are conditions associated with limited subjectivity.

And when a person attempts an extraordinary operation from the standpoint of desire and aversion, they’re not necessarily established in the unrestricted lordship of consciousness.

Aiśvarya-āveśa.

Immersion in divine sovereignty.

So they’re still operating under:

Niyati.

Determination.

The limiting principle through which the world exhibits particular constraints and causal regularities.

Remember Chapter Eight?

Niyati governs the ordinary relations through which actions produce particular results.

And the limited individual doesn’t simply escape those conditions because they happen to want something.

So Abhinavagupta says that ordinary operations may depend upon:

Abhyāsa.

Repeated practice.

And other supporting conditions.

Now imagine someone announcing:

“I’m infinite Śiva, so I should be able to manifest a gold palace immediately.”

And Abhinavagupta asks:

“Are you actually established in unrestricted divine freedom?”

“Well, no.”

“Are you acting through attachment to a particular result?”

“Yes.”

“Then why the bloody hell are you surprised that your actions remain subject to limitation?”

There’s your distinction.

The ordinary practitioner is operating within desire and determination.

But the true guru’s liberating action is understood differently.

Why?

Because it arises through:

Anugrahātmaka-parameśvaratāveśa.

Immersion in supreme lordship whose nature is grace.

Now that’s a magnificent compound.

Anugraha — grace.

Ātmaka — having the nature of.

Parameśvaratā — supreme lordship.

Āveśa — immersion, entry or possession.

So:

Immersion in supreme lordship characterised by grace.

And Abhinavagupta says the guru’s action rests upon that.

Not ordinary desire.

Not hostility.

Not the wish to dominate another individual.

But the divine operation of grace.

Now comes one of the most important statements in the chapter.

9. It isn’t ultimately the guru doing the initiating
Here’s the actual Sanskrit:

ATTESTED PROSE — 16.13

परमेश्वर एव हि गुरुशरीराधिष्ठानद्वारेणानुग्राह्याननुगृह्णाति।
Parameśvara eva hi guru-śarīrādhiṣṭhāna-dvāreṇa anugrāhyān anugṛhṇāti.

Now, this is absolutely fundamental.

Let’s break it down.

Parameśvara eva — the Supreme Lord alone.

Guru-śarīra — the body of the guru.

Adhiṣṭhāna — presiding over, inhabiting or governing.

Dvāreṇa — through the doorway or medium.

Anugrāhyān — those fit to receive grace.

Anugṛhṇāti — bestows grace upon.

So:

The Supreme Lord himself bestows grace upon the appropriate recipients through the medium of the guru’s body.

Now read that again.

Because it’s the entire metaphysical principle of initiation.

The guru isn’t an independently existing spiritual magician.

Not someone who has acquired a mysterious private force and now possesses authority over the souls of other people.

The genuine guru is understood as a manifestation through which Śiva’s own power operates.

And remember what we established in Chapter Eleven?

The true guru is qualified by:

Pūrṇa-jñāna.

Complete knowledge.

The realisation of the supreme nature of consciousness.

That’s why Abhinavagupta keeps insisting upon the teacher’s knowledge.

Because the ritual isn’t supposed to depend ultimately upon personal charisma or occult ambition.

It is grounded in the recognition of Śiva.

Now, you might still ask:

“But HOW could Śiva act through the guru in that way?”

And Abhinavagupta says:

Acintya-mahimā.

Of inconceivable greatness.

The majesty of the supreme Lord exceeds the ordinary limited individual’s understanding.

Now, that doesn’t give us an independently verifiable explanation of remote initiation.

It’s a theological explanation grounded in the doctrine of divine freedom.

But within Trika, it’s coherent with the metaphysics we’ve been developing.

The same power that manifests the apparent separation between guru and disciple is not itself bound by that separation.

And with that, we arrive at the next part of the rite.

The representation of the disciple.

And this one’s particularly peculiar.

Because apparently the representation can move.

Or not.

Depending upon the contemplative conditions.

10. The little grass disciple apparently doesn’t move — until it does
Right.

Remember the representation?

The likeness of the deceased disciple?

Made of:

Darbha.

Sacred grass.

Or:

Jātīphala.

A fruit associated in this context with nutmeg.

Now, the Sanskrit describes the intended jīva as entering that ritual representation through the Great Net operation.

Samāviṣṭa.

Entering or occupying.

But then Abhinavagupta makes a very sensible observation.

The representation doesn’t ordinarily move.

Na spandate.

Why?

Because it lacks the complete apparatus of a living embodied individual.

Manas.

Mind.

Prāṇa.

Vital energy.

And the other relevant supporting conditions.

Manaḥ-prāṇādi-sāmagrī.

Now, imagine the guru looking at the little grass effigy.

“Right.”

“He’s here.”

The disciple beside him looks puzzled.

“Guruji, it’s not moving.”

“Of course it isn’t moving.”

“Why not?”

“Because it’s made of grass, you absolute idiot.”

Now, that’s not quite Abhinavagupta’s wording.

But the distinction is real.

The ritual representation isn’t ordinarily a functioning living body.

However, Abhinavagupta says that through the power of concentrated contemplation, movement may also be manifested.

Tad-anudhyāna-balāt tu spandate’pi.

Through the strength of meditation upon it, it may even move.

Now, this is a traditional extraordinary claim.

It isn’t evidence that a constructed effigy can literally become animated by a deceased person’s consciousness.

But the role of the passage within the ritual is clear.

The representation is not treated as an empty prop.

It’s understood as the focal embodiment through which the absent disciple’s presence is ritually established.

And then something rather familiar happens.

Because we’re going to initiate this representation using the very same procedures we’ve already learned.

11. The full consecration — from sprinkling to the complete oblation
Right.

Now the representation has been established as the disciple’s ritual locus.

What comes next?

Prokṣaṇa.

Sprinkling with consecrated water.

Then the further:

Saṃskāras.

Consecrations.

And the sequence extends all the way to:

Pūrṇāhuti.

The complete oblation.

And:

Yojanikā.

Joining the disciple to the intended state.

Now, remember Chapter Fourteen?

We explained the purification of the cosmic pathways, the offerings associated with the tattvas, and the final joining into the supreme condition.

Well, Abhinavagupta isn’t going to repeat the entire procedure.

He says:

Pūrvavat.

As before.

The method already explained.

And that is important for following the chapter faithfully.

There’s no separate enormous new initiation system being introduced here.

Instead, the established initiation is adapted to the absent disciple.

But with one significant addition.

And this is where the ritual’s symbolism becomes particularly beautiful.

The representation must be dissolved into the supreme light.

12. The grass effigy dissolves into Paratejas
Here’s the actual Sanskrit:

ATTESTED PROSE — 16.16

अत्र परं पूर्णाहुत्या तस्य दार्भाद्याकारस्य परतेजसि लयः कर्तव्यः।
Atra paraṃ pūrṇāhutyā tasya dārbhādyākārasya paratejasi layaḥ kartavyaḥ.

Now, the meaning.

Through the complete oblation, the form made of sacred grass or another suitable material is to be dissolved into:

Paratejas.

The supreme radiance.

Let’s break that down.

Para — supreme.

Tejas — radiance, luminous power.

And:

Laya — dissolution, absorption.

So the representation is ritually absorbed into the supreme light.

Now, isn’t that extraordinary?

Because the whole operation began with something limited.

A particular individual.

Perhaps someone deceased.

A representation made of grass.

A definite form.

Located at one particular place.

Then the guru contemplated the complete cosmic pathways.

Invoked the divine powers.

Established the individual within the ritual representation.

And now that representation is dissolved into the supreme radiance.

The limited form is brought into its ultimate conscious ground.

Now, this isn’t simply a ritual of remembrance in the modern sense.

Abhinavagupta is claiming that the operation has a real soteriological effect upon the individual represented.

But the symbolic structure is profoundly consistent with the rest of the Tantrasāra.

Remember what we said about the tattvas?

The lower principles are pervaded by the higher.

And all ultimately rest in Śiva.

Remember the fire-wheel of Chapter Five?

Manifestation.

Maintenance.

Dissolution.

Return to the unsurpassable.

Well, here’s another ritual enactment of the same fundamental movement.

A particular form appears.

It’s established.

It’s consecrated.

And it’s absorbed into the supreme reality.

Now, here’s where the chapter becomes especially interesting.

Because Abhinavagupta distinguishes the result according to the kind of existence in which the departed individual is situated.

And he doesn’t say exactly the same thing for every case.

13. What happens if the deceased has been reborn in another realm?
Right.

Suppose the individual has died.

But their consciousness, according to the traditional rebirth cosmology, is now situated somewhere else.

Where?

Abhinavagupta gives four possibilities.

Svar.

A heavenly world.

Naraka.

A hell realm.

Preta.

A ghostly or departed-spirit condition.

Tiryak.

An animal or nonhuman birth.

Now, remember what we discussed in earlier chapters.

The bhuvanas.

The many worlds.

The numerous states of limited subjectivity.

And the hierarchy of rebirth.

Well, here those worlds become directly relevant to the initiation.

Abhinavagupta says that if the intended individual is situated in one of these conditions, they are liberated through the complete oblation.

Pūrṇāhutyā eva apavṛjyate.

Liberated through the final offering itself.

Now, that’s an enormously strong traditional claim.

The ritual is understood to reach a deceased individual even after rebirth into another realm.

And its result is liberation.

But here’s something surprising.

Human rebirth is treated differently.

And the reason is genuinely interesting.

14. A human rebirth doesn’t receive exactly the same result
Suppose the deceased individual has been reborn as a human.

Manuṣya.

Now, what happens through the rite?

Abhinavagupta says the person receives one of several possibilities.

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Yoga.

Yogic practice or attainment.

Dīkṣā.

Initiation.

Viveka.

Discernment.

Now, that’s quite a distinction.

For those situated in the heavenly, infernal, ghostly or animal states, the rite is said to liberate them through the final oblation.

But for a human rebirth, the result may be the manifestation of liberating knowledge, yoga, initiation or discrimination.

Why?

Adhikāri-śarīratva.

Because the human body is considered a suitable vehicle for the relevant spiritual undertaking.

Now, think about that.

Abhinavagupta isn’t treating every mode of embodiment as spiritually identical.

The human condition has a particular capacity.

The capacity to receive instruction.

To practise yoga.

To recognise philosophical distinctions.

To participate in initiation.

And that’s why the ritual’s result may become manifest through those capacities rather than simply being described as immediate final liberation.

Now, this is genuinely fascinating.

Because it suggests that within the traditional account, the remote initiation can work through the recipient’s existing embodied conditions.

It doesn’t necessarily abolish them.

And the result may be the arising of a new spiritual capacity within the person’s life.

Knowledge.

Yoga.

Discernment.

Initiation.

And Abhinavagupta concludes this section with:

Iti mṛtoddharaṇam.

Thus is the raising or rescuing of the deceased.

Mṛta — deceased.

Uddharaṇa — lifting up, rescuing, raising out.

Now, that’s a powerful expression.

The whole purpose of the rite is understood as the liberation or elevation of the departed individual.

And it links beautifully with the earlier verse of the Great Net.

Ākṛṣṭi.

Drawing.

Uddhṛti.

Raising.

The very same conceptual pair.

But here’s the important point.

Abhinavagupta isn’t presenting the ritual as an ordinary mechanism by which living people can reliably determine the afterlife condition of someone who has died.

These are traditional claims concerning rebirth, divine grace and ritual efficacy.

They cannot be verified simply by performing a ceremony.

Still, the inner structure of the teaching is quite remarkable.

And now comes the other half of the chapter.

We’ve discussed the dead.

What about someone who’s alive but absent?

Because this is where things become considerably simpler.

15. Initiating someone alive from a distance
Now we reach:

Jīvato’pi parokṣasya.

Even in the case of someone living but absent.

And here’s the crucial condition.

Utpanne śaktipāte.

When the descent of divine power has arisen.

Now, that’s important.

The rite isn’t presented as a licence to initiate arbitrary living people against their wishes.

The scriptural condition is that śaktipāta has arisen.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Ayam eva kramaḥ.

The same procedure applies.

But with an exception.

And it’s a rather large exception.

Dārbhākṛti-kalpanā-jīvākṛṣṭi-varjam.

Excluding the creation of a grass representation and the drawing of the living principle.

Now, think about that.

For the deceased, the rite included a representation and the Great Net operation.

But for someone living elsewhere?

The guru doesn’t need to employ those elements.

Why?

Because the absent living person can be established through contemplation alone.

Dhyānamātra-upasthāpita.

Made present by meditation alone.

Now that’s a beautiful expression.

Dhyāna — meditation.

Mātra — merely, alone.

Upasthāpita — made present, brought before one.

So the absent disciple is established within the guru’s meditation.

And the relevant consecration is performed in relation to that contemplated presence.

Now, don’t imagine this means the guru has to sit there constructing an ordinary realistic portrait of the disciple’s face.

The critical issue is the person’s presence within the contemplative and ritual field.

And the philosophical explanation is the one we’ve already encountered.

The guru’s liberating activity is grounded in Śiva’s power of grace.

Not in a physical rope connecting two bodies.

Now, this is a radical application of Trika’s nondual account of consciousness and space.

But again, a living person’s spiritual commitments and consent should not be presumed simply because someone else claims to have initiated them remotely.

The doctrine of ritual efficacy is not evidence of an actual change in another person’s status or experience.

Now, here’s something interesting.

You might think the initiation would necessarily produce only one result.

Liberation.

But Abhinavagupta says no.

It can have two kinds of result.

And the explanation returns us to a word we encountered several chapters ago.

Vāsanā.

16. Your old desires don’t necessarily vanish just because you’ve been initiated
Right.

What does remote initiation bestow?

Abhinavagupta says:

Bhoga-mokṣa-ubhaya-dāyinī.

Giving both enjoyment and liberation.

Bhoga — enjoyment, experience, sometimes extraordinary attainments.

Mokṣa — liberation.

Ubhaya — both.

Now, why both?

Because different individuals possess different dispositions.

And the Sanskrit says:

Svavāsanā-balīyastva.

The greater strength of one’s own latent tendencies.

Now, remember vāsanā.

A residual impression.

A disposition.

A tendency toward a particular kind of experience.

And Abhinavagupta makes a very practical observation within his doctrine.

The desires associated with enjoyment cannot simply be assumed to disappear at once.

Bhogavāsanā-vicchedasya asambhāvyamānatvāt.

Because the severing of the tendencies toward enjoyment may not be feasible for many practitioners.

Now imagine a disciple announcing:

“Guruji, I want supreme liberation.”

“Excellent.”

“And I’ve renounced all worldly desire.”

“Wonderful.”

“And I’m completely detached from every possible attainment.”

“Very impressive.”

“Although, if there’s a particular siddhi that lets me understand all languages, I wouldn’t mind that.”

Ah.

There it is.

Still wants a little something.

And Abhinavagupta isn’t ignoring that complexity.

The ritual system recognises that practitioners may retain desires for particular experiences even when genuinely drawn toward liberation.

Now, here’s where the Sanskrit becomes quite compressed.

It introduces:

Ūrdhva-śāsana-saṃskāra.

The transformative impression or consecration associated with the higher teaching.

And contrasts it with another saṃskāra.

The exact syntactical relationship is debated in translation, but the passage points toward the stronger formative influence of the higher dispensation even while other tendencies and ritual effects remain involved.

Now, that’s important.

Because the initiation isn’t simply understood as erasing every previous psychological inclination.

Rather, the higher teaching establishes a powerful orientation.

And other forms of conditioned experience may still require transformation or fulfilment.

So the person may become initiated into the supreme teaching while retaining tendencies that condition the way its results unfold.

And that makes perfect sense in light of Chapter Eleven.

Remember the nine grades of śaktipāta?

Some people awaken immediately.

Some require a guru.

Some attain liberation after the present life.

Some still desire particular experiences.

And some require more than one embodiment.

Different conditions.

Different manifestations of the same power.

The present chapter applies a related distinction to remote initiation.

Now, there’s one final point Abhinavagupta wants to establish.

And it’s very important.

An absent initiate can manifest the fruits of initiation just as someone physically present can.

17. Absence doesn’t cancel the result
Here’s the closing prose claim:

Parokṣasyāpi dīkṣitasya tathaiva jñānādyāvirbhāvaḥ.

For the person initiated in absence, knowledge and the other results may arise in the same way.

Now, let’s break that down.

Parokṣasya api — even of someone absent.

Dīkṣitasya — of the initiated person.

Tathaiva — in that very way.

Jñāna-ādi — knowledge and related attainments.

Āvirbhāva — manifestation, appearance, coming forth.

So:

Even for an absent initiated disciple, knowledge and the corresponding results can become manifest.

And that’s the chapter’s culminating prose claim.

The recipient’s absence doesn’t, within the doctrine, prevent the fruit of initiation.

Now notice the two cases.

For the deceased, the ritual is understood to bring about liberation or spiritual benefit according to the being’s condition.

For the living but absent disciple, the ritual may be understood to produce the manifestations of knowledge and other attainments.

But the underlying principle is the same.

The actual source of grace isn’t the guru’s ordinary individual power.

It’s:

Parameśvara.

The Supreme Lord.

And the guru’s role depends upon their establishment in that divine consciousness.

Now, at this point, the GRETIL Sanskrit transcription ends Chapter Sixteen.

But another textual witness preserves a beautiful concluding stanza.

And this time it’s absolutely worth quoting.

Because it captures the whole argument in two lines.

18. The genuine concluding verse — what’s so surprising about initiating someone far away?
ATTESTED CLOSING VERSE — CHAPTER SIXTEEN

परमेश्वरतावेशदार्ढ्यात्स्वातन्त्र्यभाग्गुरुः।परोक्षमभिसन्धाय दीक्षितेति किमद्भुतम्॥
TRANSLITERATION

Parameśvaratāveśadārḍhyāt svātantryabhāg guruḥ.

Parokṣam abhisandhāya dīkṣiteti kim adbhutam.

This stanza is present at the end of the Devanāgarī edition of Chapter 16. It is absent from the GRETIL transcription. The two earlier Great Net passages are present in both witnesses.

Right.

Now, let’s unpack the Sanskrit properly.

Parameśvaratā-āveśa-dārḍhyāt.

Because of firmness in immersion in supreme lordship.

Parameśvaratā — the state of the Supreme Lord.

Āveśa — immersion, entry or possession.

Dārḍhya — firmness, stability.

So this isn’t merely someone who’s had a pleasant mystical experience.

It’s someone established firmly in divine consciousness.

Next:

Svātantrya-bhāk guruḥ.

The guru who participates in or possesses absolute freedom.

Svātantrya — freedom, independence, sovereignty.

Bhāk — possessing, sharing in.

Guruḥ — the teacher.

Now that’s a magnificent expression.

Because the genuine guru doesn’t simply possess a collection of techniques.

Their qualification is grounded in the freedom of consciousness itself.

Then:

Parokṣam abhisandhāya.

Having directed their intention toward the absent person.

Parokṣa — absent, beyond direct perception.

Abhisandhāya — having aimed, directed, intended.

And finally:

Dīkṣita iti kim adbhutam.

That the absent person is initiated — what’s so astonishing about that?

Now, that’s the whole bloody chapter.

Abhinavagupta has spent the entire discussion explaining how a guru established in supreme consciousness is said to act beyond the ordinary limitations of physical proximity.

And the concluding verse essentially says:

Why would that surprise you?

Mate.

This is Śiva.

The unrestricted consciousness in which the very distinction between near and far is manifested.

And you’re worried about whether the disciple is standing on the correct side of the ritual room?

Now, again, the verse expresses the tradition’s theological claim; it doesn’t demonstrate that an actual person can be initiated without their knowledge or consent.

But as a philosophical conclusion, it’s perfectly consistent with the entire argument.

The one consciousness is not ultimately divided by the spatial separation between its manifestations.

And that’s why, within the tradition, remote initiation is considered possible.

End of Chapter Sixteen — Parokṣadīkṣā
Now, let’s consider what Abhinavagupta has accomplished.

We’ve travelled a remarkable distance since Chapter One.

We began with the nature of consciousness.

Prakāśa.

The self-luminous reality.

And its power of self-apprehension.

Vimarśa.

Then we investigated the supreme freedom:

Svātantrya.

And how that freedom manifests the entire universe.

The tattvas.

The cosmic pathways.

The worlds.

The gods.

The limited individuals.

Then we investigated the methods through which consciousness can recognise its own nature.

Śāmbhavopāya.

Śāktopāya.

Āṇavopāya.

And the condition beyond methods.

Anupāya.

Then the rituals.

The sacred bath.

The consecration of the body.

The worship of the Goddess.

The initiation of the disciple.

And the deathbed rites.

Now, in Chapter Sixteen, we encounter one final problem.

What if the disciple isn’t here?

And Abhinavagupta’s answer is that absence itself isn’t the ultimate obstacle.

Because the power that establishes the relationship between guru and disciple isn’t merely spatial proximity.

It’s the power of consciousness.

The same conscious freedom that manifests space itself.

And here’s what I find particularly interesting.

The chapter doesn’t merely say:

“Everything is one, therefore distance doesn’t matter.”

It gives a highly particular account.

The qualifications of the deceased.

The reasons certain preliminaries are omitted.

The conditions that establish divine presence.

The Great Net attributed to Śambhunātha.

The representation of the absent disciple.

The role of contemplative absorption.

The distinction between different postmortem states.

The modification required for a living absent person.

And the continuing influence of vāsanā.

All these details matter.

Because Abhinavagupta is not simply making a vague statement about universal interconnectedness.

He’s explaining how his tradition understands an exceptionally specialised ritual.

But at the heart of the whole discussion lies one sentence.

Parameśvara eva hi … anugṛhṇāti.

It is the Supreme Lord himself who bestows grace.

That’s the central principle.

The ritual may employ a maṇḍala.

A mantra.

A representation.

A visualised net.

An offering.

A teacher.

And a particular recipient.

But none of these is ultimately an independent source of liberation.

The supreme agency belongs to Śiva.

Now think about the extraordinary irony.

The limited individual spends their whole life thinking:

“I’m here.”

“The world is out there.”

“The guru is somewhere else.”

“Śiva is somewhere farther away still.”

And then Abhinavagupta explains that every one of those spatial distinctions is itself an appearance within consciousness.

And what does that mean for the spiritual teacher?

If the guru is genuinely established in the freedom of Śiva, the tradition holds that their liberating activity is not ultimately confined by ordinary distance.

Quite an extraordinary claim.

But one that arises naturally from the metaphysical premises we’ve been investigating.

And the final verse gives the whole thing a wonderfully cheeky ending.

Why should it be astonishing?

Why should the freedom of Śiva be limited by the ordinary separation between physical bodies?

Why should the supreme consciousness be unable to manifest its own grace through a guru toward someone apparently absent?

For Abhinavagupta, the answer is obvious.

The whole bloody universe is Śiva.

And distance is part of the universe.

Not something standing outside it and telling Śiva what he’s allowed to do.

Absolutely magnificent.
