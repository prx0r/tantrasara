# Tantrasāra — Chapter 3

Tantrasāra — Chapter 3
शाम्भवोपायप्रकाशनम् — Śāmbhavopāyaprakāśanam

The Divine Method: or, how Śiva invented the entire bloody universe, including the alphabet, and somehow it’s all happening inside you.

A complete, irreverent retelling following the original Sanskrit chapter in order. The dialogue and jokes are invented; the philosophical arguments are Abhinavagupta’s.

Right then.

Last chapter, I explained anupāya.

No method. No technique. No spiritual gymnastics.

Just immediate recognition of your true nature.

Lovely.

But here’s the problem.

Not everyone can do that.

You tell someone they’re the infinite, self-luminous consciousness of Śiva, and they go:

“Yeah, absolutely, makes sense.”

Then five minutes later they’re furious because someone’s eaten their lunch.

Clearly, something hasn’t quite clicked.

So now we’re going to discuss Śāmbhavopāya.

The method belonging to Śambhu. The divine approach.

And here’s the beautiful thing.

We’re still not bothering with elaborate rituals.

We’re not sitting there counting breaths or chanting ten thousand mantras.

We’re going to recognise the supreme freedom of consciousness itself.

Svātantrya-śakti.

And through that recognition, without having to construct some elaborate conceptual understanding, we can enter what I call:

Bhairava-samāveśa.

Immersion in Bhairava.

The supreme condition of divine consciousness.

Right. Here’s your first instruction.

1. The entire universe is a reflection
Everything you’re experiencing right now?

All of it.

Every object, every sound, every person, every thought, every planet, every galaxy, every bloody mosquito.

All of this appears as a reflection within the space of awareness.

Bodha-gagana.

Bodha — awareness.

Gagana — sky, or open space.

The sky of consciousness.

Now, don’t misunderstand me.

I’m not saying the world is imaginary in the sense that nothing happens.

I’m saying its manner of appearing is like a reflection.

Let’s establish what a reflection actually is.

Look into a mirror.

There’s your face.

Very handsome, I’m sure.

But is there a second face physically living inside the mirror?

Of course not.

That appearance depends upon the mirror.

It cannot manifest there independently.

Now consider something else.

Imagine your favourite food.

Really imagine it.

The smell.

The taste.

That first magnificent bite.

Your mouth starts watering, doesn’t it?

You can practically taste the thing.

Except there’s no food in your mouth.

Where the hell has that taste come from?

It’s a reflected sensation.

It appears, but the actual food isn’t present.

And notice something important.

If the real food had some medicinal effect, merely imagining its flavour wouldn’t produce that same effect.

The reflected taste is not identical to the original physical experience.

Now think of a smell.

You remember someone’s perfume.

For a moment you can almost smell it.

But the actual perfume isn’t there.

Or imagine an intimate encounter with someone you desire.

Your body might respond.

Or watch someone getting stabbed with a spear.

Horrible.

You might physically recoil.

Your own stomach contracts.

Yet nobody has stabbed you.

And what about an echo?

You shout into a valley.

“HELLO!”

And back comes:

“Hello… hello… hello…”

Is there some other bloke standing behind the mountain shouting back?

No.

It’s a dependent appearance.

Now, here’s the important bit.

These experiences aren’t simply nonexistent.

They can produce genuine responses.

The imagined pleasure can move the body.

The perceived pain can make you shudder.

So we’ve established something interesting.

An appearance can be real as an appearance without existing independently in the manner it appears to.

And that, my friend, is how you should understand the universe.

The entire cosmos manifests within the light of the supreme consciousness.

Parameśvara-prakāśa.

2. But what’s the universe a reflection OF?
Now someone’s going to interrupt.

There’s always one.

“Excuse me, Abhinavagupta. If the universe is a reflection, where’s the original?”

Ah.

Good question.

There doesn’t have to be an original object.

“WHAT?”

You heard me.

“Well, surely something has to cause the reflection?”

Ah, now you’ve changed the question.

You originally asked me about an original object.

Now you’re asking about a cause.

Those aren’t the same bloody thing.

The universe doesn’t need some separate, pre-existing universe sitting outside consciousness for consciousness to copy.

Its cause is the power of consciousness itself.

Śakti.

More precisely, the sovereign freedom of the supreme Lord.

Svātantrya.

Śiva doesn’t need to look outside himself, find a universe, and reproduce it.

There’s no outside.

He manifests the universe through his own power.

And because the universe is contained within his consciousness, it follows that Śiva is not merely the observer of the universe.

Śiva is the universe in its conscious reality.

Viśvātmaka.

Having the universe as his very nature.

But hang on.

If Śiva really is everything, wouldn’t he have to be aware of everything?

Exactly.

And that’s where things get seriously interesting.

3. Śiva isn’t just a cosmic mirror
Think about an ordinary mirror.

It reflects your face.

But does it know that it’s reflecting your face?

No.

It’s a mirror.

It’s not sitting there thinking:

“Bloody hell, look at this handsome individual I’ve got inside me.”

It has no awareness.

But Śiva?

Entirely different situation.

Because Śiva’s nature is consciousness, he cannot be unaware of his own nature.

If he were completely unaware of himself, he’d be insentient.

Jaḍa.

Inert.

Like a stone.

And we’re not talking about some unconscious cosmic substance.

We’re talking about the very principle of awareness itself.

So Śiva doesn’t merely illuminate the universe.

He recognises and apprehends the universe as his own manifestation.

This is vimarśa.

Self-awareness. Reflexive apprehension.

And more specifically, we’re talking about parāmarśa — consciousness’s immediate apprehension of itself and its powers.

Now don’t imagine Śiva having an internal monologue.

“Hello, I’m Śiva. Look at me, creating some trees. Aren’t I clever?”

Absolutely not.

This isn’t ordinary conceptual thought.

It’s an intrinsic self-awareness inseparable from consciousness itself.

And there’s something astonishing about this awareness.

It has within it a supreme resonance.

Para-nāda.

The primordial resonance of consciousness.

Not a sound produced by two objects colliding.

Not an audible vibration travelling through the air.

Something far more fundamental.

The inherent expressive power of awareness.

And this brings us to one of the most extraordinary teachings in the entire Trika tradition.

4. The universe is hiding in the Sanskrit alphabet
Right.

You’re going to love this.

Śiva possesses an entire array of powers through which the universe manifests and becomes ordered.

But we can begin with three fundamental powers.

And these are expressed through three Sanskrit sounds.

Anuttara

The unsurpassable absolute. That beyond which there is nothing higher.

Icchā

Will. The power of intention.

Unmeṣa

Unfolding. The first opening of manifestation.

Three sounds.

A. I. U.

And from this fundamental triad, the entire unfolding of divine powers can be understood.

Now, why these particular sounds?

Because Sanskrit phonemes aren’t being treated here merely as convenient noises humans make when ordering lunch.

I’m explaining a sacred correspondence between the articulation of sound and the articulation of reality itself.

Not conventional historical linguistics, mind you.

A tantric account of how consciousness expresses itself.

Now.

What happens when these three powers rest within their own nature?

Something wonderful.

We get three further expressions.

The absolute resting in itself manifests as ānanda.

Bliss.

Expressed as:

आ — ā.

Will resting in itself expresses īśana.

Sovereign mastery.

ई — ī.

And unfolding resting in itself expresses ūrmi.

A wave. The first stirring of action.

ऊ — ū.

So now we have six.

अ आ इ ई उ ऊ

a, ā, i, ī, u, ū.

And here’s something beautiful.

The first three have a solar character.

Sūrya.

The sun.

Why?

Because illumination, prakāśa, predominates.

The second three have a lunar character.

Soma.

The moon.

Because repose, delight, and reflective self-awareness predominate.

Sun and moon.

Illumination and blissful repose.

Not two separate realities.

Different expressions of one divine consciousness.

And we’re not even at physical creation yet.

Not a single tree.

Not a planet.

Not so much as a bloody atom.

We’re still talking about the intrinsic powers of consciousness.

5. Consciousness starts contemplating doing something
Now imagine will beginning to take on an object.

Not an actual external object yet.

Just something intended.

Something that may become manifest.

There’s a difference between pure will and willing something particular.

And this produces another distinction.

One aspect is illumination.

The other is repose or stability.

These are associated with the underlying sounds of:

र — r

and

ल — l.

Now, be careful.

We’re not yet talking about fully formed consonants in the ordinary sense.

The intended object hasn’t become externally manifest.

It’s still an implicit possibility within consciousness.

And because this possibility combines qualities associated with vowels and consonants, we get four unusual Sanskrit vowel forms.

ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ

ṛ, ṝ, ḷ, ḹ.

Yes, Sanskrit has vowel forms of r and l.

And yes, they’re rather peculiar.

But that’s precisely why they have a place in this mystical account.

They occupy an intermediate position.

Neither simply the pure original powers nor fully externalised articulations.

Now consciousness continues its unfolding.

And the original absolute, anuttara, together with its blissful aspect, flows into the other powers.

Sounds combine.

You know how in Sanskrit:

a + i becomes e?

Well, this isn’t merely a grammar lesson anymore.

We’re understanding the transformation symbolically as a movement of divine powers.

So we get:

ए — e

ओ — o

And with further conjunction:

ऐ — ai

औ — au

These four represent the developing power of action.

Kriyā-śakti.

And now we’ve got something approaching manifestation.

The divine will is no longer merely resting within itself.

Its power is unfolding toward expression.

But before the manifold effects emerge, there’s another crucial movement.

Everything gathers back into the absolute.

Into a point.

Bindu.

Expressed by the nasal resonance:

अं — aṃ.

The creative powers are gathered into unity.

Then comes something absolutely fundamental.

अः — aḥ.

Visarga.

Emission.

The outpouring of the absolute.

The movement by which the fullness of consciousness gives rise to manifestation.

And we’ve now arrived at the sixteen fundamental vowel expressions.

The sixteen sacred vowel forms

The sixteen bīja forms in Abhinavagupta’s phonemic cosmology.

Sixteen.

And these are the bīja forms.

Seeds.

Now, what do seeds need?

6. Seeds and wombs: the universe gets going
Wombs.

Yoni.

The sixteen vowels are the seeds.

And from them arise the consonantal forms, called wombs in this scheme.

Because when vowel and consonant come together, articulated syllables become possible.

Think of a Sanskrit syllable.

Take ka.

You’ve got the consonantal articulation k together with the vowel a.

And now we have the basis of expressive speech.

But the important thing is where all these consonants come from in the divine scheme.

Let’s begin with the five groups of five.

The five consonant families

ka-varga

Anuttara — the absolute

क ख ग घ ङ
ca-varga

Icchā — pure will

च छ ज झ ञ
ṭa-varga

Object-directed will

ट ठ ड ढ ण
ta-varga

Object-directed will

त थ द ध न
pa-varga

Unmeṣa — unfolding

प फ ब भ म
Twenty-five consonants.

Five families of five.

The fivefold organisation reflects the five divine powers.

Now, the remaining articulations also have their places.

The threefold forms of will give rise to:

ya, ra, la.

Unfolding gives:

va.

The threefold will also gives:

śa, ṣa, sa.

From emission comes:

ha.

And from combining consonantal wombs comes:

kṣa.

You see what’s happening?

The entire Sanskrit sound-system is being interpreted as an expression of the powers of consciousness.

Language is not merely something Śiva uses. In this metaphysical vision, language itself embodies Śiva’s creative articulation.

And all this brings us to a name.

Kuleśvara.

The Lord of the Kula.

The Lord of the family or totality of powers.

Who is that?

The supreme absolute itself.

Anuttara.

Now, Kuleśvara possesses his own power of emission.

Kaulikī-visarga-śakti.

And through this power, the differentiated expressions of consciousness unfold toward the externally manifest universe.

The phonemic powers become associated with the tattvas.

The principles constituting reality.

The universe is emerging.

Not because some bloke in the sky has started assembling matter.

But because the supreme freedom of consciousness is manifesting its own powers in increasingly differentiated ways.

7. Three forms of the great emission
Now let’s return to visarga.

Because it’s absolutely central.

The creative emission of consciousness can be understood in three ways.

First, āṇava-visarga.

Associated with the limited individual.

Its characteristic is:

Citta-viśrānti.

The mind coming to rest.

Second, śākta-visarga.

Associated with Śakti.

Its characteristic is:

Citta-saṃbodha.

The awakening or illumination of the mind.

Third, śāmbhava-visarga.

Associated with Śambhu himself.

Its characteristic is:

Citta-pralaya.

The dissolution of mind.

And don’t misunderstand that last expression.

We’re not talking about hitting yourself over the head until you lose consciousness.

That would be a rather unfortunate interpretation of liberation.

We’re talking about the dissolution of the mind’s limited, differentiated mode into its supreme conscious ground.

Three forms.

Repose.

Awakening.

Dissolution.

And all of them belong to the same fundamental power of divine emission.

Visarga is the Lord’s power of bringing forth the universe.

Now we’re ready for another astonishing piece of arithmetic.

8. How one becomes fifty, eighty-one, and twelve
Right.

You might be looking at this enormous collection of powers and thinking:

“Abhinava, mate, how many bloody things are there?”

Excellent question.

Depends how you’re looking.

When consciousness is apprehended without any division whatsoever, there is only one.

Bhagavān.

The Blessed Lord.

But when we distinguish the seed and womb aspects, we speak of two.

Śiva and Śakti.

The possessor of power and power itself.

Then, examining the eight consonantal groups together with their presiding lord, we speak of nine classes.

If we differentiate the individual phonemes, we get fifty.

And when we include finer subdivisions of their articulation, we can speak of eighty-one.

But here’s the important point.

We haven’t discovered eighty-one different supreme realities.

We’re describing one reality according to different modes of apprehension.

One.

Two.

Nine.

Fifty.

Eighty-one.

The divisions arise in how consciousness articulates and apprehends its powers.

And beneath all these classifications remain the six fundamental expressions we encountered earlier.

a, ā, i, ī, u, ū.

Through expansion and reabsorption, these are considered in a twelvefold arrangement.

And those twelve powers?

They’re associated with the blessed Kālīs.

Śrī Kālikāḥ.

The divine powers of articulation and awareness.

Not merely decorative goddesses.

The very dynamic expressions through which consciousness knows and manifests itself.

And now we’re approaching the final part of our chapter.

9. How divine consciousness becomes ordinary language
We’ve discussed the pure powers of consciousness.

But you’re probably wondering:

“Right, how does all this turn into me saying ‘pass the tea’?”

Well.

The powers don’t just suddenly jump from absolute consciousness into audible speech.

There are degrees of manifestation.

At the pure level, the phonemic expressions are powers of Śakti herself.

As differentiation begins, they assume forms associated with śuddhavidyā.

Pure knowledge.

Here, we’re dealing with mantras and the lords of mantras.

Vidyā.

Vidyeśvara.

But as the power of māyā develops, differentiation becomes more pronounced.

The unified powers begin to appear as distinct expressions.

They take on the forms of articulated language.

And here we encounter three important levels of speech.

Paśyantī.

The visionary level.

Speech before its full internal differentiation.

Madhyamā.

The intermediate level.

Inner, mentally articulated language.

Vaikharī.

The external, audible expression of speech.

The words coming out of your mouth.

So when you open your mouth and say something absolutely ridiculous, that audible expression belongs to the outermost level of a much deeper process of manifestation.

The divine power of expression has taken on a highly differentiated form.

And at the furthest reach of this development, the same powers are understood in relation to the externally manifest tattvas.

The principles of the experienced world.

Now.

Here’s something important about mantras.

10. Why a mantra isn’t just a magic word
You might think:

“Brilliant. So if I learn all these letters, I can just assemble some sounds and become Śiva.”

Not quite, mate.

Because the letters, in their ordinary differentiated condition, belong to the domain of māyā.

They’re like bodies.

Forms.

Structures.

A body without animating consciousness isn’t going to do much.

And similarly, a mantra isn’t fully understood merely by its visible letters or audible syllables.

It must be enlivened by the pure powers of awareness.

Śuddha-parāmarśa.

Those powers are like the life-breath entering a body.

When the mantra is awakened through them, it becomes:

Savīrya.

Possessing potency.

And such an enlivened mantra can bestow both:

Bhoga — experience or enjoyment.

And:

Mokṣa — liberation.

Do you see the distinction?

The efficacy of mantra, within this teaching, isn’t simply a property of mechanically repeating a sequence of sounds.

It’s intimately connected with the consciousness and powers those sounds express.

And now — after all this business with mirrors, vowels, consonants, divine emissions and cosmic goddesses — we’re finally ready to return to the original question.

How does any of this liberate you?

11. The actual Śāmbhava recognition
Right.

Forget the alphabet for a moment.

Not because it was pointless.

Quite the opposite.

Because now you’ve seen the structure.

The whole multiplicity of existence.

Every category.

Every principle.

Every living being.

Every world.

Every form of experience.

All of it has been understood as the differentiated manifestation of one supreme consciousness.

And what’s required now isn’t another complicated conceptual performance.

It’s an immediate, nonconceptual recognition.

I am the very ground in which all these reflections appear and come to rest.

Not this particular body alone.

Not merely my thoughts.

Not my memories.

Not the limited identity I’ve constructed around myself.

The very reality within which all these distinctions arise.

That’s Śāmbhava-samāveśa.

Immersion through the divine approach.

And when this recognition is complete, the person is liberated while living.

Jīvanmukta.

Not because the universe has disappeared.

Not because the body has evaporated.

Not because the person has become incapable of seeing distinctions.

But because those distinctions are no longer apprehended as realities separate from the supreme consciousness.

They are recognised as its own manifestations.

And just as I told you in the previous chapter, at this level there is no necessity for being constrained by mantra repetition or similar techniques.

Because the recognition itself is immediate.

The entire universe, with all its categories, beings, and worlds, rests in that one supreme consciousness.

Which brings us to my closing verse.

12. The final verse
ORIGINAL SANSKRIT

अन्तर्विभाति सकलं जगदात्मनीहयद्वद्विचित्ररचना मुकुरान्तराले।बोधः परं निजविमर्शरसानुवृत्त्याविश्वं परामृशति नो मुकुरस्तथा तु॥
MY SLANG RENDERING

Look.

The entire universe shines within the Self.

Just as a fantastically intricate world might appear within a mirror.

But here’s the difference.

The mirror doesn’t know a bloody thing about what it’s reflecting.

Consciousness does.

Consciousness apprehends the entire universe through the very delight of its own self-awareness.

Vimarśa-rasa.

The flavour of knowing itself.

And that is something no ordinary mirror can ever do.

So the universe isn’t simply an image appearing in a dead reflective surface.

It is an appearance within a living, self-aware, utterly free consciousness.

And the fundamental reality of that consciousness?

Śiva.

End of Chapter Three — Śāmbhavopāya.
