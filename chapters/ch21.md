# Tantrasāra — Chapter 21

एकविंशमाह्निकम् — Āgamaprāmāṇyaprakāśanam

The Authority of Scripture: or, why everyone thinks their holy book is the bloody truth, why Abhinavagupta thinks they’re not entirely wrong, and how the entire argument leads back to the consciousness that’s reading the book.


Right then.

Twenty-one chapters.

Twenty-one bloody chapters.

We’ve spent an absolutely outrageous amount of time investigating the nature of consciousness.

We’ve examined Śiva.

Śakti.

The thirty-six tattvas.

The six cosmic pathways.

The Sanskrit alphabet.

The nature of knowledge.

The descent of grace.

The various initiations.

The deathbed rituals.

The appropriate conduct of the initiated practitioner.

And in the last chapter, Abhinavagupta gave us the entire sacred calendar, an esoteric festival involving divine circles, a sacred thread with enough knots to represent the universe, and some very sensible instructions for explaining Sanskrit properly.

Lovely.

Now, throughout this entire undertaking, I’ve been referring to scripture.

Śāstra.

Āgama.

The teachings of Śiva.

The Trika revelation.

And I’ve repeatedly insisted that the highest scriptures disclose the supreme nature of consciousness.

But now somebody at the back of the room has put their hand up.

And they’ve got a rather awkward question.

“Excuse me, Abhinavagupta.”

Yes?

“You’ve spent twenty chapters telling us that the scriptures are authoritative.”

Correct.

“And that the Trika scriptures are the highest.”

Indeed.

“And you’ve used those scriptures to explain why they’re right.”

Well—

“BUT WHY SHOULD WE BELIEVE THE SCRIPTURES IN THE FIRST PLACE?”

Ah.

There it is.

The question we’ve been avoiding.

Because, let’s face it, there are an awful lot of religious books in the world.

The Vedas.

The Upaniṣads.

The Vaiṣṇava scriptures.

The Sāṅkhya teachings.

The Śaiva Siddhānta Āgamas.

The Trika Tantras.

And they don’t all say the same thing.

Some tell you there are multiple ultimately distinct realities.

Others tell you there is one.

Some claim liberation means the isolation of pure consciousness from material nature.

Others speak of becoming equal to Śiva.

And Abhinavagupta says the highest teaching culminates in the recognition of non-difference from Paramaśiva.

Well, they can’t all be giving identical descriptions of the ultimate reality.

Can they?

And if they contradict one another, what exactly gives any of them authority?

Now, that’s a serious philosophical problem.

And Abhinavagupta knows it.

So he’s going to give us an extraordinary argument.

Not merely:

“Believe my scripture because Śiva said so.”

And not:

“All religions are the same, mate. Doesn’t matter what you believe.”

Absolutely not.

He’s going to argue that scriptural authority is rooted in the very structure of conscious experience.

And then he’ll explain why different people become established in different teachings.

Why apparently contradictory revelations need not invalidate one another in every respect.

Why even perception and inference depend upon something more fundamental than formal proof.

And finally, why the highest authority is the one that leads beyond the limitations of ordinary human conviction.

This is one of the most philosophically important chapters in the whole work.

And it’s surprisingly short.

Seven paragraphs of prose.

Two concluding poetic teachings.

But bloody hell, they’re dense.

Let’s begin.

1. First, what does Āgamaprāmāṇya actually mean?
Āgamaprāmāṇya.

आगमप्रामाण्य

Let’s break that down.

Āgama.

आगम

A transmitted or received teaching.

A tradition or revelation that has come down through a recognised line of transmission.

In Indian philosophy, the term can designate an authoritative body of received teaching.

And:

Prāmāṇya.

प्रामाण्य

Validity.

Authority as a source of knowledge.

From:

Pramāṇa.

प्रमाण

A means of valid cognition.

Now, that last distinction is crucial.

We’re not merely asking whether a scripture is beautiful.

Or ancient.

Or particularly effective at making you feel spiritual.

We’re asking:

By what right can it claim to produce genuine knowledge?

Because that’s what prāmāṇya concerns.

The status of something as cognitively authoritative.

Now, Abhinavagupta begins:

एवं समस्तं नित्यं नैमित्तिकं कर्म निरूपितम्।

Evaṃ samastaṃ nityaṃ naimittikaṃ karma nirūpitam.

Thus all the regular and occasional ritual activities have been explained.

Remember Chapter Twenty?

Nitya.

Regular obligatory practice.

Naimittika.

Practice performed on particular occasions.

Well, we’ve done that.

Now:

अधुनास्यैवागमस्य प्रामाण्यमुच्यते।

Adhunāsyaivāgamasya prāmāṇyam ucyate.

Now the authority of this very revelation will be explained.

And that’s an important phrase.

Asya eva āgamasya.

Of this very Āgama.

Not a general philosophical investigation wholly detached from Trika.

Abhinavagupta is defending the revealed teaching upon which the preceding chapters depend.

And he begins from something we’ve already established.

The entire universe is consciousness.

Saṃvid-mātra-maya.

Let’s break that down.

Saṃvid — consciousness.

Mātra — only, nothing but.

Maya — consisting of.

So:

The universe consists of consciousness alone.

Now, if you haven’t accepted that premise, Abhinavagupta’s argument won’t automatically prove it for you.

But within the philosophical system he’s developed, that premise has already been established.

And now we’re going to see what follows.

Because if the universe is consciousness, what is scripture?

Something outside consciousness?

Some independent substance descending from an entirely foreign reality?

Of course not.

It’s an expression of consciousness itself.

And that takes us straight into:

Vimarśa.

2. Consciousness doesn’t merely shine — it knows that it shines
Right.

Remember our original distinction?

Prakāśa.

प्रकाश

Luminosity.

The shining or manifesting character of awareness.

And:

Vimarśa.

विमर्श

Reflexive awareness.

Consciousness’s capacity to apprehend its own nature.

Now imagine a hypothetical light that illuminates everything but has absolutely no awareness of itself.

It shines.

But it doesn’t know that it shines.

For Abhinavagupta, that wouldn’t adequately describe supreme consciousness.

Because awareness isn’t merely something that makes objects appear.

It’s also inherently self-apprehending.

Prakāśa and vimarśa.

The light.

And its living self-awareness.

Now, the Sanskrit says:

Saṃvidi ca vimarśātmikāyām.

Consciousness is of the nature of vimarśa.

And then comes something especially important.

Vimarśasya ca śabdanātmakatāyāṃ siddhāyām.

The verbal or expressive nature of vimarśa has been established.

Now, here’s the technical point.

Śabdana.

Expression through sound or verbal articulation.

And:

Ātmaka.

Having the nature of.

So Abhinavagupta is connecting reflexive awareness with the power of expression.

Remember Chapter Three?

The phonemes?

The vowels?

The divine speech-powers?

The way manifestation unfolds through the living self-apprehension of consciousness?

Well, all of that is returning here.

And it’s absolutely essential.

Because if consciousness’s capacity to know itself is intrinsically expressive, then articulated knowledge isn’t something completely foreign to consciousness.

Speech is not merely an arbitrary external invention placed on top of awareness.

Within Trika, the power of verbal manifestation is an expression of Śakti.

And scripture is a particularly important form of that expression.

Now, don’t misunderstand this as the claim that consciousness literally speaks English or Sanskrit sentences every moment.

Abhinavagupta’s doctrine of speech reaches deeper than audible words.

It concerns the preconceptual and conceptual powers through which awareness articulates and apprehends what appears.

And that’s why he’s using vimarśa and śabdana together.

Now comes the definition of scripture.

And it’s magnificent.

3. What actually is a scripture?
Here’s the central phrase:

यद् विमर्शनं तदेव शास्त्रम्।

Yad vimarśanaṃ tad eva śāstram.

That reflective apprehension is itself scripture.

But reflective apprehension of what?

Abhinavagupta specifies two things.

First:

Sakala-jagat-niṣṭha-vastu.

The reality present throughout the entire universe.

Second:

Karma-phala-sambandha-vaicitrya.

The variety of relationships between actions and their fruits.

Let’s unpack that monster.

Karma — action.

Phala — fruit, result.

Sambandha — relationship.

Vaicitrya — diversity, variety, manifold complexity.

So:

The manifold relationships between actions and their consequences.

Now, why is that important?

Because scripture doesn’t merely tell you abstract facts about the universe.

It also tells you what certain actions are supposed to accomplish.

Perform this rite.

Receive this initiation.

Contemplate this principle.

Undertake this discipline.

And the resulting condition will be such and such.

The scriptures describe reality and connect particular practices with particular outcomes.

And Abhinavagupta says that this whole articulation of reality and its relations is an expression of consciousness’s own power of self-apprehension.

Now consider how radical that is.

Scripture isn’t an alien message thrown into an otherwise unconscious universe.

It is consciousness expressing and apprehending the structures of its own manifestation.

And if the universe consists of consciousness, then the scripture itself is not separate from the reality it describes.

Which leads to the next statement.

Parameśvara-svabhāva-abhinna.

Not different from the essential nature of the Supreme Lord.

Now, that’s a bloody extraordinary claim.

Because it includes the entire body of scripture.

Samastaḥ śāstra-sandarbhaḥ.

The whole collection of scriptural teachings.

And Abhinavagupta says they’re fundamentally directed toward:

Eka-phala.

One fruit.

And:

Eka-adhikārin.

One ultimately intended recipient or qualified subject.

Now, be careful.

He’s not saying every scripture offers identical immediate results.

He’ll explicitly distinguish them shortly.

He’s saying that the whole scriptural manifestation belongs to one supreme conscious reality and, when understood in its ultimate unity, has a single highest fulfilment.

But then comes the obvious question.

“If everything is one revelation, why are there so many different bloody scriptures?”

Excellent.

And Abhinavagupta gives an answer.

Niyati.

4. Niyati — why people end up in different religious worlds
Remember the five kañcukas?

The limiting coverings.

One of them is:

Niyati.

नियति

Determination.

The limitation through which unrestricted freedom appears as particular, ordered relationships.

Now, within the supreme consciousness, no independent external force determines what Śiva can manifest.

But through Śiva’s own freedom, the experience of limited determination arises.

And Abhinavagupta says that this power explains why people become established in different portions of scripture.

The actual phrase is:

Parameśvara-niyati-śakti-mahimnā.

Through the greatness of the Supreme Lord’s power of determination.

And the result is:

Bhāge bhāge rūḍhir lokānām.

People become established in different portions.

Now, rūḍhi is a magnificent word.

It can mean established usage, settled convention, accepted understanding or something firmly rooted.

And in this chapter, it becomes absolutely central.

People don’t approach the entire corpus of teachings from a completely neutral position.

They become established in particular traditions.

Particular forms of understanding.

Particular inherited classifications.

Particular accounts of what liberation means.

Now imagine five philosophers sitting around a table.

One has spent his life studying the Vedas.

Another is a Sāṅkhya philosopher.

Another is a Vaiṣṇava.

Another follows Śaiva Siddhānta.

And the fifth follows Trika.

They’ve all got elaborate intellectual systems.

They’ve got scriptures.

Authorities.

Arguments.

Practices.

And rather strong opinions about one another.

Everyone is convinced.

Everyone thinks their understanding has a legitimate foundation.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Of course they do.

The manifestation of these different forms of established understanding belongs to the divine power of determination.

The plurality of doctrinal positions is not outside Śiva.

It’s part of the manifestation of Śiva.

Now, that doesn’t mean all the positions are equally adequate.

Abhinavagupta absolutely does not say that.

But it explains why differentiation is possible within an ultimately nondual reality.

And now he’s going to give us the actual hierarchy.

This part is especially important.

Because it tells us exactly how Trika understands the relationship between its own revelation and other Indian philosophical systems.

5. Abhinavagupta’s hierarchy of revelations — and why Trika comes last
Right.

We’ve established that all the scriptural traditions arise within consciousness.

And that different people become rooted in different systems through the power of niyati.

But what exactly are these systems?

Abhinavagupta now names them.

And he gives us a hierarchy.

Not a modern comparative-religion classification.

A specifically Trika Śaiva hierarchy of how completely different scriptures apprehend the supreme reality.

Let’s go through it.

The Vedas — reality understood through difference
First:

Veda-āgama-ādi-śāstra.

The Vedas and related teachings.

And the governing form of understanding is:

Māyocita-bheda-parāmarśa.

Now that’s a magnificent compound.

Māyā — the power through which differentiation and limitation become manifest.

Ucita — appropriate to, suited to.

Bheda — difference.

Parāmarśa — reflective apprehension.

So:

An apprehension of difference appropriate to the domain of Māyā.

Now, think about what that means.

Within the differentiated world, subjects and objects appear distinct.

Individuals perform actions.

Those actions produce consequences.

There are duties.

Prohibitions.

Gods.

Rituals.

Different states of existence.

And the scriptures describe and regulate those distinctions.

That’s a genuine domain of manifestation.

But it isn’t the ultimate nondual recognition Abhinavagupta is attempting to establish.

Now imagine a Vedic ritualist.

He knows the ceremonies.

The appropriate chants.

The duties.

The sacrificial arrangements.

The relation between action and result.

He’s got an entire system.

And Abhinavagupta isn’t simply saying that none of it exists or that the entire tradition is meaningless.

He’s saying its central apprehension, insofar as it is organised through difference, belongs to a particular level of consciousness’s manifestation.

It’s an expression of Śiva, but not necessarily the fullest recognition of Śiva.

Now the next group.

Sāṅkhya and Vaiṣṇava teachings — liberation understood through separation or a particular divine goal
Abhinavagupta names:

Sāṅkhya.

And:

Vaiṣṇava-śāstra.

And he introduces them through:

Mokṣābhimāna.

An identification or conception concerning liberation.

Now, that’s a rather sharp expression in context.

Because he’s discussing traditions that possess an idea of liberation, but which he considers less adequate than the highest Trika understanding.

Let’s take Sāṅkhya.

Remember:

Puruṣa.

Pure consciousness as understood in Sāṅkhya.

And:

Prakṛti.

Primordial nature.

The Sāṅkhya account distinguishes puruṣa from prakṛti and understands liberation as the isolation of the conscious principle from its entanglement with material nature.

Kaivalya.

Isolation.

Now, within Sāṅkhya’s own philosophical structure, that’s a profound claim.

But for Abhinavagupta, it doesn’t express the supreme recognition of one unrestricted consciousness whose freedom manifests the entire world.

Because the Sāṅkhya distinction between puruṣa and prakṛti remains fundamental.

And what does Trika say?

That nothing exists independently outside the freedom of Śiva.

So from Abhinavagupta’s perspective, Sāṅkhya’s liberation remains restricted by its account of ultimate difference.

And he similarly assigns Vaiṣṇava scriptures a place within his hierarchy, rather than granting their descriptions of the divine the same ultimate status as Trika’s.

Now, that’s a doctrinal judgment from within Trika.

A Sāṅkhya philosopher or Vaiṣṇava theologian would naturally offer their own account, and may reject Abhinavagupta’s classification.

We’re preserving his argument rather than pretending he’s delivering a neutral verdict accepted by everybody.

Next comes something closer to his own tradition.

Śaiva Siddhānta — recognising Śiva, but still distinguishing him
Now we arrive at:

Śaivasiddhānta.

शैवसिद्धान्त

And the relevant description is:

Vivikta-śiva-svabhāva-āmarśana-sāra.

Let’s unpack it.

Vivikta — separated, distinguished, set apart.

Śiva-svabhāva — the essential nature of Śiva.

Āmarśana — apprehension or contemplation.

Sāra — essence.

So:

A teaching essentially concerned with apprehending the nature of Śiva as distinguished or set apart.

Now this is very interesting.

Because we’re no longer merely discussing a system organised around worldly religious differences.

The teaching is directly concerned with Śiva.

And it possesses a sophisticated account of liberation and divine reality.

But within the Śaiva Siddhānta framework, important distinctions between the Lord, the bound soul and its bonds remain significant.

Pati.

Paśu.

Pāśa.

The Lord.

The bound individual.

The bonds.

And Abhinavagupta considers the fully nondual recognition to lie beyond those distinctions.

So the teaching may be genuinely Śaiva.

And yet not possess the highest understanding according to Trika.

That’s the hierarchy.

Now we move one level further.

The Mataṅga and related scriptures — Śiva as the reality of everything
Next:

Mataṅga-ādi-śāstra.

The scriptures beginning with the Mataṅga.

And Abhinavagupta describes them as:

Sarvamaya-parameśvaratā-āmarśana-sāra.

Now that’s beautiful.

Sarva — all.

Maya — consisting of, having the nature of.

Parameśvaratā — supreme lordship.

Āmarśana — apprehension.

Sāra — essence.

So:

Teachings essentially concerned with apprehending the Supreme Lord as consisting of everything.

Now we’re getting closer.

Because the divine reality isn’t merely being conceived as separate from the world.

The Lord is apprehended as the reality pervading the whole manifested universe.

Sarvamaya.

Consisting of all.

And that’s an enormous advance within Abhinavagupta’s hierarchy.

Now, imagine the philosopher from the previous system saying:

“Śiva is supreme, but he remains distinguished from the bound individual.”

And now someone else says:

“Hang on. What if the entire universe is the expression of the Supreme Lord?”

Ah.

Now we’re getting somewhere.

But Abhinavagupta isn’t finished.

Because the ultimate Trika recognition goes beyond merely saying:

“Everything belongs to God.”

It concerns the essential nature of the unrestricted consciousness through which everything manifests.

And apparently the people who actually establish themselves in that recognition are exceptionally rare.

Trika — the rarest of the rare
Here’s the Sanskrit:

Virala-viralāḥ.

विरलविरलाः

The rare among the rare.

Now I absolutely love that.

Not merely rare.

Rare-rare.

Abhinavagupta apparently looked at the spiritual population of the world and decided that one virala wasn’t sufficient.

You’ve got to double it.

And what are these rare individuals established in?

Śrī-Trika-śāstra-krama.

The venerable succession of Trika scripture.

Now, how does he describe the reality disclosed by that teaching?

Listen carefully.

Samasta-avaccheda-vandhya.

Free from every limitation.

Samasta — all.

Avaccheda — delimitation, restriction.

Vandhya — barren of, devoid of.

Then:

Svātantrya.

Absolute freedom.

Ānanda.

Bliss.

Paramārtha.

Ultimate reality.

Saṃvinmaya.

Consisting of consciousness.

And:

Parameśvara-svarūpa-āmarśana.

The apprehension of the Supreme Lord’s essential nature.

Put it together.

The Trika scriptures disclose the essential nature of Parameśvara as unrestricted consciousness, absolute freedom and bliss, devoid of all limitation.

Now, that’s the culmination.

Not merely a God who rules the universe.

Not merely a pure conscious subject isolated from material nature.

Not merely a supreme Lord distinct from the individual.

Not even merely the assertion that everything is divine.

But:

The recognition that the essential reality of the individual and the universe is the unrestricted, self-aware freedom of Paramaśiva.

That’s why Abhinavagupta considers Trika supreme.

And here’s an important distinction.

This is not an argument that being a member of the Trika tradition automatically makes someone spiritually superior to everybody else.

He has spent several chapters warning about the difference between genuine knowledge and mere religious status.

The hierarchy here concerns the completeness of the doctrine’s account of reality, as he understands it.

Now there’s one final detail.

You don’t necessarily have to pass through every tradition in order.

Abhinavagupta explicitly allows two ways of moving beyond previous teachings.

Pūrva-pūrva-tyāga-krama.

Abandoning earlier systems successively.

Or:

Laṅghana.

Leaping across them.

Now isn’t that magnificent?

You can progress through the hierarchy.

Or leap over intermediate stages.

Remember Chapter Eleven?

The different degrees of śaktipāta?

Some people awaken immediately.

Others require gradual development.

Some move through several initiatory systems.

Others pass directly to the higher teaching.

The same principle is operating here.

Now, Abhinavagupta concludes this section by reaffirming:

Eka-phala-siddhiḥ ekasmād eva āgamāt.

The attainment of the single fruit through the one revelation.

And that’s how he understands the different scriptures within one divine manifesting order.

But now comes an objection.

And it’s an excellent one.

Because our philosopher from the back of the room has been listening very carefully.

And he’s noticed a rather enormous problem.

6. If all scriptures come from Śiva, what happens when they contradict each other?
“Excuse me, Abhinava.”

Oh God.

Here we go.

“You’ve just told us that every scripture belongs to the manifestation of one supreme consciousness.”

Correct.

“And that every religious tradition is, in some sense, an expression of divine power.”

Indeed.

“But you’ve also told us that they give different accounts of reality.”

Yes.

“And some of them flatly contradict one another.”

Well, yes.

“So HOW CAN THEY ALL BE AUTHORITATIVE?”

Excellent question.

Because ordinarily, if two people make incompatible claims about the same thing under the same conditions, they can’t both be correct.

Suppose one scripture tells you:

“Do this.”

And another says:

“Never do this.”

Well, that’s quite a problem, isn’t it?

Imagine consulting a religious manual.

“Perform the offering.”

Wonderful.

Then opening another.

“Under no circumstances perform that offering.”

Right.

Fantastic.

Very helpful.

Which bloody instruction are we supposed to follow?

Now, Abhinavagupta gives a rather sophisticated answer.

First, he says that even a dualistic philosopher can accept the authority of multiple scriptures if they are understood as arising from one divine source.

He then names grounds of credibility such as:

Ekadeśa-saṃvāda.

Agreement in some particular domain.

Avigītatā.

The absence of relevant contradiction or defeat.

And a further technical condition involving a mode of reference not confined to the immediately presented particular — anidaṃtā-pravṛtti.

The last expression is compressed and admits interpretation; we shouldn’t pretend it is as transparent as the first two.

But the decisive point is what follows:

Paraspara-bādho viṣaya-bhedāt akiṃcitkaraḥ.

Mutual contradiction is ineffective where the subject matter differs.

Now, that’s extremely important.

Paraspara — mutual.

Bādha — contradiction, cancellation, defeat.

Viṣaya — subject matter, domain, object.

Bheda — difference.

Akiṃcitkara — accomplishing nothing, having no effect.

So:

An apparent contradiction does not necessarily invalidate two statements if they’re concerned with different domains.

Now, consider a simple modern example.

Suppose someone says:

“Water boils at 100°C.”

And another says:

“Water boils below 100°C.”

Contradiction?

Well, not necessarily.

Because the relevant atmospheric pressure may differ.

The statements need the proper qualifications.

Similarly, Abhinavagupta argues that religious instructions must be understood according to their scope and the conditions of the intended practitioner.

Now, this doesn’t mean every contradiction can be dissolved through an appeal to context.

If two scriptures make genuinely incompatible assertions about exactly the same reality under identical conditions, there remains a philosophical dispute.

But Abhinavagupta is drawing attention to the fact that not every apparent conflict is a genuine conflict concerning the same subject.

And then he introduces a rather severe example.

7. The example of killing a Brahmin — when scriptural rules seem to clash
Now the Sanskrit says:

Brahma-hanana-tan-niṣedha-vat.

Like the killing of a Brahmin and its prohibition.

Now, that’s an exceptionally stark example.

Some scriptural prescriptions or discussions involving this act can appear to stand in tension with prohibitions against it.

And Abhinavagupta invokes the difference in:

Saṃskāra.

Consecration, qualification or formative condition.

And:

Adhikāra.

Eligibility or jurisdiction.

Now, remember how important adhikāra has been throughout the initiatory chapters.

Not everyone can receive every mantra.

Not everyone can perform every ritual.

Not every initiated disciple is qualified to become a guru.

And different instructions apply to different religious statuses.

So Abhinavagupta says that the scope of a rule depends upon the relevant qualification.

The person authorised for one act isn’t necessarily authorised for another.

And the same action may be treated differently within distinct ritual contexts.

Now, he gives an analogy:

Āśrama-bheda.

The difference between religious life-stages.

Remember the traditional system?

Brahmacarya.

Student life.

Gṛhastha.

Householder life.

Vānaprastha.

Forest-dweller or retired stage.

Saṃnyāsa.

Renunciation.

Different forms of life.

Different obligations.

A practice appropriate to one may be inappropriate to another.

That doesn’t necessarily mean the entire religious system has contradicted itself.

It means the instructions have different intended recipients.

Now, that’s the logic Abhinavagupta is applying to scriptural authority.

But we should be precise about the disturbing example.

This is a hermeneutical illustration of conflicting religious injunctions, not a modern justification for killing someone. The relevant Sanskrit doesn’t give us grounds to declare violence permissible simply by invoking a special initiation.

The philosophical point is about interpreting a text according to its specified subject and qualifications.

And now Abhinavagupta adds another criterion.

One that reveals how strongly his system is oriented toward liberation.

The excellence of a scripture is determined by the excellence of its fruit.

8. Which scripture is highest? Look at what it claims to accomplish
Here’s the Sanskrit:

Phalotkarṣāc ca utkarṣaḥ.

Its excellence comes through the excellence of its result.

Let’s unpack it.

Phala — fruit, result.

Utkarṣa — superiority, excellence, elevation.

So:

A higher result establishes a higher rank within the hierarchy.

And Abhinavagupta gives a familiar example.

The Upaniṣadic portion of the Vedas.

Upaniṣad-bhāga.

Within the broader Vedic corpus, the Upaniṣads are concerned with profound questions of ultimate reality, consciousness and liberation.

And Abhinavagupta invokes them as an example of an elevated fruit within an established scriptural body.

Now, imagine two teachings.

The first promises a particular heavenly enjoyment.

A pleasant future birth.

Perhaps prosperity.

The second promises release from the conditions that sustain restricted existence.

Which result is more comprehensive?

Within Abhinavagupta’s scheme, the second.

Now imagine another teaching that promises liberation but still preserves some ultimate separation between the individual and supreme consciousness.

And then Trika claims the recognition of complete non-difference.

Again, Abhinavagupta ranks the latter more highly.

It’s not the age of the scripture or the grandeur of its ceremonial language that ultimately determines its rank. It’s the kind of liberation it discloses.

Now, this still depends upon accepting the Trika conception of the supreme fruit.

A philosopher from another school may disagree about what constitutes the highest result.

And that’s precisely why the dispute cannot be resolved by merely repeating:

“Our scripture is highest because our scripture says so.”

But Abhinavagupta’s criterion is now explicit.

The more complete the disclosed freedom, the higher the teaching within his system.

And here’s another fascinating point.

He says that even if different texts have different apparent authors, one can still understand them as arising through the knowledge of those possessing complete insight.

Sarva-sarvajña-kṛtatva.

The attribution to the all-knowing or fully knowing.

Now, this isn’t an independently established historical assertion that every religious book was written by an omniscient person.

It’s a theological argument concerning the source of authoritative revelation.

Abhinavagupta is refusing to make ordinary differences in textual authorship the final determinant of spiritual authority.

The truth and scope of the teaching matter more than the merely external identity of its human transmitter.

And then he goes further.

Because apparently the authority of scripture isn’t just a problem for religious people.

It’s a problem for anybody who claims to know anything whatsoever.

Including the logicians.

And Abhinavagupta has something rather devastating to say about their foundations.

9. The goldsmith, the silver, and why you trust people without realising it
Right.

Now comes the really fascinating argument.

Because Abhinavagupta introduces a word that will dominate the rest of the chapter.

Prasiddhi.

प्रसिद्धि

And I want you to pay particular attention to this.

Because it connects almost everything we’ve discussed about knowledge, recognition and the authority of tradition.

Let’s break the word down.

Pra — forth, completely, prominently.

Siddhi — accomplishment, establishment or something having become established, from the verbal root √sidh.

So:

Prasiddhi.

That which is well established.

Accepted.

Recognised.

Familiar through established understanding.

Not simply a rumour.

And not necessarily a revealed scripture in the narrow sense.

It can also refer to something that has become established as trustworthy within ordinary experience.

Now, Abhinavagupta makes a remarkable claim.

Even the authority of perception and inference depends, at some level, upon established understanding.

Here’s the Sanskrit:

अन्वयव्यतिरेकाध्यक्षादीनां तत्प्रामाण्यस्य तन्मूलत्वात्

Let’s unpack it.

Anvaya — positive concomitance.

Vyatireka — negative concomitance.

Adhyakṣa — direct perception.

Prāmāṇya — validity as a source of knowledge.

Tan-mūlatva — having that as its root.

So Abhinavagupta is arguing that the recognised validity of perception and inferential relations is rooted in prasiddhi.

Now, that’s a remarkably bold epistemological claim.

Because consider the ordinary philosopher.

They say:

“I don’t need scripture.”

“Excellent.”

“I rely on perception.”

“Good.”

“And inference.”

“Very good.”

“And I accept only those things that can be established through proper reasoning.”

Wonderful.

But Abhinavagupta asks:

How did those means of knowing become established for you as reliable in the first place?

And he gives an example.

A goldsmith.

Sauvarṇika.

A person possessing specialist knowledge of precious metals.

Now imagine you bring a piece of metal to an expert.

“Excuse me, mate.”

“Yes?”

“Is this genuine silver?”

The goldsmith examines it.

Perhaps through the relevant characteristics and skills of the craft.

And announces:

“Yes. Genuine silver.”

Now, what happens?

You accept that judgment.

But why?

Because the goldsmith possesses recognised expertise.

The knowledge has become established through a practice and community of understanding.

And Abhinavagupta invokes a phrase:

Satyaṃ rajataṃ paśyāmi.

“I see genuine silver.”

Now, the important point isn’t that you must blindly believe every goldsmith.

Some goldsmiths might be incompetent.

Some might be dishonest.

And if you possess the necessary knowledge yourself, you can test their judgment.

But Abhinavagupta is drawing attention to something more fundamental.

Our ordinary world of knowledge already depends upon established forms of trust and recognition.

You don’t begin every encounter with an entirely fresh proof of every principle.

You’ve learned what silver is.

You’ve learned how people identify it.

You’ve learned which experts are worth consulting.

You’ve inherited a network of recognitions.

And within that network, you act.

Now, here’s the subtlety.

Abhinavagupta isn’t simply saying:

“Trust tradition because tradition is always right.”

That would be a terrible argument.

He’s questioning the assumption that rational investigation could ever begin from an absolutely empty standpoint, without relying upon any previously established understanding.

And that brings us to his next example.

One of the most brilliantly ordinary examples in the chapter.

A hungry child.

10. The hungry child — apparently the foundations of knowledge begin with lunch
Right.

Imagine a small child.

Hungry.

Perhaps crying.

Perhaps pointing at some food.

And then, when the food becomes available, eating it.

Quite straightforward.

But Abhinavagupta asks:

Did the child first construct a formal inference proving that eating removes hunger?

Of course not.

Imagine the baby.

“Right, before accepting this banana, I must establish the necessary concomitance.”

“When food is present, the cessation of hunger is observed.”

“When food is absent, that result is not observed.”

“Therefore, by anvaya and vyatireka, I infer that eating this banana is appropriate.”

Absolutely bloody ridiculous.

The child eats.

And Abhinavagupta’s point is that the child’s activity already rests upon an established practical orientation.

A familiarity.

A recognition of how things work.

Prasiddhi.

The passage explicitly says:

बुभुक्षितो भुङ्क्त इति बालस्य प्रसिद्धित एव तत्र तत्र प्रवृत्तिः।

Bubhukṣito bhuṅkta iti bālasya prasiddhita eva tatra tatra pravṛttiḥ.

The hungry person eats; the child’s various activities proceed through established familiarity.

And then:

Na anvaya-vyatirekābhyām.

Not through the formal operation of positive and negative concomitance.

Why?

Because the child hasn’t yet developed that kind of inferential reasoning.

Now, the child may later grow up.

Become a philosopher.

Study Nyāya.

Learn formal logic.

Understand the relationship between evidence and conclusion.

Wonderful.

And Abhinavagupta doesn’t say inference is therefore useless.

But he insists that it doesn’t simply appear as a completely independent foundation outside the field of established understanding.

Even when reasoning develops later, it presupposes a world in which relevant concepts and practices have already become meaningful.

Before you could formally prove anything, you were already living within a world you understood well enough to act in.

Now, that’s an astonishing point.

And it’s easy to miss how radical it is.

Because the supposed alternative to scriptural authority was:

“I’ll simply trust direct perception and reasoning.”

And Abhinavagupta has responded:

“Very good. But even your confidence in what counts as valid perception and sound reasoning operates within an already established field of understanding.”

That doesn’t make reasoning invalid.

It means its authority isn’t being established from an absolute vacuum.

Now, there’s an important limitation to the argument.

Showing that human cognition depends upon prior understanding does not, by itself, prove that every supernatural claim in every religious scripture is true.

That would require a further argument.

Abhinavagupta’s purpose is to defend the possibility and necessity of authoritative transmission, and then distinguish different transmissions according to their aims and results.

And that brings us to the next distinction.

Because some forms of prasiddhi concern things you can directly observe.

Others concern things you can’t.

And apparently the latter include several different kinds of liberation.

11. Ordinary recognition and extraordinary recognition
Now, Abhinavagupta distinguishes established understandings by their fruits.

First:

Dṛṣṭa-phala.

दृष्टफल

Producing a visible or directly experienced result.

Dṛṣṭa — seen, observed.

Phala — fruit.

For example:

A hungry person eats.

The relevant result occurs within ordinary experience.

You can observe it.

It’s not especially mysterious.

But then there are teachings associated with:

Adṛṣṭa-phala.

अदृष्टफल

Results that aren’t directly visible or ordinarily observable.

Now we’re entering the domain of religious and metaphysical teachings.

And Abhinavagupta gives several examples.

Vaidehya.

A bodiless condition.

Prakṛti-laya.

Dissolution into primordial nature.

Puruṣa-kaivalya.

The isolation or absolute separateness of the conscious principle as understood in Sāṅkhya.

Now, these are very different results from ordinary hunger being satisfied.

They belong to competing philosophical accounts of spiritual attainment.

And Abhinavagupta isn’t pretending that the existence of these states is directly demonstrated simply because a child eats food when hungry.

He’s pointing to different kinds of established teaching.

Some deal with ordinary observable results.

Others concern results beyond ordinary perception.

And then he gives higher Śaiva outcomes.

Śiva-samānatva.

Equality with Śiva.

And finally:

Aikya-paryavasāyinī.

That which culminates in unity.

Now, let’s break down that beautiful compound.

Aikya — unity, oneness.

From eka — one.

Paryavasāyinī — culminating in, ending in, having as its final result.

So:

A teaching that culminates in oneness.

And that’s the crucial Trika distinction.

Some systems promise particular experiences.

Others promise a state of separateness or isolation.

Others promise equality with the supreme Lord.

But the highest culminates in complete recognition of non-difference.

Now imagine our group of philosophers again.

The Sāṅkhya philosopher says:

“Liberation is the isolation of puruṣa from prakṛti.”

The Śaiva Siddhāntin says:

“The bound soul attains liberation through the grace of Śiva, while the relevant distinctions remain meaningful.”

And Abhinavagupta says:

“The highest liberation is the recognition that the essential nature of consciousness was never ultimately different from Paramaśiva.”

They have different understandings of the goal.

And those differences structure their practices.

Their scriptures.

Their philosophical arguments.

And their expectations.

Now, here’s something subtle.

Abhinavagupta says that even within these general categories, there are numerous varieties.

Pratyekam anekavidhā.

Each is of many kinds.

So we’re not looking at a simple chart with exactly one practice and one outcome for every school.

The religious world contains a vast diversity of authoritative traditions and claimed results.

And their followers become established in those traditions according to their particular orientation.

Which returns us to:

Prasiddhi.

But now it has a much deeper meaning.

Because a person’s religious conviction isn’t merely something they recite with their mouth.

It comes to rest in the Heart.

12. The Heart chooses the teaching that becomes real to it
Now comes one of the most beautiful phrases in the chapter.

Hṛdaya-paryavasāyinī.

हृदयपर्यवसायिनी

Let’s unpack it.

Hṛdaya — Heart.

Paryavasāyinī — ending in, coming to rest in, culminating in.

So:

That which comes to rest in the Heart.

And Abhinavagupta says:

Yo yādṛśo bhaviṣyan sa tādṛśīm eva prasiddhiṃ … abhimanyate.

The person who is going to become established in a particular condition takes up the corresponding established conviction.

Now, here’s the philosophical point.

Different people don’t merely encounter religions as collections of neutral sentences and then select one through an entirely detached calculation.

A teaching becomes authoritative to a person when it becomes firmly established within their own field of understanding.

It reaches the Heart.

Hṛdaya.

Not necessarily because it produces a dramatic emotion.

Not simply because it makes them feel pleasant.

But because it becomes a settled conviction governing how they apprehend reality.

And now remember our earlier chapters.

Bhāvanā.

Contemplative cultivation.

Vāsanā.

Established tendencies.

Pratipatti.

Recognition or ascertainment.

Niścaya.

Certainty.

These are not merely decorations upon an otherwise fixed human experience.

They shape the manner in which reality becomes intelligible to the practitioner.

And Abhinavagupta is connecting that process with the authority of religious tradition.

Now imagine someone who spends years studying a particular philosophy.

Perhaps at first it’s simply interesting.

They understand some concepts.

Memorise a few Sanskrit words.

Perhaps admire the elegance of its arguments.

But they haven’t actually become convinced.

Then one day a particular distinction becomes clear.

Something clicks.

And the understanding is no longer merely an external proposition.

It has become their own settled insight.

That illustrates, in a limited way, what it means for a teaching to become established in the Heart.

Now, we shouldn’t confuse psychological certainty with objective correctness.

People can become absolutely convinced of things that are false.

That’s precisely why the quality of the teaching and its reasons still matter.

But Abhinavagupta is giving an account of why traditions become effective for particular practitioners.

The teaching becomes a lived orientation.

And within his theological framework, the diversity of those orientations belongs to the manifestation of Śiva’s power.

Now, notice where the argument has brought us.

We began by asking:

Why should any scripture be authoritative?

Then Abhinavagupta argued that all cognition operates within consciousness.

That the expressive power of consciousness makes scriptural articulation possible.

That different traditions arise within the divine manifestation.

That apparent contradictions require proper attention to their different objects and recipients.

That even ordinary reasoning depends upon an already established field of understanding.

And that different convictions become rooted in the Hearts of different individuals.

But now he asks something rather devastating.

If you’re prepared to accept any established source of knowledge at all, why categorically exclude Āgama?

13. The person who rejects every authority while secretly relying on authority
Right.

Here’s the final paragraph of prose.

And Abhinavagupta becomes rather cheeky.

He invokes:

Rikta-jantu.

रिक्तजन्तु

Literally, an empty creature.

Not exactly the most flattering description of one’s philosophical opponents.

And he says that elaborate verbal argument is pointless for such a person.

Atiriktā vāco-yuktiḥ.

Excessive verbal reasoning.

Now, the point isn’t merely:

“Anyone who disagrees with me is an idiot.”

His argument is that someone who relies upon established understanding in ordinary cognition but categorically denies the possibility of authoritative transmission has a problem.

Because they are already relying upon prasiddhi.

And then comes the crucial sentence.

तासां काञ्चन प्रसिद्धिं प्रमाणीकुर्वताभ्युपगन्तव्यमेवागमप्रामाण्यम्।

Tāsāṃ kāṃcana prasiddhiṃ pramāṇīkurvatābhyupagantavyam evāgamaprāmāṇyam.

Now let’s unpack it.

Tāsām — among those established understandings.

Kāṃcana — some particular one.

Prasiddhim — established recognition or convention.

Pramāṇīkurvatā — by one who treats it as authoritative.

Abhyupagantavyam — must be admitted or accepted.

Āgama-prāmāṇyam — the authority of Āgama.

So Abhinavagupta argues:

Someone who accepts any established understanding as authoritative must also admit the possibility of authority through received teaching.

Now, notice the distinction.

That does not mean the person must accept every scripture’s every assertion as true.

It means testimony or authoritative transmission cannot simply be excluded in principle while one continues to rely upon established understanding.

And here’s where Abhinavagupta makes his final choice.

Because once you’ve admitted that a received teaching can be authoritative, which one should you follow?

Well, the text gives a remarkably direct answer.

The one with the highest fruit.

Svātantrya.

Anuttara.

What the bloody hell is all this?

But then you start investigating.

You relate the terms to experience.

You examine the arguments.

You work through the distinctions.

Perhaps you discover that something you previously assumed about consciousness isn’t quite right.

And a new understanding begins to arise.

Eventually the words may cease to feel like a collection of foreign propositions.

The meaning becomes established.

Pratipatti.

Recognition.

Niścaya.

Certainty.

Prasiddhi.

Settled understanding.

Now, this doesn’t mean you’ve necessarily attained the supreme state simply because the philosophy has become intellectually persuasive.

Abhinavagupta has already distinguished conceptual understanding from the deeper overcoming of contracted individuality.

But the movement from received words toward genuine recognition is central to his account of the scriptural path.

And here’s the most remarkable thing.

The scripture is not the final object you’re supposed to acquire.

The scripture is a means of disclosing the reality of consciousness.

And the reality of consciousness isn’t something the book has to manufacture.

It is the awareness through which the book is being read.

Now, remember Chapter One?

The opening declaration:

Jñānaṃ mokṣakāraṇam.

Knowledge is the cause of liberation.

And the emphasis upon the proper understanding of reality.

Well, twenty chapters later, Abhinavagupta has returned to the same theme.

Only now we’ve examined the full process through which the teaching is transmitted, practised, contemplated and recognised.

The scripture isn’t merely a source of information about liberation.

Within Trika, it is an expression of the divine power through which recognition may arise.

And in its highest form, that recognition is:

Parameśvara-svarūpa-āmarśana.

The apprehension of the essential nature of the Supreme Lord.

Now, consider the final irony.

You spent twenty-one chapters studying a scripture.

Learning its Sanskrit.

Examining its arguments.

Understanding its cosmology.

Investigating its rituals.

And finally asking:

“Abhinavagupta, mate, why should I trust this bloody book?”

And Abhinavagupta responds by directing your attention to the very consciousness through which the question is being asked.

What is the ultimate nature of the knower?

What makes knowing possible?

What is the conscious freedom through which every object and every act of understanding appears?

And his answer?

Cit.

Vimarśa.

Svātantrya.

Anuttara.

Paramaśiva.

Not a reality standing somewhere outside the act of knowing.

But the essential consciousness through which all knowing becomes possible.

And here’s the final joke.

You’re trying to establish whether a scripture can reveal the nature of consciousness.

And the whole time, consciousness is right there, reading the scripture, investigating the argument and attempting to recognise its own nature.

Śiva is reading a book about Śiva and asking Śiva whether Śiva is telling the truth.

The absolute bloody cheek.

End of Chapter Twenty-One — Āgamaprāmāṇyaprakāśanam.
