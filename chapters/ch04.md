# Tantrasāra — Chapter 4

शाक्तोपायप्रकाशनम् — Śāktopāyaprakāśanam

The Method of Śakti: or, how to think your way out of the absolute bloody mess that thinking got you into.


Right then.

Last chapter was Śāmbhavopāya.

Very elegant.

Very immediate.

You recognise the entire universe as the expression of your own divine consciousness, and that’s that.

But there’s a slight issue.

Some of you are still thinking.

Quite a lot, actually.

You’ve been thinking your entire life.

You’ve constructed this enormous conceptual prison, furnished it with beliefs, decorated it with anxieties, and now you’re sitting in the middle of it going:

“Well, I suppose this is who I am.”

Absolutely tragic.

But fear not.

Because Śakti has got another trick up her sleeve.

And this one’s rather clever.

We’re going to use your own thinking to destroy the thinking that’s keeping you bound.

1. The problem is what you’ve convinced yourself you are
Let’s begin.

You think you’re bound.

Baddham ātmānam abhimanyante.

You regard yourself as a captive, a limited being.

And why?

Because of the power of vikalpa.

Conceptual construction.

You’re constantly telling yourself what you are.

“I’m this body.”

“I’m this personality.”

“I’m this miserable little individual.”

“I’m bound by my past.”

“I’m separate from everything else.”

“I’m impure.”

“I’m powerless.”

And you’ve repeated these ideas so often that you’ve started taking them for reality itself.

Now, here’s the remarkable thing.

The very power that constructs these limiting convictions can also construct their opposites.

This is pratidvandvi-vikalpa.

An opposing conceptual understanding.

And when it arises with sufficient force, it can shatter the conceptual understanding responsible for perpetuating saṃsāra.

Saṃsāra-hetuṃ vikalpaṃ dalayati.

It crushes the thought that causes bondage.

Imagine you’ve spent your entire life convinced that there’s a gigantic tiger waiting outside your bedroom.

Absolutely terrifying.

Every morning you wake up, look at the door, and think:

“Well, can’t go outside. Tiger.”

Then someone who actually understands the situation comes along and explains that there’s no tiger.

You investigate.

You reason.

You see what you’ve misunderstood.

And eventually the false conviction collapses.

Now, have you physically removed a tiger?

No.

There wasn’t one.

You’ve corrected your understanding.

And that’s the point.

The correction doesn’t manufacture freedom. It removes a mistaken conviction.

Now we’re getting somewhere.

2. So what exactly should I be thinking?
Excellent question.

Here’s the liberating understanding.

Listen carefully.

Beyond every limited principle of reality — even the principle called Śiva when considered as one member of the hierarchy of tattvas — lies the supreme, unrestricted reality.

Pure consciousness.

Aparicchinna-saṃvit.

Consciousness without limitation.

And this isn’t some abstract substance sitting somewhere beyond the universe.

It’s the very ground of everything that exists.

Vastuvyavasthāsthānam.

The foundation upon which the ordering of reality rests.

It’s the vitality of the universe.

Viśvasya ojaḥ.

That by which the entire cosmos lives.

And now comes the important bit.

Tad eva cāham.

That very reality am I.

Not merely something I belong to.

Not something I might eventually meet when I die.

I am that.

Therefore:

Viśvottīrṇo viśvātmā cāham.

I transcend the universe, and I am the Self of the universe.

Both.

Simultaneously.

Not one or the other.

Because if you’re only the universe, you’ve limited yourself to its forms.

And if you’re only beyond the universe, you’ve excluded the very manifestations of your own consciousness.

Neither will do.

You’re both transcendent and immanent.

The universe appears within your nature, without exhausting what you are.

Absolutely magnificent.

3. Why doesn’t everyone recognise this?
Now you might be wondering:

“Abhinava, mate, if it’s that straightforward, why doesn’t everyone just understand it?”

Well.

Because not everyone possesses the discernment necessary to arrive at this understanding.

And some people are positively determined not to.

They’ve become attached to limited doctrines.

They’ve got their particular scriptures, particular conceptions of divinity, particular explanations of reality.

And they’re absolutely delighted with them.

“THIS is the highest truth.”

“NO, THIS is the highest truth.”

“MY deity is supreme.”

“YOUR philosophy is nonsense.”

And while they’re all having a tremendous argument, the unrestricted consciousness within which they’re arguing goes entirely unrecognised.

Now, Abhinavagupta makes a rather pointed criticism of Vaiṣṇava and other traditions here.

He cites a Śaiva scripture claiming that attachment to limited religious knowledge prevents people from recognising the highest reality.

Rather sectarian of him, isn’t it?

But understand his underlying argument.

If your understanding of the ultimate is limited by the very categories you’re trying to transcend, you haven’t reached the ultimate.

If your religious worldview prevents you from investigating beyond its own boundaries, you’ve turned knowledge into another form of bondage.

And this is why you need three things.

Sat-tarka.

Correct reasoning.

Sad-āgama.

An authentic revelatory teaching.

Sad-guru.

A genuine teacher.

Not just someone with an impressive beard.

Not someone with thirty thousand followers and a particularly convincing photograph of themselves in the Himalayas.

Someone who can actually communicate the means of recognition.

Because the point of the teaching is to transform your understanding until you recognise what was already true.

Now, here’s where the philosophy gets really interesting.

4. But can you really think your way into something beyond thought?
Ah.

Here comes the clever objection.

“Hang on, Abhinava.”

Go on.

“You’ve just told me that I need to think correctly about the supreme reality.”

Yes.

“But if I can think about it, surely it must be a concept?”

No, you absolute menace.

You’ve misunderstood the whole thing.

The concept isn’t the supreme reality.

The concept removes the mistaken understanding that prevents you from recognising the supreme reality.

There’s an enormous difference.

Imagine you’re looking through a window that’s covered in dirt.

You clean the glass.

Has the cleaning created the sunlight?

No.

Has it improved the sun?

No.

Has the sun suddenly become more luminous?

Of course not.

You’ve simply removed the obstruction.

And even that analogy isn’t perfect, because the obstruction to recognising consciousness isn’t a genuinely independent thing.

It’s a mistaken way of apprehending what’s already present.

The highest reality is svaprakāśa.

Self-luminous.

Already shining.

Everywhere.

As everything.

No thought can improve it.

No thought can damage it.

Correct understanding merely dispels the habitual impression that you’re separate from it.

Dvaitādhivāsa-bhaṅga.

The breaking of the ingrained impression of duality.

And that’s the role of vikalpa.

Not to create Śiva.

Not to describe Śiva exhaustively.

But to dismantle a misunderstanding.

Very important distinction.

5. Some people are initiated by the goddesses themselves
Now, something fascinating.

For certain individuals, this discernment arises spontaneously.

They’ve been struck by such an extraordinarily powerful descent of Śakti that they don’t require someone to sit them down and work through all the philosophical arguments.

Ati-dṛḍha-śaktipāta.

An exceedingly intense descent of power.

The appropriate understanding simply arises within them.

Sāṃsiddhika.

Naturally accomplished.

And these extraordinary individuals are said to be:

Devībhir dīkṣitaḥ.

Initiated by the goddesses.

Imagine that.

No elaborate ceremony.

No priest announcing:

“Congratulations, you’re now officially qualified to recognise your own consciousness.”

The awakening has occurred through the divine powers themselves.

But for others, this understanding develops through scripture and instruction.

And I’ll explain the different degrees of śaktipāta properly in a later chapter.

For now, let’s clarify what the teacher actually does.

The teacher explains the āgama.

The scripture gives rise to correct understanding.

That understanding leads to further correct understanding.

And gradually there’s a continuous, coherent unfolding of insight.

One clarification leads to another.

One mistaken assumption collapses, exposing another.

You investigate that one.

It collapses too.

And this continuous process is called sat-tarka.

Sound discernment.

Now here’s something beautiful.

That very process is also called:

Bhāvanā.

Contemplative realisation.

Why?

Because something that already exists, but which wasn’t clearly apparent, becomes manifest through this process.

It’s not being manufactured.

It’s becoming evident.

Like gradually recognising a face in the darkness.

The face was already there.

Your seeing becomes clear.

And this brings us to something rather controversial.

6. Why yoga can’t actually give you Śiva
Right.

Put down your yoga mat.

We’ve got a problem.

You see, none of the ordinary limbs of yoga is, by itself, a direct means to supreme recognition.

“WHAT?”

Yes.

I said it.

Let’s investigate.

You’ve got yama.

Ethical restraints.

Very good.

Nonviolence and all that.

You’ve got niyama.

Discipline, austerity and observances.

Lovely.

You’ve got prāṇāyāma.

Breathing exercises.

Inhale.

Exhale.

Hold.

Try not to fall over.

Then pratyāhāra.

Withdrawal of the senses.

Then dhāraṇā, dhyāna, samādhi.

Concentration, meditation, absorption.

Magnificent collection of practices.

But I have one question.

What exactly are you trying to change?

Because all these practices operate upon something knowable.

Your body.

Your breath.

Your mental faculties.

Your attention.

Even the object of your meditation.

But consciousness itself?

The very reality through which you know any of these things?

What are you going to do to that?

Imagine you’re practising lifting enormous stones.

Over time your body becomes stronger.

Fine.

You’re practising dance.

You become an excellent dancer.

Beautiful.

You’re studying some complicated philosophical treatise.

Eventually you understand the arguments.

Wonderful.

But in each case, practice is developing some particular capacity.

You’re bringing a potential into expression.

Now tell me.

What additional capacity could possibly be added to unlimited consciousness?

Nothing.

And what could be removed from it?

Nothing.

It’s already complete.

Pūrṇa.

So how exactly are you going to practise your way into becoming what you already are?

“Well, surely samādhi helps?”

Samādhi can produce a profound identification with the object of meditation.

But that’s still a particular accomplishment.

A particular state.

A particular relationship between the meditator and something contemplated.

Supreme consciousness is not merely another meditative object.

It’s the reality in which the meditator, meditation, and object of meditation all appear.

And if you’re thinking:

“Right, so reasoning is also pointless then?”

No, mate.

We’ve already dealt with this.

Reasoning doesn’t manufacture consciousness.

It removes your mistaken view of it.

It has a different function.

And here’s an even subtler point.

The so-called impression of duality isn’t some actual substance that needs to be physically scraped away.

It’s svarūpākhyāti.

Failure to recognise one’s own nature.

What actually happens is that supreme consciousness, which has freely assumed the appearance of nonrecognition, progressively manifests itself more clearly.

First it turns toward expansion.

Vikāsonmukha.

Then it expands.

Vikasat.

Then it stands fully expanded.

Vikasita.

Like a lotus opening.

But the opening itself is also an expression of Śiva.

Nothing has occurred outside him.

Even the process of awakening belongs to the freedom of that which awakens.

So yoga practices can support discernment.

They can help prepare and refine the faculties.

But in this method, the direct means is sat-tarka.

And sat-tarka itself is an expression of:

Śuddhavidyā.

Pure knowledge.

Now, this doesn’t mean we’re abandoning religious practices.

Quite the contrary.

I’m about to explain what they really mean.

And this is where things get absolutely beautiful.

7. What worship actually means
Let’s begin with yāga.

Worship.

Now, ordinary worship might involve offering flowers, fragrances, food and other beautiful things to a deity.

Very nice.

But what’s actually happening at the deepest level?

You’re offering everything to Parameśvara.

Not because he needs your flowers.

The supreme Lord isn’t sitting there going:

“Bloody hell, nobody’s brought me a jasmine garland today.”

He’s complete.

What the offering does is cultivate the recognition that every single thing already rests within him.

Nothing exists apart from Parameśvara.

So when you offer a flower, you’re recognising that the flower is already a manifestation of the divine consciousness.

And why use beautiful things?

Why flowers?

Why fragrances?

Why delightful offerings?

Because pleasure naturally draws consciousness into a state of absorption.

A lovely fragrance.

A beautiful flower.

A delightful taste.

These can more readily become occasions for entering into the Heart of awareness.

So the external ritual has a deeper significance.

You’re not transporting an object from the secular world into a separate divine world.

You’re recognising that the supposed division was misconceived in the first place.

True worship is the offering of all appearances into the consciousness in which they already exist.

Now let’s take it further.

8. The real fire sacrifice
Next.

Homa.

The fire offering.

Traditionally, you establish a sacred fire and offer substances into it.

But here’s the internal meaning.

All things are constituted by the radiance of the supreme Lord.

Parameśvara-tejas.

And consciousness itself can be contemplated as a fire.

Saṃvid-anala.

The fire of awareness.

Now imagine every experience being offered into that fire.

Every sight.

Every sound.

Every thought.

Every sensation.

Every remembered experience.

Everything.

And as the fire consumes them, what remains?

The radiance of consciousness itself.

Not a pile of spiritual ashes.

Not some cosmic bonfire in the sky.

The understanding that every manifested form is nothing apart from the divine luminosity.

So the deepest homa is the dissolution of all differentiated appearances into their own conscious essence.

And the fire?

That’s Śiva.

The offerings?

Also Śiva.

The person making the offerings?

You guessed it.

Śiva.

The entire sacrificial business is Śiva offering Śiva into Śiva.

Rather familiar, isn’t it?

9. What mantra repetition actually means
Now we come to japa.

Mantra repetition.

You might imagine japa is simply repeating some Sanskrit syllables a few thousand times.

And yes, vocal repetition has its place.

But the deeper japa of this approach is the internal recognition:

The supreme reality is my own essential nature, regardless of the particular things appearing within or outside me.

Let the objects change.

Let thoughts change.

Let sensations change.

Let the entire world undergo its endless transformations.

The supreme consciousness isn’t diminished by any of it.

Your contemplation is the repeated recognition of that truth.

Not merely:

“Om namaḥ śivāya, Om namaḥ śivāya, Om namaḥ śivāya.”

While you’re secretly thinking about what you’re having for dinner.

Rather, an inward parāmarśa.

The direct apprehension of the supreme reality as your own nature.

That’s the essential point.

And then comes a particularly extraordinary definition.

10. The highest religious vow
Vrata.

A vow.

A sacred observance.

You might imagine this means keeping a strict schedule, avoiding particular foods, or following a set of ritual instructions.

But here’s my definition.

Look upon absolutely everything as equal in its essential reality to the supreme Lord.

Your body?

Śiva.

That pot sitting over there?

Śiva.

The person you like?

Śiva.

The person you can’t stand?

Also Śiva.

The world is not divided into things possessing divine consciousness and things entirely excluded from it.

All are manifestations of that same fundamental reality.

And I’m not inventing this.

The Nandiśikhā states:

सर्वसाम्यं परं व्रतम्
Sarvasāmyaṃ paraṃ vratam.

The supreme observance is complete equality.

Now, doesn’t that turn the entire idea of religious observance upside down?

The highest vow isn’t merely something you do at a particular hour.

It’s a way of apprehending reality.

Everywhere.

Always.

No exception to the ultimate unity of consciousness.

11. And finally, yoga
Now that we’ve explained worship, fire-offering, mantra repetition and religious observance, we can return to yoga.

Because there’s a deeper form of yoga consistent with what I’ve been saying.

Through these varied forms of correct contemplation, the supreme reality becomes evident.

It’s not created by concepts.

But its nature is clarified through them.

And then a special form of contemplation arises.

Yoga.

The sustained investigation and recognition of one’s true nature, establishing the clarity of its ever-present manifestation.

So you see?

I’m not saying all practice is useless.

I’m saying the real value of practice depends on what it reveals.

The ritual, the contemplation, the mantra, the observance and the yoga all converge upon one reality.

And that reality is not waiting to become divine.

It already is.

Right.

Now we’ve got to discuss Śakti herself.

12. Śakti has more names than anyone could possibly remember
The supreme Lord is complete consciousness.

Pūrṇa-saṃvit.

And his power is precisely that completeness.

Pūrṇatā.

Now, different scriptures describe that fullness under different names.

Kula.

The totality.

Sāmarthya.

Potency.

Ūrmi.

The wave.

Hṛdaya.

The Heart.

Sāra.

The essence.

Spanda.

The pulsation.

Vibhūti.

The manifesting power.

Trīśikā.

The threefold goddess.

Kālī.

The dark goddess.

Karṣaṇī.

She who draws or pulls inward.

Caṇḍī.

The fierce goddess.

Vāṇī.

Speech.

Bhoga.

Experience and enjoyment.

Dṛk.

Seeing.

Nityā.

The eternal one.

And so on.

Now, are these fifteen entirely separate ultimate realities?

No.

They’re names that express different aspects and activities of one supreme power.

The scriptures use different names so that the contemplator can approach the divine reality through the aspect most appropriate to their understanding.

And what is the ultimate power of this reality?

Its completeness.

Its possession of all powers.

Now, how many powers does Śiva actually have?

Innumerable.

Seriously.

Try counting them.

Everything in the universe is an expression of these powers.

Every possible manifestation.

Every capacity.

Every event.

Every form.

How could I possibly explain them individually?

We’d be here until the next cosmic dissolution.

So I’ll simplify matters.

We can understand the whole universe through three fundamental powers.

And now we’re entering the heart of Trika.

13. The three great goddesses
First.

Parā-śakti.

The supreme power.

Through her, Parameśvara manifests, apprehends and sustains the entire universe — from the highest principle of Śiva right down to the earth — as undifferentiated consciousness.

No ultimate division.

No separate realities.

Everything is apprehended in its identity with pure awareness.

This is Parā.

Now, second.

Parāparā-śakti.

The intermediate power.

Here there’s an experience of both unity and differentiation.

Remember the mirror from our last chapter?

Imagine a mirror containing an image of an elephant.

You see the elephant.

You see the mirror.

There’s an apparent difference between them.

But the elephant appearing in the mirror has no independent existence within that mirror apart from its reflective surface.

So there’s difference and nondifference.

Bhedābheda.

Parāparā manifests this intermediate relationship.

Now third.

Aparā-śakti.

The lower power.

And here we have explicit differentiation.

You experience yourself as one thing.

The world as another.

One object distinct from another object.

Different people.

Different places.

Different experiences.

Difference predominates.

Bheda.

Now, don’t imagine Aparā is somehow a rival goddess who’s gone rogue and started manufacturing an evil universe.

She’s still Śakti.

Differentiation is itself one of the powers of supreme consciousness.

So we’ve got three principal ways reality is apprehended.

Undifferentiated unity.

Unity together with difference.

And explicit difference.

But now comes the fourth.

14. The goddess who swallows all three
Right.

We’ve got Parā.

Parāparā.

Aparā.

Now what happens when consciousness draws all three modes back into itself?

When even the distinction between these modes is absorbed?

We encounter a supreme, all-embracing power.

Also spoken of as Śrī Parā, in her transcendent aspect.

And she possesses names such as:

Mātṛsadbhāva.

The essential reality of the knowing subject.

And:

Kālakarṣiṇī.

She who draws time into herself.

Now that is a terrifyingly beautiful name.

Because what is time?

The succession of appearances.

The ordering of things into before and after.

The very field in which experiences arise, endure and disappear.

And here is the goddess who draws that entire process back into its own conscious source.

She consumes the threefold manifestation.

Not because the universe is something disgusting that needs destroying.

But because the totality of manifestation is never independent of her.

All distinction can be gathered back into its supreme ground.

Now.

We have four fundamental aspects of Śakti.

And each can operate through three processes.

Creation.

Maintenance.

Dissolution.

Four times three.

Twelve.

And this gives us the twelve Kālīs.

Oh, yes.

We’re going deeper.

15. The twelve Kālīs — how consciousness devours its own universe
Now, before you imagine twelve enormous dark goddesses stomping across the cosmos, understand something.

We’re investigating the actual process of awareness.

How it brings forth appearances.

How it sustains them.

And how it withdraws them.

These twelve powers can be understood as twelve moments in the activity of consciousness.

Watch carefully.

First.

Consciousness forms an appearance internally.

Before the thing becomes explicitly manifest, it takes shape within awareness.

An internal emergence.

The beginning of creation.

Second.

The appearance becomes distinct and explicit.

And consciousness takes an interest in it.

It becomes coloured by attraction.

Rakti.

A kind of fascination with its own manifestation.

Imagine seeing something absolutely beautiful.

For a moment, consciousness becomes absorbed in the object.

There’s the appearance.

And there’s delight in its appearing.

Third.

Now the movement turns inward.

Consciousness begins to withdraw that appearance.

The thing that seemed to stand before awareness begins to be reabsorbed.

Fourth.

But something interrupts.

Doubt.

Śaṅkā.

A hesitation.

A resistance to withdrawal.

And consciousness generates this hesitation and consumes it.

Absolutely extraordinary.

The obstruction itself is another manifestation of consciousness.

It doesn’t have some mysterious independent existence.

Fifth.

With the hesitation consumed, the object is absorbed inward.

The thing that appeared separate is gathered back into awareness.

Sixth.

Now consciousness recognises its own power of withdrawal.

It apprehends that the capacity to dissolve appearances belongs to its nature.

Not merely:

“That object disappeared.”

But the deeper recognition:

“I am that within which its manifestation and disappearance occur.”

Seventh.

Now even the residual traces of the experience are considered.

Some remain as subtle impressions.

Vāsanā.

Others are completely dissolved into pure consciousness.

The experience is being withdrawn at increasingly subtle levels.

Eighth.

The entire apparatus through which experiences arise is drawn inward.

The karaṇa-cakra.

The wheel of faculties.

Not merely the visible objects.

But the powers through which they’re known.

Ninth.

Even the presiding principle of those faculties is withdrawn.

Karaṇeśvara.

The lord of the instruments of cognition.

Even that organising centre has no final independence.

Tenth.

Now the limited subject itself is withdrawn.

The constructed individual knower.

The one who says:

“I am this person observing these things.”

This is the māyīya pramātṛ.

The subject formed under the conditions of māyā.

And consciousness consumes even this.

Eleventh.

Now there’s an extraordinary movement toward complete expansion.

Contraction is being abandoned.

The subject that is no longer confined to ordinary individuality approaches unrestricted awareness.

Even this expanded knower is gathered back.

Twelfth.

And finally, even the fully expanded manifestation is absorbed into the supreme ground.

Nothing remains as a separate entity standing over against consciousness.

The process has returned to its source.

Now, pay attention.

This isn’t merely some cosmological story about what happens when the universe ends.

These twelve movements can occur within a single act of awareness.

The emergence of a thought.

Your fascination with it.

Its persistence.

Its disappearance.

The dissolution of the apparent object.

The withdrawal of the faculties.

And ultimately the recognition of the conscious ground.

Nor do these twelve movements have to arise one after another in a rigid sequence.

They may appear together.

In pairs.

In groups of three.

Across multiple subjects or within a single subject.

They revolve like a wheel.

Cakra.

And their pattern can be contemplated externally in larger cycles.

The months.

The solar phases.

The zodiacal divisions.

Or even smaller ritual arrangements involving pots, cloths and other objects.

All reflect patterns of manifestation, maintenance and withdrawal.

And these twelve goddesses are called:

Śrī Kālī.

Now, why Kālī?

Because of the multiple meanings associated with kalana.

Movement.

Projection.

Knowing.

Reckoning.

Experiencing.

Articulation.

And absorption into oneself.

The goddess creates.

The goddess knows.

The goddess experiences.

The goddess withdraws.

She is not merely operating upon an already existing universe.

She is the very power through which the universe is manifested, known and reabsorbed.

My teacher Bhūtirāja explained this in terms of the goddess’s powers of projection and knowledge.

And there’s much more to be said.

I’ve explored these teachings in my other works.

But there’s a reason I’m not putting every single esoteric detail in one place.

My teachers taught me that the deepest secrets should neither be revealed all together nor concealed absolutely.

So that’s as far as we’re going here.

For now.

16. Stop treating limited gods as the absolute
Now, let’s return to our five practices.

Worship.

Fire-offering.

Japa.

Vows.

Yoga.

Everything I’ve explained must be understood in relation to this supreme Mahēśvara.

The unrestricted Lord.

Not merely a particular limited form somewhere within the hierarchy of reality.

Now this is an important point.

Within our philosophical account, even the divine principles ranging from Viṣṇu to the tattva called Śiva are not to be mistaken, in their differentiated forms, for the unsurpassable absolute.

Paramaśiva.

The highest reality isn’t merely one more item in a hierarchy.

It’s the very ground of the hierarchy.

And the yogin seeking anuttara, the unsurpassable, must relinquish any understanding that mistakes a limited manifestation for the totality.

My predecessor Vidyādhipati made a particularly forceful point about this in his Anubhavastotra.

The supreme reality must not be reduced to some limited theological conception.

It’s the living reality of experience itself.

And as understanding becomes established, the practices should be undertaken in whatever form proves appropriate.

Now, there’s one final matter.

And this is going to upset some very serious religious people.

17. Stop tormenting yourself over purity rules
Right.

Let’s talk about food.

Because apparently humans have developed an extraordinary talent for turning eating into a philosophical catastrophe.

“This food is pure.”

“That food is impure.”

“You can eat this on Tuesday.”

“Absolutely forbidden on Thursday.”

“Touching that object contaminates you.”

“Unless you’re performing this ceremony, in which case it’s suddenly fine.”

Enough.

Let’s investigate what purity actually is.

Is purity an intrinsic property of an object?

Like its colour?

Take something blue.

Under ordinary circumstances, its blueness is a property of the thing.

But ritual purity?

Consider how the very same act can be declared permissible in one religious context and forbidden in another.

For example, a charitable donation might ordinarily be approved.

Yet under particular Vedic sacrificial restrictions, an initiated individual may be forbidden from giving it.

So has the donation itself physically transformed?

Has something inside it suddenly become metaphysically contaminated?

No.

The difference comes from the applicable religious injunction.

Now someone objects:

“But Abhinava! Those rules are authoritative because the Veda says so!”

Very well.

But other scriptures prescribe different rules.

“Yes, but those don’t count because the Veda overrides them!”

Ah.

And why exactly should that be accepted?

Within our Śaiva tradition, we hold that Śiva’s injunction possesses the overriding authority, supported by reasoning and scriptures such as the Sarvajñānottara.

So merely appealing to another injunction won’t settle the matter.

But the deeper issue for our present purpose is this:

Purity and impurity, as ritual classifications, aren’t inherent features of objects in the way ordinary physical properties are.

They’re established through systems of conceptual distinction and prescription.

Now, do not misunderstand me.

I’m not telling you that every action is equally wise, harmless or beneficial.

We’re discussing the metaphysical status of ritual distinctions within the liberated vision.

And at the level of practice we’re describing, the yogin should not exhaust himself through endless conceptual anxieties about what may be eaten, what may not, what is pure, and what is impure.

These distinctions are not the supreme reality.

In fact, even the various prescriptions and prohibitions found throughout religious traditions — whether Vedic, Śaiva Siddhānta, tantric, Kula, or other systems — have no independent capacity to confer the final recognition we’re discussing.

They may have their functions in particular contexts.

But they’re not the direct means here.

The direct means remains clear discernment of the supreme nature of consciousness.

And the practitioner should approach the five practices in whatever manner actually cultivates that recognition.

Not through blind anxiety.

Not through compulsive rule-following.

But through insight.

And now we can conclude.

18. The final verses — you’re not the little creature you think you are
Let me tell you what the bound individual believes.

From Abhinavagupta’s first concluding verse:

Jaḍo’smi.

I am inert.

Karma-sampāśito’smi.

I am tied up by the bonds of karma.

Malino’smi.

I am impure.

Parerito’smi.

I am driven about by something other than myself.

That’s the ordinary condition of the paśu.

The bound being.

And notice something.

These are not merely fleeting thoughts.

They’re convictions.

Niścaya.

Fundamental certainties about what one is.

The person has become utterly convinced of their own limitation.

But suppose another conviction arises.

A conviction opposed to all these limiting understandings.

And suppose it becomes firmly established.

Then, says Abhinavagupta, the bound being immediately recognises the condition of the Lord.

Pati.

Whose body is the entire universe.

Viśva-vapus.

Whose nature is consciousness.

Cid-ātmā.

Now, isn’t that remarkable?

The transition from paśu to pati.

From the apparent captive to the Lord.

Not through manufacturing an entirely new consciousness.

But through the collapse of the mistaken understanding of what consciousness is.

And the second verse gives the instruction.

However this correct conviction is attained, the supreme yogin should continually cultivate it.

Don’t abandon it because somebody has come along with an impressive argument based on a mistaken conception of reality.

Don’t allow your understanding to collapse merely because the world is full of contradictory opinions.

Examine things according to their actual nature.

Vastu-yāthātmya.

And remain established in the understanding that reveals that nature correctly.

So here’s where we’ve arrived.

You began believing:

“I’m this limited individual.”

Then you investigated.

You saw that the reality of every experience is consciousness.

You saw that consciousness is not something separate from your own essential nature.

You saw that every apparent object, every action and every form of worship arises within that reality.

And through correct discernment, the conviction of separateness is progressively overcome.

Until the very thought that you’re a captive loses its hold.

And now?

The awareness within which the universe appears recognises itself as the supreme Lord.

You haven’t gone anywhere.

You haven’t obtained an entirely different Self.

You’ve recognised what you are.

Śiva, who freely appeared as the limited individual, recognises himself through Śakti, the very power by which he appeared limited.

And that’s Śāktopāya.

The method of Śakti.

Using the power of understanding to bring the misunderstanding of separation to an end.

Rather clever, isn’t it?

End of Chapter Four — Śāktopāya.
