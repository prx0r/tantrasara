# Tantrasāra — Chapter 15

पञ्चदशमाह्निकम् — Pañcadaśam Āhnikam

The Deathbed Initiation: or, how Śiva decided even the Grim Reaper wasn’t getting the final bloody word.


Right then.

Last chapter.

Quite a ceremony, wasn’t it?

We built a magnificent trident maṇḍala.

Installed Parā, Parāparā and Aparā.

Consecrated the entire universe.

Performed offerings to the deities.

Brought the disciple into the sacred space.

Purified all six cosmic pathways.

And eventually united the disciple’s consciousness with the supreme Lord.

Absolutely magnificent.

But you’ll remember something important.

At the very end, I made an exception.

The currently operative karma wasn’t destroyed.

Vartamāna-karma.

The karma sustaining the present embodiment.

So the disciple could continue living.

They could eat.

Sleep.

Study the scriptures.

Practise their mantras.

Have a cup of tea.

And eventually, when the body’s natural course was complete, attain the result promised by initiation.

Lovely.

But now imagine someone approaches us with a rather different situation.

Their life is coming to an end.

They are already dying.

And suddenly, right at that moment, something extraordinary occurs.

Śaktipāta.

The descent of divine power.

Now, are we seriously going to tell this person:

“Ah, terribly sorry, mate. You’ve missed the application deadline.”

“Initiations take three working days.”

“Perhaps come back in your next incarnation.”

Absolutely not.

Because Abhinavagupta has a specific rite for this.

And it’s called:

Samutkramaṇa-dīkṣā.

Let’s understand that word.

Sam-ut-kram-aṇa.

The act of proceeding or passing upward and out.

And dīkṣā — initiation.

Initiation of departure or ascent.

Now, I should make something clear before we enter the ritual.

This is a historical tantric death-related rite, and its Sanskrit explicitly links the ceremony to the departure of the individual’s life-consciousness. We can study its metaphysics without treating it as a safe method of hastening death. Nothing in this narration is a practical instruction to manipulate someone’s breathing, vital points or dying process.

Right.

Let’s begin.

1. Śaktipāta can happen when someone’s already dying
Here’s the opening Sanskrit:

ATTESTED PROSE — 15.1

यदा पुनरासन्नमरणस्य स्वयं वा बन्धुमुखेन शक्तिपात उपजायते तदास्मै सद्यः समुत्क्रमणदीक्षां कुर्यात्।
Yadā punar āsannamaraṇasya svayaṃ vā bandhumukhena śaktipāta upajāyate tadāsmai sadyaḥ samutkramaṇadīkṣāṃ kuryāt.

Now let’s break this down.

Āsanna-maraṇa.

Approaching death.

Svayam.

By itself, spontaneously.

Bandhu-mukhena.

Through the agency or mediation of a relative.

Śaktipāta upajāyate.

The descent of power arises.

And then:

Sadyaḥ.

Immediately.

The guru is to perform the initiation without delay.

Now that’s fascinating.

Because someone can apparently receive śaktipāta at the very end of life.

Perhaps the person has never previously received the full initiation.

Perhaps their whole life has been spent in ordinary concerns.

Perhaps they’ve never studied a line of the Tantrāloka.

Not even the introduction.

And suddenly, as death approaches, the divine power descends.

Now, remember Chapter Eleven.

We established that śaktipāta isn’t ultimately produced by karma.

It isn’t a reward distributed according to some cosmic accounting system.

It’s the manifestation of Śiva’s unrestricted freedom.

Svātantrya.

So there’s no metaphysical reason why the end of bodily life should make recognition impossible.

The freedom of Śiva is not subordinate to the body’s remaining lifespan.

And that explains why this chapter comes directly after the putraka initiation.

The previous rite provides the fuller ritual framework.

This one addresses the special condition of approaching death.

But here’s the important thing.

We’re not merely saying pleasant words at someone’s bedside.

The original text prescribes an esoteric rite understood to effect the departure of the individual life-principle.

And the deity central to that process?

Well.

She’s absolutely terrifying.

In the most magnificent tantric sense.

2. Kālarātrī — the goddess who cuts the bonds
Now we meet:

Bhagavatī Kālarātrī.

भगवती कालरात्री

The venerable Goddess Kālarātrī.

Let’s break down her name.

Kāla — time, with associations of mortality and destruction.

Rātrī — night.

The Night of Time.

Now, that’s a rather extraordinary deity to encounter at the threshold of death.

And she has a particular epithet here.

Marmakartanī.

मर्मकर्तनी

Marma — a vital or vulnerable point, a hidden junction, or something essential.

Kartanī — a cutter.

So:

She who cuts the vital junctions.

Now, here’s what the Sanskrit says happens within the rite.

First, the six cosmic pathways are installed in the disciple.

Samasta-adhvan.

All six.

Remember them?

Bhuvana.

Tattva.

Kalā.

Pada.

Mantra.

Varṇa.

The worlds.

The principles.

The cosmic divisions.

The expressions.

The mantras.

The phonemes.

The whole bloody universe.

Again.

Because apparently you can’t initiate someone in Trika without first giving them the entire cosmos to deal with.

And then the guru purifies this sixfold manifestation successively.

Kramaśaḥ śodhayitvā.

After that, Kālarātrī is ritually installed as the power that severs the bonds associated with the vital junctions.

Marma-pāśa.

Now, we’ve encountered pāśa before.

A bond.

A fetter.

Something that restricts the individual.

But here the bonds are associated specifically with the vital structure of embodiment.

And the text attributes their severing to Kālarātrī.

Now, don’t imagine the point is ordinary anatomical surgery.

We’re dealing with a tantric ritual theory of the life-principle and its relation to the body’s subtle structures.

Nevertheless, the rite’s intended effect is directly connected with death and departure, not merely psychological relaxation.

And after the removal of these bonds, the disciple’s consciousness is described as being established at:

Brahmarandhra.

ब्रह्मरन्ध्र

The cranial aperture of Brahmā.

The uppermost opening in the traditional subtle-body account.

Now, that should sound familiar from our earlier chapters.

We’ve discussed subtle upper centres.

The dvādaśānta.

The ascent of vital power.

The dissolution of differentiated consciousness.

And the supreme reality beyond ordinary spatial limitation.

But here the symbolism has a particular function.

The individual life-consciousness is ritually directed toward departure.

And then comes the concluding offering.

Which is where the entire ceremony reaches its decisive moment.

3. The final offering — and there is no coming back for another lifetime
Right.

We’ve purified the cosmic pathways.

Kālarātrī has been invoked.

The ritual has addressed the bonds associated with embodied existence.

And now comes:

Pūrṇāhuti.

पूर्णाहुति

The complete oblation.

Remember Chapter Fourteen?

The fire offering that completed the purification of the tattvas?

Well, here the concluding oblation serves a particularly decisive purpose.

Yojanikā.

Joining the disciple to the intended state.

And Abhinavagupta says that at the conclusion of the offering, the departed individual becomes non-different from Paramaśiva.

Paramaśiva-abhinnaḥ.

Now let’s appreciate how strong that claim actually is.

The disciple isn’t merely going to a particularly pleasant heavenly world.

Not simply becoming a slightly more impressive celestial being.

Not being transferred to a deluxe apartment in Brahmaloka.

The intended outcome is:

Non-difference from supreme Śiva.

And in this particular death rite, the text describes the individual life-consciousness departing at the completion of the oblation.

Jīvo niṣkrāntaḥ.

The individual has departed.

Now, this is more than a metaphor for an expanded state of meditation. The rite is expressly connected with the end of embodied life, and historical accounts of it raise serious questions about ritual intervention in dying.

So this is a doctrine to understand, not a procedure to attempt.

But within the theology, the point is extraordinary.

Remember what we established at the beginning of the Tantrasāra?

Your essential nature isn’t the body.

The body is a manifestation of consciousness.

And the contracted individual is Śiva appearing through limitation.

So when the limiting bonds are overcome, the result isn’t the creation of a new divine substance.

It’s the ending of the limited condition.

And because the person is at the threshold of death, the rite is directed toward that transition rather than the continuation of ordinary embodied life.

Now, here’s a question.

“What if the dying person doesn’t actually want immediate supreme liberation?”

Ah.

Interesting.

Because some people still desire:

Bhoga.

Experience.

And Abhinavagupta has accounted for that.

4. What if they still want one last cosmic holiday?
Right.

Bubhukṣu.

Someone who desires enjoyment or experience.

Now, remember the distinction from Chapter Fourteen.

A mumukṣu desires liberation.

A bubhukṣu desires experience.

And the difference matters because the ritual is directed toward different outcomes.

For the enjoyment-seeker, Abhinavagupta prescribes a second complete oblation.

Dvitīyā pūrṇāhutiḥ.

And what does that second oblation accomplish?

The disciple is joined with:

Bhoga-sthāna.

The realm or condition of the desired experience.

Now imagine someone approaching the guru at the end of life.

“Guruji.”

“Yes?”

“I’d like liberation.”

“Very good.”

“But before that, could I perhaps experience a magnificent divine world?”

Ah.

There it is.

Still making requests.

Even on the way out.

Now, Abhinavagupta doesn’t simply dismiss the possibility.

He distinguishes the intended result and assigns a further ritual action to it.

The initial rite addresses departure and the dissolution of the limited life-condition.

The additional oblation concerns the desired realm of enjoyment.

And the text says:

Na atra śeṣavartanam.

There’s no remaining continuation of the present life-process here, or no further remaining procedure, depending upon how the compressed phrase is interpreted.

Either way, this is not the same arrangement as Chapter Fourteen, where an initiated disciple receives a ritually purified body for the continuation of life.

Here the condition of impending death is fundamental.

And now, after all this extraordinary business involving Kālarātrī, cosmic pathways, vital bonds and complete oblations, Abhinavagupta suddenly gives us a much simpler alternative.

And I think this is the most beautiful part of the chapter.

Because apparently you can perform a profoundly significant deathbed religious act by speaking into someone’s ear.

5. Brahmavidyā — the teaching whispered at death
Here’s the actual Sanskrit:

ATTESTED PROSE — 15.5

ब्रह्मविद्यां वा कर्णे पठेत्
Brahmavidyāṃ vā karṇe paṭhet.

Or one should recite the Brahmavidyā into the ear.

Now, this is extraordinary.

Brahman — the absolute reality.

Vidyā — knowledge, sacred understanding; here also designating an esoteric formula.

Brahmavidyā.

Knowledge of the Absolute.

And:

Karṇa.

Ear.

Paṭhet.

One should recite.

So there you are.

Someone is approaching death.

And instead of the elaborate rite involving the full purification of the cosmic pathways, the text allows the Brahmavidyā to be recited into their ear.

Now, why would that work?

Abhinavagupta gives an explanation.

And it’s not simply that the person hears some comforting words.

He says the Brahmavidyā is:

Parāmarśa-svabhāvā.

Of the nature of reflexive self-apprehension.

Now listen carefully.

This is the very principle we’ve been investigating throughout the entire work.

Vimarśa.

Consciousness aware of itself.

Not merely a light illuminating external objects.

But the living self-awareness through which consciousness apprehends its own nature.

And Abhinavagupta says that the Brahmavidyā awakens:

Prabuddha-vimarśa.

Awakened self-awareness.

Within the consciousness of the limited being.

Paśu-caitanya.

And this happens:

Sadyaḥ.

Immediately.

Now, that’s magnificent.

Because think about the apparent simplicity.

An individual has spent an entire lifetime identifying with the body.

They’ve experienced pleasure.

Pain.

Attachment.

Separation.

All the ordinary movements of saṃsāra.

And now they’re dying.

And the tradition proposes that the appropriately transmitted sacred knowledge can awaken the recognition of consciousness’s own nature.

Not because the words magically manufacture a new Self.

But because, in this teaching, the formula’s significance lies in its capacity to disclose the self-awareness that was always fundamental.

The sound points consciousness back toward its own reality.

Now, remember Chapter Three.

The phonemes were expressions of divine powers.

And Chapter Five.

Mantra wasn’t merely language referring to external objects.

It could embody the spanda, the pulsation of consciousness itself.

And Chapter Eleven.

A genuine teacher could communicate liberating recognition through speech.

All those teachings converge here.

A sacred utterance.

At the threshold of death.

Directed toward awakened self-awareness.

Now, Abhinavagupta does not spell out the actual syllables of the Brahmavidyā in this short passage.

So we’re not going to invent a mantra, call it his deathbed formula and pretend it’s present in the Sanskrit.

But the doctrinal mechanism is explicit:

Parāmarśa gives rise to prabuddha-vimarśa.

Reflexive apprehension awakens self-recognition.

And then comes an unexpectedly generous detail.

6. Even a samayī may recite it
Remember the samayī?

The initiate from Chapter Thirteen.

The person qualified for daily worship, mantra practice, listening and study, but not necessarily authorised to perform every advanced ritual.

Well, Abhinavagupta says:

Samayyāder api caitatpāṭhe’dhikāraḥ.

Even a samayī and others beginning with that category are authorised to perform this recitation.

Now, isn’t that interesting?

Because we’ve spent several chapters distinguishing initiatory qualifications.

Not everyone can perform every rite.

The guru has particular responsibilities.

The higher rituals require appropriate knowledge and authorisation.

But here, a comparatively junior initiate can participate in this act of sacred recitation.

Now imagine someone dying.

And there’s no fully qualified Trika master standing beside them.

No elaborate maṇḍala.

No complete initiatory fire ceremony.

But there is someone initiated into the tradition.

And the text allows that person to recite the Brahmavidyā.

That’s quite a significant exception to the otherwise elaborate ritual hierarchy.

But again, the stated result is a traditional religious claim.

It doesn’t mean that saying a particular formula guarantees a measurable postmortem outcome, or that words should be imposed upon someone who doesn’t want them.

In practice, compassionate spiritual care should respect the dying person’s wishes and never replace appropriate medical or palliative care.

Now, we might think the chapter is finished.

But Abhinavagupta has another case to discuss.

And it’s rather peculiar.

What if someone has received śaktipāta but cannot understand the full teaching?

What if the person is so deeply confused that the ordinary initiatory disciplines are impossible?

And what if we want the initiation to remove even the seeds of further karmic production?

Right.

Now comes:

Nirbījā-dīkṣā.

Seedless initiation.

And Abhinavagupta is about to set the guru’s right hand on fire.

Not literally.

It’s a ritual visualisation.

But the image is spectacular.

7. The Hand of Śiva — an absolutely ferocious visualisation
Now we’re discussing:

Sapratyayā nirbījā dīkṣā.

Seedless initiation accompanied by clear assurance or conviction.

And the recipient is described as:

Mūḍha.

Confused or incapable of grasping the ordinary instruction.

But with an important qualification.

Āyāta-śaktipāta.

The descent of divine power has come to them.

Now, remember Chapter Eleven.

Śaktipāta doesn’t necessarily imply that the person has an elaborate conceptual understanding.

Some may receive immediate insight.

Others may need a teacher.

And here, someone may be unable to fulfil the usual intellectual requirements while nevertheless being considered a recipient of divine grace.

So the rite is directed toward the removal of the remaining seeds of bondage.

Now, the relevant ceremony involves:

Śivahasta.

The Hand of Śiva.

And Abhinavagupta describes an extraordinary visual image associated with it.

A fiery triangular maṇḍala.

Trikoṇa.

Filled with fierce flames.

Jvālā-karāla.

Sparks associated with the phoneme:

Repha — र्.

And a great wheel of wind.

Vātyācakra.

Imagine the image.

Fire.

Wind.

A blazing triangle.

The expressive power of Sanskrit sound.

All brought together in the divine hand.

Now, the purpose of this visualisation isn’t to injure someone physically.

The rite is presented as the destruction of the subtle potency responsible for continued bondage.

But the Sanskrit is quite specific about the symbolism.

A physical seed represents the generative power.

Janana-śakti.

And that power is ritually subjected to the fire of the mantra.

The protective and destructive syllable:

Phaṭ — फट्.

Remember that from Chapter Thirteen?

The weapon-mantra associated with removing obstacles?

Well, here it appears in connection with the destruction of the seed’s generative capacity.

Now, notice something beautiful about the image.

A seed is small.

Almost insignificant.

You might barely notice it.

Yet it contains the capacity to produce an entirely new plant.

So the seed is an excellent symbol for latent potential.

A whole future manifestation exists in possibility.

And here the ritual is directed toward destroying that potential.

Not a particular already-manifest object.

But the power through which further manifestation would arise.

And that brings us to the actual meaning of:

Nirbīja.

8. Nirbīja — no more bloody seeds
Let’s break down the word.

Nir — without.

Bīja — seed.

Seedless.

Now, think about how this connects with karma.

Karmic impressions are understood as possessing the potential to produce further experience.

Saṃskāra.

A latent impression.

A tendency.

A condition capable of giving rise to future results.

Like a seed.

And a seed doesn’t need to be producing a plant right now to possess that potential.

It may remain dormant.

Then, under appropriate conditions, it gives rise to another manifestation.

Similarly, the tantric account treats certain bonds as retaining the capacity to generate further conditioned existence.

Now Abhinavagupta says the ritual destroys:

Svakāryakaraṇa-sāmarthya.

The capacity to produce one’s own effect.

Let’s break that down.

Sva — one’s own.

Kārya — effect, what is produced.

Karaṇa — causing or producing.

Sāmarthya — capacity, potency.

So:

The capacity to generate the corresponding result.

And its destruction is:

Vidhvaṃsinī.

That which destroys.

Now that’s a remarkably precise metaphysical idea.

The rite isn’t merely understood to remove one particular painful experience.

It’s directed toward the very capacity of the remaining karmic seeds to bring about further bondage.

And here the traditional image becomes clear.

A seed whose productive capacity has been destroyed no longer gives rise to the corresponding plant.

Likewise, within the ritual theory, the seeds of further conditioned existence no longer produce their previous effects.

Nirbīja-dīkṣā is directed toward the exhaustion of the productive power of bondage.

Now, there’s an important nuance.

Abhinavagupta relates the seed used in the rite and the disciple’s latent generative capacity through the same symbolic operation.

The first supplies the ritual representation.

The second is the intended metaphysical target.

And then he says something absolutely astonishing.

Because apparently this kind of initiation can be extended beyond ordinary human beings.

Much further.

9. Apparently even trees can be initiated
Now, here’s the Sanskrit:

Sthāvarāṇām api dīkṣyatvena uktatvāt.

Because even stationary beings are said to be eligible for initiation.

Now, sthāvara is a fascinating word.

From the root associated with standing or remaining stationary.

It can refer to immobile beings, especially plants and trees.

Now imagine the scene.

A guru approaches a tree.

“Right, mate.”

“You’re getting initiated.”

The tree remains silent.

Obviously.

It’s a tree.

But the philosophical implication is rather important.

Because we’re no longer restricting the possibility of divine participation to beings possessing sophisticated conceptual understanding.

Remember our earlier chapters?

Consciousness manifests through different forms.

Not every manifestation possesses the same faculties.

Not every subject has the same capacity for articulation and understanding.

But all manifestations are grounded in Śiva.

And the tradition’s claim that even stationary beings may be initiated follows from this much broader understanding of conscious manifestation.

Now, that isn’t biological evidence that plants possess the same subjective experiences as human beings.

Nor does the Sanskrit establish a modern scientific theory of plant consciousness.

But within Trika metaphysics, the eligibility of stationary beings for initiation underscores a radical claim:

The divine reality isn’t confined to intellectually sophisticated human subjects.

Now, that’s beautiful.

We’ve been studying classifications of subjects for several chapters.

Sakala.

Pralayākala.

Vijñānākala.

The divine mantra subjects.

And ultimately Śiva.

Here the scope of ritual grace extends to beings incapable of undertaking the normal practices themselves.

And then Abhinavagupta gives us one final visualisation.

Something involving wind.

Lightness.

And, strangely enough, weighing scales.

10. The disciple becomes light
Now the guru contemplates the disciple within:

Vāyu-pura.

The realm or city of wind.

And the disciple is imagined as:

Dodhūyamāna.

Shaken or stirred.

And:

Laghūbhūta.

Having become light.

Now here’s something rather interesting.

Laghu means light.

Not heavy.

Not burdened.

And the text even refers to lightness as observed through a balance.

Tulā.

Weighing scales.

Now, you might imagine the initiate literally becoming weightless.

And the historical ritual does include a striking claim concerning the disciple’s lightness.

But there’s no reliable basis for treating such a visualisation as a method of physically reducing someone’s mass or producing levitation.

What matters to our interpretation is that lightness becomes the ritual sign associated with the removal of a burdening condition.

The disciple is contemplated as no longer held in the previous configuration of bondage.

And in the chapter’s condensed presentation, this is the final visualisation.

Now think about the full sequence we’ve just encountered.

A dying individual.

The descent of Śakti.

The purification of the cosmic pathways.

Kālarātrī and the severing of vital bonds.

The intended departure of consciousness.

The complete oblation.

The alternative recitation of Brahmavidyā.

Then seedless initiation.

The fiery Hand of Śiva.

The destruction of latent generative power.

And finally the disciple imagined as light within the wind.

It’s quite an extraordinary progression.

But there’s one final Sanskrit verse.

And this time it is genuinely attested in the Devanāgarī witness.

11. The closing verse — Śiva performs the rite through the appropriate means
ATTESTED CLOSING STANZA — CHAPTER 15

मर्मकर्तनविधौ लघुभावे बीजभावविलये यदि मन्त्रः।तत्तथोचितपथेन नियुक्तस्तत्तदाशु कुरुते परमेशः॥
Marmakartanavidhau laghubhāve bījabhāvavilaye yadi mantraḥ.

Tattathocitapathena niyuktas tattad āśu kurute parameśaḥ.

Right.

Let’s break it down.

Marma-kartana-vidhau.

In the procedure concerning the cutting of the vital bonds.

Laghu-bhāve.

In the condition of lightness.

Bīja-bhāva-vilaye.

In the dissolution of the seed-condition.

Now:

Yadi mantraḥ.

If a mantra is employed.

And then:

Tathā-ucita-pathena niyuktaḥ.

Engaged or directed through the appropriate means.

And:

Tat tad āśu kurute parameśaḥ.

The Supreme Lord swiftly accomplishes the respective result.

Now, that’s a remarkable ending.

Because it brings us back to the relationship between mantra and divine power.

The mantra isn’t being presented as an autonomous little machine that operates independently of consciousness.

The ultimate agent is:

Parameśaḥ.

The Supreme Lord.

And when the relevant mantra is employed within the appropriate ritual framework, the traditional claim is that the desired result is accomplished through his power.

Now, remember Chapter Eight?

The potter.

The clay.

The wheel.

And the entire causal complex?

We established that the ultimate agency belongs to Parameśvara.

Well, here’s precisely the same idea applied to ritual.

The mantra.

The visualisation.

The guru.

The disciple.

The sacred powers.

The entire process.

All belong to the manifesting activity of consciousness.

And what finally produces the result?

Śiva’s own freedom.

Svātantrya.

Now, that’s the teaching.

And the conclusion is almost absurdly appropriate.

Because here we are, at the threshold of death.

The moment when the limited individual ordinarily appears most helpless.

The body is failing.

The senses are withdrawing.

The familiar world is coming to an end.

And Abhinavagupta’s response is to return to the one reality he has been discussing from the beginning.

The consciousness whose essential nature was never exhausted by the body.

The supreme freedom through which embodiment and its dissolution appear.

Paramaśiva.

And within this metaphysical framework, even the end of bodily life is not understood as something standing outside the freedom of Śiva.

End of Chapter Fifteen — Samutkramaṇa-dīkṣā and Nirbīja-dīkṣā
Now, think about how far we’ve come.

Back in Chapter One, Abhinavagupta distinguished two kinds of ignorance.

One belonging to the understanding.

Bauddha-ajñāna.

And another belonging to the contracted condition of individuality itself.

Pauruṣa-ajñāna.

He explained that correct knowledge removes the former and that initiation can address the deeper condition of bondage.

Well, we’ve now arrived at the most extreme application of that initiatory framework.

The final transition of embodied existence.

And the chapter offers different ritual possibilities for different recipients.

For the person receiving śaktipāta at the approach of death, a special initiation of departure.

For someone whose desired end involves a particular realm of experience, the corresponding joining rite.

For someone who can receive the Brahmavidyā, the possibility of a sacred formula awakening self-awareness.

And for someone unable to undertake the ordinary observances, the seedless initiation directed toward the removal of further karmic potential.

Different circumstances.

Different ritual operations.

But the same supreme reality.

Now here’s what I find especially striking.

Abhinavagupta never needs to introduce a second ultimate consciousness to explain what happens at death.

The individual consciousness isn’t a little object that must travel outside Śiva to find its destination.

The possibility of embodiment, departure, bondage and liberation all belong to the same unrestricted conscious reality.

That is the doctrinal argument.

And the deathbed rite is a historical expression of it.

The ritual’s claimed effects on death and postmortem liberation remain religious claims, not established medical or scientific facts. In actual end-of-life care, the relevant priorities are comfort, consent, non-harm and respect for the person’s wishes.

But as an account of Trika’s understanding of the relationship between consciousness and mortality, the chapter is remarkable.

You began thinking you were a little being inside the universe.

Then you learned that the entire universe is a manifestation of consciousness.

You investigated the body.

The breath.

The senses.

The mind.

The cosmic principles.

The powers of Śiva.

And now we’ve reached the very dissolution of the embodied condition.

And Abhinavagupta is still telling you the same thing:

The supreme reality isn’t somewhere else.

The body appears through it.

The body disappears through it.

But the essence of consciousness isn’t reduced to the body.

The apparent individual is Śiva manifesting under limitation.

And liberation is the overcoming of that limitation.

So the final joke is almost too good.

You’ve spent your entire life worrying about death.

And Abhinavagupta has spent fifteen chapters explaining that the ultimate reality you’ve been looking for is not a thing that begins and ends.

The whole bloody business is Śiva.
