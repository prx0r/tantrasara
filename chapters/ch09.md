The ground doesn’t have to be manufactured.

It becomes manifest.

Prakāśate.

It’s revealed as conceptual determination subsides.

And Abhinavagupta identifies this with careful attentiveness to the relation between the grasper and the grasped.

Grāhya-grāhaka-sambandhe sāvadhānatā.

Now that’s an absolutely magnificent instruction.

Because he’s not merely saying:

“Stop thinking.”

He’s asking you to investigate the relation between subject and object with extreme precision.

What is actually occurring when something is experienced?

How does the apparent object emerge?

How does the knowing subject emerge?

And what is the awareness through which their relation is manifest?

Now, as the differentiation of explicit idantā — this-ness — diminishes, the corresponding divisions disappear in pairs.

Two at a time.

Sixteen.

Fourteen.

Twelve.

Four.

Two.

Dvituṭikaḥ śivāveśaḥ.

Immersion in Śiva characterised by two tuṭis.

Now, don’t turn this into a challenge to suppress your breathing to an impossibly small movement.

This is an esoteric account of the reduction of differentiated manifestation, expressed through traditional measures of prāṇa.

And Abhinavagupta says that at this final stage, the two divisions have distinct characteristics.

The first is:

Sarvataḥ pūrṇā.

Full in every respect.

Completely replete.

Nothing lacking.

But the second is associated with the manifestation of omniscience and omnipotence through cultivation.

Sarvajñatva.

All-knowingness.

Sarvakartṛtva.

All-agency.

And why isn’t the first division described as producing those attainments?

Because the first is already full.

There’s nothing to acquire.

The second permits an expression of powers through differentiation.

Now that is remarkably elegant.

At the most fundamental level, fullness doesn’t require an accomplishment.

But when the divine power manifests through a differentiated mode, particular attainments can arise.

Fullness itself is not an achievement. Manifest powers belong to its expressive freedom.

And then Abhinavagupta invokes an earlier teacher.

One you really ought to know.

Kallaṭa.

The great early Spanda master.

And a particular expression:

Tuṭipāta.

The falling or descent of a tuṭi.

But then Abhinavagupta does something absolutely outrageous.

He connects the meaning of pāta with:

Śrīmatī Kālī.

The revered goddess Kālī.

And:

Mātṛsadbhāva.

The true being of the knowing subject.

And:

Bhairava.

And:

Pratibhā.

Immediate illuminating insight.

Now, imagine that.

We started by looking at a blue object.

We’ve passed through sixteen minute divisions of cognition.

Then reduced them through diminishing conceptual differentiation.

And now the very principle of that transition is being identified with Kālī and Bhairava.

The goddess of cosmic manifestation and withdrawal is also implicated in the most intimate transition within awareness.

Absolutely beautiful.

Alam rahasyārahasyanena.

Enough of disclosing the secret.

Oh, come on, mate.

You’ve just introduced the supreme goddess into a discussion of minute divisions of perception and now you’re calling it a day?

Apparently so.

But he doesn’t fully unpack the esoteric teaching here.

So we won’t pretend he has.

Right.

The states of consciousness.

The fourth.

12. Waking, dreaming and deep sleep aren’t simply things that happen to you
Right.

Ordinarily, you think of the states like this.

You’re awake.

Then you go to sleep.

Perhaps you dream.

Perhaps you don’t.

Then you wake up.

Very straightforward.

But Abhinavagupta says a state of consciousness is not simply a property of the object.

Nor simply a property of consciousness considered in isolation.

Nor two independent things standing side by side.

It’s the particular relation between the knowable and the awareness apprehending it.

Anyonyāpekṣa.

Mutual dependence.

Now, that’s a very important idea.

Because we’re not talking about a consciousness existing completely apart from all its manifestations, with states merely arriving from somewhere else.

We’re discussing the configurations through which experience becomes manifest.

Let’s begin with waking.

Jāgrat — waking
When the object appears as external, as something standing before the knower, we call that:

Jāgrat.

Waking.

The object appears outside.

The knower apprehends it.

The knowing process relates them.

Now consider the statement:

“There’s a flower over there.”

That’s a familiar waking configuration.

The object is apprehended in an externalised form.

Bahirūpatā.

But now suppose the form is no longer appearing as an externally established object.

Instead, it appears through ideation.

Svapna — dreaming
Saṅkalpa.

Mental construction.

Now the appearance is constituted differently.

Think about dreaming of a flower.

You can see it.

You can smell it.

You can even imagine picking it up.

But the appearance doesn’t possess the same relation to waking externality.

The object appears within a different configuration of awareness.

And that’s dreaming.

But now go further.

Suppose the differentiated appearance doesn’t manifest explicitly at all.

Instead, it remains in the condition of a seed.

Suṣupti — deep sleep
Bījātmatā.

The condition of being present in seed form.

The manifest object has subsided.

The differentiated appearance is not present in the ordinary way.

But the possibility of manifestation remains.

Now here’s something crucial.

Abhinavagupta isn’t simply equating deep sleep with the supreme condition of liberation.

The absence of differentiated experience is still a particular condition.

And it’s connected with the subject in whom those appearances may arise.

Now, he says each of these states can be considered in relation to the knowable, the means of knowledge and the knower.

And each has further subdivisions.

But there is something beyond the three.

Because we’ve still not arrived at the fundamental freedom of consciousness.

And that brings us to:

13. Turya — the fourth state
Turya.

Literally:

The fourth.

Now, in the familiar analysis, waking, dreaming and deep sleep are three modes of experience.

But Turya is not merely one more mode alongside them.

It’s the apprehension of the fullness of the knower.

Pramātuḥ pūrṇatā.

And Abhinavagupta gives a beautiful progression.

Ordinarily, you have three aspects.

The form.

Rūpam.

The seeing faculty.

Dṛśā.

And:

Ah am.

The I.

Or, properly joined:

Aham.

Now imagine the sentence:

“I see a form.”

You’ve got an object.

An instrument.

And a subject.

But in Turya, the separate conceptualisation of these aspects is transcended in the immediate apprehension:

Paśyāmi.

I see.

And here, the act of knowing isn’t grounded in a separately constructed instrument.

It’s an expression of immediate subjectivity.

Anupāyikā pramātṛtā.

Knowership without an intervening means.

And its essence is:

Svātantrya.

Freedom.

Now, don’t misunderstand this.

Abhinavagupta isn’t saying every ordinary use of the phrase “I see” is the fourth state.

That would be a remarkably easy path to liberation.

You could walk around announcing:

“I see!”

And be fully enlightened by lunchtime.

No.

He’s pointing toward the immediate conscious agency underlying the differentiated relation between seeing, seer and seen.

And this Turya has three aspects corresponding to its relation to the previous three states.

It bestows grace upon them.

Anugraha.

It illumines their underlying nature.

So the fourth doesn’t merely annihilate waking, dreaming and deep sleep.

It reveals the freedom through which they appear.

Now, there are different traditional names for these states.

And Abhinavagupta gives two sets.

The yogins call them:

Piṇḍastha.

Padastha.

Rūpastha.

Rūpātīta.

Those devoted to systematic contemplative discernment use:

Sarvatobhadra.

Vyāpti.

Mahāvyāpti.

Pracaya.

And he refers to the Tantrāloka and his metrical commentary for the detailed explanations.

So he’s explicitly giving us the terminology while reserving the fuller analysis for other works.

But there’s still one more state.

And this one’s particularly important.

14. Turyātīta — beyond even the fourth
Now we’re talking.

Turyātīta.

T urya — the fourth.

Atīta — gone beyond, transcended.

Properly joined:

Turyātīta.

Beyond the fourth.

And what is this?

The fullness that contains everything while transcending everything.

Sarvāntarbhūta.

Containing all.

Sarvātīta.

Beyond all.

Now, that should sound familiar.

Remember how we began the whole Tantrasāra?

Śiva is both immanent and transcendent.

Viśvamaya.

Of the nature of the universe.

Viśvottīrṇa.

Beyond the universe.

Well, here’s the same fundamental principle expressed through the analysis of states.

The supreme reality is not merely one state competing with other states.

It is the fullness in which all states arise.

And Abhinavagupta gives another name.

Mahāpracaya.

The great fullness or supreme accumulation.

Now, this is important.

Because if you imagine liberation as entering Turya and staying there forever, you’ve still potentially constructed an opposition between the fourth state and ordinary waking experience.

But Turyātīta is not defined through that exclusion.

It’s the all-inclusive fullness that transcends every limitation.

Not merely consciousness without the world, but consciousness whose freedom includes the entire world.

And this brings us back to the seven subjects.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to connect the five states of consciousness with the various levels of knowership.

15. Different subjects inhabit different kinds of experience
Now let’s examine the states again.

Waking.

Dreaming.

Deep sleep.

Turya.

Turyātīta.

And here’s an important qualification.

Abhinavagupta says the term waking can be used wherever an appearance is clear, stable and continuously connected.

Sphuṭa.

Clear.

Sthira.

Stable.

Anubandhin.

Connected or enduring.

Dreaming is the contrary condition, where that stable clarity isn’t present.

And then he associates the states with different levels of the knowing subject.

The experience of a Pralayākala corresponds to the dreaming condition.

That of a Vijñānākala to a condition of nonapprehension associated with deep sleep.

That of the Mantra class and the higher mantra lords to Turya, in which the knowable is brought into unity.

And finally:

Śiva.

The condition of non-difference between every existent thing and Śiva.

Bhāvānāṃ śivābhedaḥ.

That’s Turyātīta.

Now isn’t that interesting?

The same categories we’ve been analysing as kinds of subjectivity can also be understood through the configurations of conscious experience.

And Abhinavagupta then gives a series of correspondences between the different numerical divisions and the five states.

In the fifteenfold system, the own-nature of the object together with Sakala is associated with waking.

Pralayākala with dreaming.

Vijñānākala with deep sleep.

The group comprising Mantra, Mantreśvara and Mantramaheśvara with Turya.

And Śiva with Turyātīta.

But as we move upward through the thirteenfold, elevenfold, ninefold, sevenfold, fivefold and threefold arrangements, the specific allocations change.

Because the lower forms of knowing subjectivity have ceased to apply.

And even within the undivided Śiva-tattva, contemplative analysis can provisionally distinguish five aspects through:

Kriyā.

Action.

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Icchā.

Will.

Ānanda.

Bliss.

Cit.

Consciousness.

Now notice the beautiful circularity.

We began Chapter Eight with Śiva’s five powers.

Then followed those powers downward into increasingly differentiated manifestation.

Now, at the end of Chapter Nine, we’ve arrived back at those same five powers.

But we’ve understood that even their distinction is an analytical construction within the fullness of Śiva.

The same consciousness manifests the many, experiences the many, and recognises the many as itself.

संविद्विश्रान्तिवन्ध्यं स्फुरति स बहुधा मातृभावोऽस्य यस्मात्॥

Right.

One final teaching.

Among all the tattvas beginning with earth, there is no form whatsoever that fails to rest in the consciousness of the knower.

Not one.

Why?

Because the nature of the knower shines forth in manifold ways.

Bahudhā.

In multiplicity.

Now think about the entire chapter.

We began with seven different knowing subjects.

Then the seven powers through which those subjects apprehend their objects.

Then the fifteenfold structure of manifestation.

Then the progressively smaller sets of distinctions.

Then the emergence of cognition through the minute movements of prāṇa.

Then waking, dreaming, deep sleep, Turya and Turyātīta.

And now Abhinavagupta tells you:

Nothing in the entire network of reality is without repose in consciousness.

Not the earth.

Not the sky.

Not the gods.

Not the ordinary individual.

Not a single conceivable appearance.

And then comes the instruction.

Within this entire network of knowable things, establish yourselves in the state where conceptual succession is transcended.

The state whose essence is the apprehension of the absolute freedom of I-consciousness.

Ahantā-svātantrya.

Not the constructed ego from Chapter Eight.

Not the little identity that says:

“I’m this body.”

“I’m this personality.”

“I’m this particular creature.”

The unrestricted I-consciousness through which all these limited manifestations appear.

And then:

Prāpnuta svātmasattām.

Attain the true being of your own Self.

Now that’s a rather extraordinary ending.

Because we began by asking:

“How can seven different subjects experience the same universe differently?”

And the answer was:

Through the differentiation of Śakti.

The same supreme consciousness manifests multiple modes of knowership, multiple modes of cognition and multiple modes of objectivity.

But none of these divisions establishes an independent reality outside consciousness.

And as the distinctions subside, the underlying freedom becomes increasingly evident.

Until, at the highest level, no division remains.

Not seven.

Not fifteen.

Not even two.

Only the fullness of Śiva.

Now, here’s what I find particularly beautiful.

You don’t have to imagine that the entire universe disappears in liberation.

The objects can continue appearing.

The senses can continue functioning.

The mind can continue forming concepts.

But the fundamental understanding is no longer restricted to the opposition between a little subject and a separate world.

The universe is apprehended as the manifold expression of the one conscious Self.

And the seven kinds of knower?

Different expressions of that Self’s own powers.

So there you are.

Looking at a blue flower.

Something you’ve done a thousand times.

But according to Abhinavagupta, that apparently simple act involves the entire metaphysical structure of experience.

The object.

Its mode of manifestation.

The power of cognition.

The knowing subject.

The articulation of conceptual distinctions.

And the conscious freedom through which all of these arise.

You’ve spent your whole life thinking you’re a little bloke looking at things.

And Abhinavagupta has just spent an entire chapter explaining that the very possibility of looking at anything is an expression of Śiva’s own self-awareness.

Absolutely bloody magnificent.
