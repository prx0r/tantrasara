First:

Devotion to the guru.

And not merely polite appreciation.

Sarvātmanā bhakti.

Devotion with one’s whole being.

The same devotion extends to scripture and deity.

And the disciple is instructed to turn away from what opposes them.

Now, we’ve already discussed Abhinavagupta’s unusually high conception of the guru in Chapter Eleven.

So it’s no surprise that this appears first.

But here it becomes a formal religious commitment.

Second:

Honour the guru’s spiritual relations.

The guru’s son.

Those joined to the guru through the transmission of knowledge.

Previously initiated members of the tradition.

These are to be treated with appropriate respect.

And the text also refers to relationships of sexual or familial association, emphasising their relation to ritual service rather than merely personal gratification.

This is part of a historically specific tantric social structure.

It shouldn’t be read as giving a teacher or community unrestricted authority over another person’s intimate relationships.

Third:

Do not treat women with contempt on the specified grounds.

The Sanskrit includes a prescription concerning women and aversion, including reference to barrenness.

The point is that a disciple should not make a woman’s condition a cause for disgust or rejection.

Now, that’s worth noticing amid the more hierarchical rules that follow.

Fourth:

Don’t casually pronounce the deity’s name, the guru’s name or the mantra outside the proper context of worship.

The sacred names are governed by rules of ritual usage.

Not simply to be shouted about whenever you feel like it.

Fifth:

Don’t use the guru’s personal possessions, such as their bed.

Again, the formal separation between teacher and disciple is being maintained.

Sixth:

Don’t engage in casual worldly play or amusement in the guru’s presence.

This is about maintaining the appropriate religious decorum.

Seventh:

Don’t regard anyone else as superior to the guru.

Now, this is an explicitly hierarchical rule.

And it follows from the conception of the guru as the embodiment of the highest liberating knowledge.

It is not a reliable practical safeguard against a teacher’s misconduct; that requires independent judgment and accountability.

Eighth:

Honour the guru in rites such as ancestral offerings.

Śrāddha.

Ninth:

Avoid certain esoteric terms, including Śākinī, in specified occasional rites.

The ritual vocabulary itself is regulated.

Tenth:

Observe sacred festival days.

Parvan.

Now, here’s where the sectarian character of the historical text becomes extremely explicit.

Abhinavagupta instructs disciples not to associate religiously with Vaiṣṇavas and others whom his tradition classifies as holding inferior views.

And even if someone previously accepted such a person as a guru through curiosity about another scripture, the disciple is instructed to leave that relationship without treating themselves as superior.

Now, that’s a doctrinal boundary of this particular religious system.

It shouldn’t be confused with a universal requirement to avoid people of other faiths.

But it is present in the Sanskrit, and we’re not going to pretend Abhinavagupta was writing modern interfaith pluralism.

He wasn’t.

Now comes another fascinating instruction.

Do not judge members of the tradition by their previous birth-status or caste.

Pūrva-jāti-buddhi.

And within the sacred circle, do not construct distinctions between superior and inferior participants on the basis of birth.

Now, this is especially striking.

The text strongly asserts a hierarchy of religious doctrines and guru qualifications, yet also rejects the relevance of birth-based hierarchy within the consecrated tantric circle.

So the same chapter contains both strong sectarian boundaries and a repudiation of certain conventional social distinctions inside the initiated community.

That tension is part of the tradition’s actual historical structure.

Now, the guru’s community is to be honoured when its members come to the disciple’s home.

Other religious ascetics may be treated respectfully without mixing ritual observances.

And the disciple is instructed to abandon doubts that obstruct the religious commitment.

But here’s a particularly beautiful instruction.

Don’t regard any external shrine or pilgrimage place as superior to the body itself.

Śarīrāt ṛte na anyat āyatana-tīrthādikaṃ bahumānena paśyet.

Now consider that.

We’ve discussed sacred places.

Mountains.

Pīṭhas.

Temples.

The ritual room.

But now the initiated disciple is told that the body itself possesses profound sacred significance.

Because the body is not merely something separate from the divine.

It’s a support in which the divine powers can be installed and recognised.

And through the appropriate understanding, it becomes a sacred field.

Now, the final observance is particularly important.

Mantra-hṛdayam anavarataṃ smaret.

Remember the Heart of the mantra continuously.

Now, that’s beautiful.

Not merely the external sound.

Not simply the number of repetitions.

The Heart.

The living conscious reality expressed by the mantra.

And the disciple is instructed to maintain that remembrance.

Now, do you see the shape of the whole initiation?

The external ceremony prepares the disciple.

The divine powers are installed.

The mantra is entrusted.

The religious commitments establish a continuing discipline.

And the disciple is directed toward constant remembrance of the inner essence of the mantra.

That’s the actual transition.

Not merely receiving a certificate saying:

“Congratulations, you’re officially tantric now.”

But assuming a new ritual and contemplative relationship with the powers of Śiva.

And now the chapter comes to its final acts.

29. The disciple honours the guru and the community
Having heard the observances, the disciple bows.

Praṇamya.

And accepts them.

Abhyupagamya.

Then the guru is honoured with:

Dakṣiṇā.

The traditional gift or fee offered to the teacher.

And the wording here is unusually sweeping.

It refers to offerings extending even to wealth, wife and body.

Dhana-dāra-śarīra-paryantayā.

Now, that is a historically important but ethically troubling formulation.

It belongs to a ritual culture in which offering everything to the guru could be used to signify total surrender.

It is not a legitimate modern basis for transferring control of one’s spouse, body or personal autonomy to a religious teacher.

But faithfully describing the passage requires acknowledging the actual breadth of the prescription.

Then the disciple makes offerings to previously initiated members of the tradition.

And to:

Dīna.

The distressed or impoverished.

Anātha.

Those without support or protection.

Now isn’t that interesting?

The initiation doesn’t conclude exclusively with the guru.

The disciple is also instructed to provide for vulnerable people.

And then the divine circle of forms is satisfied through the prescribed procedure.

Mūrti-cakra-tarpaṇa.

Now, after all that, Abhinavagupta gives the actual result.

30. Congratulations. You’re now a samayī.
Itthaṃ samayī bhavati.

Thus one becomes a samayī.

And what does that entitle the disciple to do?

Four principal things.

Mantrābhyāsa.

Practise the mantra.

Nitya-pūjā.

Perform daily worship.

Śravaṇa.

Listen to the teachings.

Adhyayana.

Study the scriptures.

Now, this is an important limitation.

The initiation qualifies the disciple for these activities.

It doesn’t automatically qualify them to perform every advanced ritual independently.

For occasional or special rites, naimittika, the disciple must seek the guru’s assistance or authorisation.

So there are levels of qualification.

Remember Chapter Eleven?

Different degrees of initiation.

Different capacities.

Different kinds of guru.

Well, here’s the first formal stage.

The samayī.

And Abhinavagupta ends the chapter with a wonderfully concise sentence.

And now, consider what Abhinavagupta has accomplished.

He began with the condition of the Heart.

The place where divine immersion becomes possible.

Then he installed Śakti in the body.

In the ritual vessels.

In the sacred space.

He explained why the entire action of worship must be recognised as non-different from Śiva.

He transformed the directions into expressions of consciousness.

Then the body into a divine support.

Then the entire cosmic hierarchy into a trident and lotus.

Then defined worship as offering the whole universe into the fullness of awareness.

And after all that, he constructed the external ceremony.

The sacred ground.

Gaṇeśa.

The vessels.

The fire.

The mantra.

The offerings.

The blindfolded disciple.

The fiery right hand.

The lunar left hand.

The forty-eight consecrations.

And finally the observances by which the disciple enters the discipline of Trika.

Now, here’s the most important thing.

The outward ritual isn’t simply an elaborate performance designed to entertain the gods.

It’s an organised transformation of the entire field of experience.

The person performing the action.

The materials used in the action.

The recipient of the action.

The very space in which the action occurs.

All are consecrated as manifestations of Parameśvara.

And through that process, the ritual becomes a method of recognition.

Now think about how remarkable that is.

You began as a little individual who believed:

“I’m here.”

“Śiva is there.”

“I’m going to offer him something.”

But by the end of the ritual, every one of those apparently separate things has been recognised within one conscious reality.

The worshipper.

The deity.

The offering.

The instrument.

The act of worship.

All Śiva.

And the disciple is no longer simply an outsider admiring the tradition.

They’ve been brought into its consecrated field and entrusted with the discipline that sustains its practice.

So there you are.

You’ve spent eleven chapters studying metaphysics.

One chapter having a bath.

And now an entire chapter learning how to conduct divine worship and initiate somebody into the tradition.

And somehow, after all those mantras, flowers, vessels, fires, goddesses, directions, subtle centres and sacred obligations, you’ve arrived at precisely the same recognition with which we started.

There is nothing outside the freedom of consciousness.

The whole bloody ritual is Śiva recognising Śiva.

And that is the heart of pūjā.
