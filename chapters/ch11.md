# Tantrasāra — Chapter 11

Tantrasāra — Chapter 11
एकादशमाह्निकम् — Śaktipātaprakāśanam

The Descent of Grace: or, why Śiva doesn’t give a flying fuck about your spiritual loyalty points.

An original slang audiobook adaptation of Abhinavagupta’s eleventh chapter, following the Sanskrit in order. The arguments and technical terms come from the text; the dialogue, jokes and modern analogies are invented. The concluding verse is genuinely attested, with a textual qualification explained at the end.

Right then.

Ten chapters.

Ten absolutely enormous chapters.

We’ve examined consciousness, bliss, divine will, the Sanskrit alphabet, thirty-six tattvas, 118 worlds, innumerable cosmic eggs, and enough metaphysical classifications to give an entire university department a nervous breakdown.

And all of it was directed toward one thing.

Liberation.

Apavarga.

Freedom from the contracted condition of limited individuality.

Now, some people might become liberated directly through the knowledge I’ve already explained.

Others will require initiation.

Dīkṣā.

And since I’m going to explain initiation in the following chapters, there’s a rather important question we ought to settle first.

Who exactly is qualified to receive it?

Who gets initiated?

Who experiences genuine spiritual awakening?

Why does someone spend forty years studying the scriptures without recognising their own nature, while another hears a single sentence and suddenly everything becomes clear?

And what determines whether the descent of divine power occurs?

That descent is called:

Śaktipāta.

Śakti — power.

Pāta — falling, descending.

The descent of Power.

And I’m going to explain something quite radical.

Something that will upset an extraordinary number of religious people.

Śaktipāta is not a reward for good behaviour.

Not ultimately.

It’s not something you can earn by completing enough rituals, accumulating sufficient merit or convincing Śiva that you’ve been a very good boy.

Because if it were, the supreme freedom of consciousness wouldn’t be supreme freedom.

It would be subordinated to something else.

And that’s completely unacceptable.

Right.

Let’s investigate.

1. Surely knowledge is what causes grace?
Now, some philosophers propose the following explanation.

They say:

“Look, Abhinavagupta, this is all quite straightforward.”

Oh, is it?

“Yes. Saṃsāra arises from ignorance.”

Correct.

“And when ignorance disappears, knowledge arises.”

Very good.

“And that produces śaktipāta.”

Ah.

Hang on.

We’ve got a problem.

What produces the knowledge?

Because you’ve just told me that correct knowledge causes the descent of grace.

But why does correct knowledge arise in one person and not another?

“Well, perhaps karma produces it.”

Karma?

Really?

Let’s examine that.

If knowledge is produced by karma in the same way that pleasure and pain arise as karmic fruits, then you’ve made liberating knowledge into an ordinary karmic result.

Bhoga.

Experience.

And if someone is still enjoying karmic fruits, why should they suddenly become entitled to the supreme descent of grace?

You haven’t explained the distinction.

You’ve simply renamed an ordinary result of karma as liberation.

Now suppose you say:

“No, no. Īśvara causes the knowledge to arise.”

Right.

But then why did you need to say that knowledge causes grace?

You’ve made the explanation circular.

Knowledge arises through divine will.

Grace arises through knowledge.

But the relevant divine intervention has already happened.

You’re going round in circles.

And if you imagine that Īśvara chooses whom to favour because of some personal desire, you’ve introduced another problem.

Rāga.

Attachment.

A preference arising from incompleteness.

Are you seriously suggesting the supreme Lord is sitting there going:

“Hmm, I like this bloke.”

“Not that one.”

“This one’s got a lovely beard.”

“Give him enlightenment.”

Absolutely ridiculous.

You’ve attributed the limitations of an ordinary individual to the unrestricted Lord.

So no.

Simply saying that knowledge causes grace doesn’t explain the matter.

We need a deeper account.

And now comes a very popular theory.

2. The karma-balancing theory — spiritual accounting gone completely mad
Right.

Some people say that śaktipāta happens when two opposing karmas become equal.

Karma-sāmya.

The equilibrium of karma.

Imagine you’ve got two sets of karmic results.

One produces pleasure.

Another produces pain.

One is pulling you in one direction.

The other pulls you in the opposite direction.

And when they’re perfectly balanced, they cancel each other out.

Then grace descends.

Lovely theory.

Very neat.

Almost mathematical.

But here’s the problem.

Why should two opposite karmas cancel each other?

Suppose one karma produces its result on Monday.

And the other produces its result on Thursday.

Where’s the contradiction?

There isn’t one.

They simply produce their results at different times.

Krama.

Succession.

And if they can operate successively, there’s no need for mutual obstruction.

“Ah, but suppose they produce opposite results at exactly the same time!”

Fine.

Let’s grant that possibility.

What about all the other karmas?

Because you’ve got quite a collection.

Presumably your entire karmic history isn’t composed of precisely two opposing actions.

There are other karmas that aren’t in conflict.

Why wouldn’t those continue producing their fruits?

“Well, perhaps they’re temporarily inactive.”

Right.

All of them?

And what about the karma sustaining your present embodiment?

Because if no karma were continuing to sustain your birth and lifespan, shouldn’t the body simply fall away?

Now the opponent tries another distinction.

“Perhaps the karmas producing birth and lifespan remain active, while only those producing pleasure and pain are obstructed.”

Oh, very convenient.

And why exactly should grace depend upon that particular arrangement?

If divine power can descend while one set of karmas remains operative, why should it be frightened of the other set?

Abhinavagupta’s question is magnificent:

Tataḥ sā bhogapradāt kiṃ bibhiyāt?

Why should that Power be afraid of karma that gives experience?

Imagine Śakti approaching the individual.

“Right, I’m here to awaken you.”

Then looking at the karmic accounts.

“Oh dear. You’ve still got three units of pleasure and four units of pain outstanding.”

“Terribly sorry.”

“Come back once your balance reaches zero.”

Mate.

This is the supreme power of Śiva.

Not a bank clerk.

If grace depends upon karma being arranged just so, karma has become the governing authority over grace.

And that contradicts the very freedom we’re supposed to be explaining.

So karma-sāmya doesn’t solve the problem.

But there’s another theory.

And this one’s rather seductive.

3. Maybe your impurity has to ripen first?
Right.

Mala-paripāka.

The ripening of impurity.

Remember mala?

The fundamental contraction through which consciousness experiences itself as incomplete.

The concealment of fullness.

Now, some people propose that this impurity gradually matures.

And when it’s sufficiently ripe, it falls away.

Then grace descends.

Very nice.

Almost like spiritual fruit.

Your mala is a little green mango.

You meditate for a while.

Perform some rituals.

Become increasingly religious.

Eventually your mala turns golden.

And Śiva announces:

“Wonderful. This one’s ripe.”

But Abhinavagupta has a question.

What causes the ripening?

What exactly is its nature?

And why should it happen at one particular moment?

If you say karma causes it, we’re back to the previous problems.

If you say divine will causes it, then divine will is already the ultimate explanation.

And if you say it happens through religious practice, we have another difficulty.

Because people propose all sorts of things as causes of grace.

Vairāgya.

Dispassion.

Dharma-viśeṣa.

Particular religious virtue.

Viveka.

Discernment.

Sat-sevā.

Service to the good.

Sat-prāpti.

Association with or attainment of the good.

Deva-pūjā.

Worship of the deity.

And so on.

All lovely things.

But none explains why the supreme Lord must wait for these conditions before revealing his own nature.

Now, don’t misunderstand me.

I’m not saying that discernment, worship and devotion are useless.

We’ve spent several chapters explaining their functions.

But the question here is more fundamental.

Can any conditioned activity compel the supreme, unconditioned freedom of Śiva to manifest?

No.

Because then that activity would become more fundamental than Śiva’s freedom.

And if you try explaining the effectiveness of these practices through grace, then grace is the explanatory ground rather than the consequence.

So Abhinavagupta concludes that the dualistic explanations are inadequate.

Bhedavādinām sarvam asamañjasam.

The accounts proposed by the advocates of difference are incoherent.

Quite a strong verdict.

He doesn’t exactly mince his words, does he?

Right.

Now that we’ve demolished three explanations, we can introduce our own.

And it’s absolutely beautiful.

4. Śiva is playing hide-and-seek with himself
Here’s the central principle.

Svatantra-parameśa-advayavāda.

The nondual teaching of the absolutely free supreme Lord.

And within this teaching, the whole problem becomes intelligible.

How?

Because the very same consciousness that appears bound is the consciousness that appears liberated.

Remember Chapter One?

Śiva possesses unrestricted freedom.

Svātantrya.

Nothing outside him determines what he can or cannot manifest.

And through that freedom, he can conceal his own nature.

Svarūpācchādana-krīḍā.

The play of covering his essential nature.

Now that’s a magnificent compound.

Svarūpa — one’s own essential nature.

Ācchādana — covering, concealing.

Krīḍā — play.

The play of self-concealment.

And through this play, Parameśvara appears as:

Paśu.

The bound being.

Pudgala.

The individual entity.

Aṇu.

The contracted subject.

That’s you.

The little bloke.

Now, imagine the absurdity.

Infinite consciousness.

Nothing beyond it.

Nothing limiting it.

And through its own freedom, it appears as a particular individual who thinks:

“I’m this body.”

“I’m located here.”

“I exist at this time.”

“I know some things.”

“I don’t know others.”

And there’s no contradiction.

Because Śiva’s freedom includes the power to manifest differences of space, time and individual character.

Deśa.

Kāla.

Svarūpa-bheda.

Now here’s the crucial point.

The same freedom through which Śiva conceals himself can bring that concealment to an end.

Svarūpa-sthagana-vinivṛtti.

The cessation of the covering of one’s own nature.

And then:

Svarūpa-pratyāpatti.

The recovery or recognition of one’s true nature.

Now, that can happen in two ways.

Jhaṭiti.

Suddenly.

Or:

Krameṇa.

Gradually.

Just like that.

Or through a process.

And when the limited individual enters into that recovery, we say it has become a recipient of śaktipāta.

But here’s the thing.

Nothing outside Śiva has produced the awakening.

The bound condition arose through his freedom.

The cessation of that condition also arises through his freedom.

Therefore, the descent of power leading to recognition is:

Nirapekṣa.

Independent.

Not dependent upon something else.

Now, this isn’t arbitrary divine favour in the ordinary psychological sense.

We’re not proposing that Śiva has human preferences and occasionally chooses someone because he likes their personality.

We’re saying that the manifestation and withdrawal of limitation belong to his unrestricted freedom.

And that freedom isn’t governed by karma, merit or some other independent principle.

So when a person recognises their true nature, what has happened?

Śiva has ceased concealing himself in that particular mode of manifestation.

Absolutely magnificent.

But now there’s an objection.

And it’s a good one.

5. Why does grace descend upon THIS person and not THAT one?
Right.

Suppose one person experiences profound recognition.

Another doesn’t.

Why?

Why does śaktipāta occur in one individual rather than another?

Why now?

Why here?

Why that particular being?

And Abhinavagupta gives an absolutely devastating response.

Who exactly is this separate individual you’re asking about?

Because the question assumes there are independently existing individuals standing outside Śiva.

One of whom receives divine power.

And another of whom doesn’t.

But our entire philosophical account has established that individual subjectivity is itself a manifestation of supreme consciousness.

So the question:

“Why does Śiva awaken this separate person?”

Already assumes a division that isn’t ultimately real.

Abhinavagupta says:

Sa eva parameśvaras tathā bhāti.

That very Parameśvara appears in that way.

And then:

Ko’sau pumān nāma?

Who, in truth, is this person?

Now that’s absolutely extraordinary.

Because the objection assumes that Śiva has a collection of independent beings lined up in front of him.

And he’s picking favourites.

But the nondual account doesn’t begin with independent beings.

It begins with one consciousness freely manifesting diverse forms of subjectivity.

So one individual appears contracted.

Another appears awakened.

And the difference itself belongs to the manifestation of Śiva.

You might still ask why the manifestation takes this particular form rather than another.

But you cannot explain the ultimate freedom of consciousness by appealing to a more fundamental determining condition.

Because then it wouldn’t be ultimate freedom.

Svātantrya isn’t the final link in a causal chain. It’s the ground through which causal chains themselves appear.

Now.

We’ve established why grace cannot be reduced to karmic accounting.

But there’s a practical question.

Because clearly people don’t all awaken in the same way.

Some experience immediate recognition.

Some require a teacher.

Some require initiation.

Some spend years practising.

And some are still mostly interested in acquiring supernatural powers.

How do we explain these differences?

Well.

Abhinavagupta has another classification.

Of course he does.

Nine degrees of śaktipāta.

And this one’s particularly interesting.

6. The nine degrees of grace — Śiva’s awakening intensity settings
Right.

Here’s the basic classification.

There are three principal degrees.

Tīvra.

Intense.

Madhya.

Moderate.

Manda.

Mild.

And each of these is subdivided into three.

Superior.

Intermediate.

Inferior.

Three times three.

Nine.

Now, don’t imagine these are nine different Śaktis, each issued by some separate cosmic department.

We’re talking about variations in the intensity and effect of the descent.

And the distinctions matter because different intensities produce different relationships with knowledge, initiation, practice and liberation.

Let’s begin at the very top.

6.1. Tīvra-tīvra — the most intense descent
Utkṛṣṭa-tīvra.

The highest form of intense grace.

And what happens?

Dehapāta.

The falling away of the body.

And immediately:

Parameśatā.

The condition of the supreme Lord.

Now, that’s an extraordinary traditional claim.

Abhinavagupta is describing a descent so intense that ordinary embodied existence does not continue.

The person attains the supreme condition at the body’s dissolution.

He’s not prescribing a method of bringing about death, and this is certainly not something to attempt or induce through physical practice.

It’s a metaphysical description of an exceptionally intense form of grace.

Now imagine someone asks:

“So they don’t need initiation?”

No.

“Scriptures?”

No.

“Breathing techniques?”

No.

“An expensive retreat with a certificate at the end?”

Absolutely bloody not.

In this classification, the descent itself is decisive.

There is no remaining process through which the individual must gradually cultivate recognition.

The supreme condition is attained.

Right.

Next.

And this one’s fascinating.

6.2. Tīvra-madhya — the person who just KNOWS
Now we get the intermediate degree of intense śaktipāta.

Madhya-tīvra.

And here something remarkable happens.

Prātibha-jñāna.

Spontaneous, intuitive knowledge.

Let’s break that down.

Prātibha — arising from immediate illumination or intuitive insight.

Jñāna — knowledge.

Now, this person doesn’t require a scripture or an external teacher to produce the recognition.

Śāstra-ācārya-anapekṣin.

Independent of scripture and teacher.

They possess:

Svapratyaya.

Their own direct conviction or ascertainment.

Imagine a person who suddenly understands something with absolute clarity.

Not because someone has carefully explained every step.

Not because they’ve memorised a philosophical system.

But because the actual reality has become evident to them.

And within Abhinavagupta’s framework, such a person can become:

Prātibha-guru.

An intuitively awakened teacher.

Now, here’s the extraordinary thing.

They can bestow both:

Bhoga.

Experience or enjoyment.

And:

Apavarga.

Liberation.

Without requiring external consecration.

Bāhya-saṃskāra.

And they aren’t dependent upon the usual preliminary initiation classifications, such as samayī.

They’re not merely someone with a strong personal opinion about religion.

The defining claim is that genuine liberating knowledge has arisen spontaneously.

And that knowledge itself constitutes their qualification.

Now, Abhinavagupta introduces a subtle qualification.

Even among these spontaneous teachers, there are degrees.

Why?

Icchā-vaicitryāt.

Because of the diversity of divine will.

Some may consult scriptures.

Some may seek confirmation.

Saṃvāda.

Agreement or corroboration.

So someone possessing prātibha knowledge might still study a text or converse with another teacher.

But that doesn’t mean the scripture caused the awakening.

The text may confirm what has already been recognised.

And this produces different types of spontaneous teachers.

Some without external supports.

Nirbhitti.

Others with supports.

Sabhitti.

But in every case, Abhinavagupta says the spontaneous insight is the powerful element.

Pratibhāṃśo balīyān.

The element of immediate illumination predominates.

Now, here’s something rather amusing.

Imagine someone who’s genuinely attained this spontaneous knowledge.

Then another teacher comes along with an enormous ritual manual.

“Excuse me, you haven’t completed the necessary initiation sequence.”

“Right.”

“And you haven’t received the proper authorisation.”

“Okay.”

“And who exactly is your guru?”

The person already possesses the liberating insight.

Abhinavagupta’s point is that the authority of such insight cannot be reduced to external qualifications.

Now, don’t confuse that with saying anyone who declares themselves spiritually enlightened is automatically a prātibha guru.

The text’s criterion is actual liberating knowledge, not self-advertisement.

A rather important difference.

And now comes the third degree of intense grace.

6.3. Tīvra-manda — the person who desperately needs a genuine teacher
Manda-tīvra.

The lower degree of intense grace.

And here’s what happens.

Sadguru-viṣayā yiyāsā.

A desire to approach a genuine guru.

Now that’s interesting.

Because the immediate effect of the descent isn’t necessarily full recognition.

It’s an attraction toward the person capable of communicating that recognition.

The individual develops a genuine urge to find the appropriate teacher.

Not merely someone wearing orange robes.

Not merely someone who can sit in full lotus while maintaining a particularly impressive expression.

Someone possessing real knowledge of the supreme reality.

And Abhinavagupta makes an important distinction.

If the attraction is toward an unqualified teacher, he classifies it as concealment rather than genuine liberating grace.

Tirobhāva.

But if someone begins with an inadequate teacher and later comes to a genuine one, that later movement is attributed to śaktipāta.

So the movement toward a true teacher is itself a manifestation of grace.

Now, that leads us to a rather important question.

What exactly makes a guru genuine?

And Abhinavagupta is about to be exceptionally demanding.

But first, notice the structure.

Three degrees of intense grace.

At the highest, immediate attainment of the supreme condition.

At the middle, spontaneous knowledge.

At the lower, the urge to approach a genuine teacher.

Different manifestations.

Same ultimate divine freedom.

And Abhinavagupta gives a comparison.

Just as, in a dualistic view, liberated divine beings have no independent jurisdiction over cosmic creation and dissolution in the presence of beginningless Śiva, so too the authority of lesser instruction is subordinated to fully awakened knowledge.

In other words:

The authority of the realised is grounded in the knowledge, not in a collection of external signs.

Right.

Now let’s discuss the genuine guru.

Because apparently the requirements are quite severe.

7. What actually makes someone a Sadguru?
First:

Sadguru.

Sat — true, genuine.

Guru — teacher.

A genuine guru.

And Abhinavagupta defines this person as:

Samasta-etac-chāstra-tattva-jñāna-pūrṇaḥ.

Full of knowledge of all the principles taught in this scripture.

Now, that’s a proper requirement.

Not someone who has read three inspirational quotations on the internet.

Not someone who can make you feel rather relaxed by playing a singing bowl.

Not someone who’s discovered the word nonduality and started putting it on their business cards.

We’re talking about someone who possesses complete knowledge of the teachings.

And Abhinavagupta goes further.

Such a teacher is:

Sākṣād bhagavad-bhairava-bhaṭṭāraka eva.

The venerable Lord Bhairava himself, directly manifest.

Now, this isn’t merely a compliment about the teacher’s personality.

Within the Trika metaphysics, the genuine teacher embodies the liberating knowledge of Bhairava.

And that’s what gives the guru the capacity to liberate.

But here’s an important distinction.

A yogin might possess impressive attainments.

Siddhis.

They might perform extraordinary feats.

They might be capable of producing unusual experiences.

But that doesn’t automatically mean they possess complete liberating knowledge.

Abhinavagupta is explicit.

A yogin’s capacity to liberate depends upon cultivated knowledge.

Svabhyasta-jñāna.

And other forms of qualification are irrelevant to that function.

Like physical attractiveness.

Lāvaṇya.

Or good fortune.

Saubhāgya.

Now imagine someone introducing a guru.

“He’s incredibly handsome.”

Very nice.

“He’s got a magnificent voice.”

Lovely.

“He’s extremely charismatic.”

Wonderful.

“He’s got two million followers.”

Congratulations.

Does he possess the knowledge that liberates?

Because that’s the actual question.

And Abhinavagupta says that anyone lacking the relevant complete knowledge is not a sadguru in this highest sense.

Strong stuff.

And now something remarkable happens.

Because the genuine guru’s instruction can itself constitute initiation.

8. Initiation isn’t necessarily an enormous ceremony
Right.

Dīkṣā.

Initiation.

You might imagine an elaborate ritual.

A fire.

Mantras.

Offerings.

Special clothing.

Several priests.

Someone waving a lamp.

Perhaps an extremely serious man announcing that you’re now a member of a secret spiritual organisation.

Lovely.

But Abhinavagupta is about to explain something much more radical.

Because the initiation received from a genuine guru is characterised by:

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Jñāna-lakṣaṇā dīkṣā.

Initiation having knowledge as its essential characteristic.

And through this, liberation may occur immediately.

Sadya eva muktaḥ.

Liberated at once.

Even while living.

Jīvan api.

Now that’s important.

Because we’re not merely discussing some reward after death.

Recognition can occur within embodied existence.

And how can such an initiation be communicated?

Abhinavagupta gives several possibilities.

Through:

Avalokana.

A look.

A glance.

Kathana.

Speech.

A spoken statement.

Śāstra-sambodhana.

Instruction or awakening through scripture.

Caryā-darśana.

Witnessing the teacher’s conduct.

And:

Caru-dāna.

The giving of a ritual offering.

Now, consider the range.

A glance.

A word.

A teaching.

Observing someone’s behaviour.

A ritual act.

Different means.

But what makes them liberating within this account isn’t merely the outward form.

It’s the knowledge communicated through the genuine teacher.

Now imagine someone asking:

“Do I need a proper formal initiation?”

And the answer is:

“Perhaps. But the decisive question is whether the liberating recognition has occurred.”

That’s an extraordinary shift.

Because the outer method is not being mistaken for the result itself.

And Abhinavagupta also mentions another kind of initiation.

One involving the separation of the vital force from the body.

Prāṇa-viyojikā dīkṣā.

He says this is to be performed at the moment of death, and that he’ll explain it later.

Again, this is a historical tantric death-time rite, not a recommendation to induce bodily death or attempt dangerous breath manipulation.

Now.

We’ve discussed the intense degrees of grace.

But not everyone belongs to that category.

Some people receive initiation and still don’t possess firm recognition.

And here’s where the intermediate grades come in.

9. Madhya śaktipāta — you want liberation, but there’s still a bit of unfinished business
Right.

We move to the moderate descent.

Madhya.

And again, we’ve got three degrees.

Superior.

Intermediate.

Inferior.

Now, the defining issue here is the relationship between knowledge, desire for liberation, and continuing desire for experience.

Let’s begin.

9.1. Superior-moderate grace
Utkṛṣṭa-madhya.

Here the person receives initiation.

But even afterward, they don’t possess absolutely firm recognition of their own Śiva-nature.

Na tathā dṛḍha-pratipattiḥ.

Their understanding isn’t fully established.

Now, this is important.

Because initiation has occurred.

The person is genuinely oriented toward liberation.

But the recognition isn’t completely mature.

And Abhinavagupta says that understanding ripens gradually.

Pratipatti-paripāka-krama.

A process of maturation.

And at the end of the person’s embodied life, they become Śiva.

Dehānte śiva eva.

So the descent is genuine.

The direction is established.

But the full fruition is associated with the end of the body.

9.2. Intermediate-moderate grace
Madhya-madhya.

Now this person wants Śiva.

Śivatotsuka.

They genuinely desire liberation.

But there’s something else.

Bhoga-prepsu.

They also desire experience or enjoyment.

And remember, bhoga here can include extraordinary yogic attainments, not merely ordinary pleasures.

So the person is essentially saying:

“Yes, absolutely, I want liberation.”

“Wonderful.”

“But perhaps I could acquire a few extraordinary powers first?”

Ah.

There it is.

The spiritual equivalent of going to the supermarket for milk and returning with a television.

You’re still heading toward liberation.

But you also have an interest in what the path might provide along the way.

Now, Abhinavagupta says such a person receives knowledge through initiation.

Then, through yogic practice, they may enjoy the fruits of their attainments in this very body.

And at the end of that life?

Śiva.

So liberation is still the ultimate result.

But the person first experiences the desired enjoyments associated with practice.

9.3. Inferior-moderate grace
Nikṛṣṭa-madhya.

Now the desire for experience is stronger, or its fulfilment is more extended.

And the person may require another body in which to enjoy the relevant fruits.

Dehāntara.

Another embodiment.

Then, after those experiences have been fulfilled, they attain Śiva-hood.

Now look at the pattern.

The strongest intermediate grace leads toward liberation at the end of the present life through ripening knowledge.

The middle degree allows enjoyments through practice in the present body before liberation.

The lowest of the three involves another embodiment before Śiva-hood.

Different trajectories.

One ultimate result.

And here’s an important distinction.

The degree of grace is not simply a measure of how intensely emotional someone’s spiritual experience feels.

Abhinavagupta is classifying the relationship between recognition, remaining desires, initiation and the fruition of liberation.

So don’t go thinking:

“I cried for four hours during kīrtan, therefore I must have superior-moderate śaktipāta.”

That’s not what the text is measuring.

Now we’ve reached the mild degrees.

And these are particularly interesting.

Because here the desire for experience becomes predominant.

10. Manda śaktipāta — you want the powers more than the liberation
Right.

Manda.

Mild.

Here the person is mainly interested in:

Bhoga.

Experience.

Attainments.

Powers.

Enjoyments.

The fruits of mantra and yogic practice.

Now you might think:

“Well, if they mostly want supernatural powers, why are we calling this śaktipāta at all?”

Good question.

Because Abhinavagupta distinguishes the desire for ordinary worldly fruits from the pursuit of divine mantra and yoga that ultimately leads toward liberation.

Someone practising a supreme Śaiva mantra might initially want some particular attainment.

Perhaps influence.

Perhaps extraordinary knowledge.

Perhaps success in a particular undertaking.

But the mantra and its divine orientation may ultimately carry the practitioner toward mokṣa.

The path has liberation as its final culmination, even if the practitioner’s immediate desire is something else.

And that’s why Abhinavagupta includes these lower forms within śaktipāta.

Now, there are three degrees here as well.

Superior-mild.

Intermediate-mild.

Inferior-mild.

But unlike the earlier categories, the Tantrasāra doesn’t give a separate detailed biography of each of these three.

It says that they differ according to degrees of intensity.

Tāratamya.

So we’re not going to invent precise outcomes for them.

The text’s central criterion is clear.

When desire for bhoga predominates, the descent is mild; nevertheless, devotion to supreme Śaiva mantra and yoga can make liberation its eventual end.

And with that, the ninefold classification is complete.

The nine grades at a glance
Grade

Characteristic result

Intense–highest

Immediate divine attainment with the body’s falling away

Intense–middle

Spontaneous liberating insight

Intense–lower

Impelled toward a true guru

Moderate–highest

Knowledge ripens; Śiva-hood at death

Moderate–middle

Enjoyments in this life; Śiva-hood at death

Moderate–lower

Enjoyments in a later embodiment before liberation

Mild, three grades

Bhoga predominates; the supreme mantra-yoga path ultimately leads toward liberation

Now, consider something.

We’ve got nine grades.

But we’re not saying there are nine separate supreme realities.

The variation belongs to the way divine freedom manifests through particular individuals.

The endpoint of complete liberation is not divided.

And now Abhinavagupta introduces a rather controversial comparison.

Because he doesn’t consider every spiritual attainment to culminate in the same ultimate condition.

And here he becomes quite unapologetically Śaiva.

11. Why Abhinavagupta doesn’t think every spiritual path ends in the same place
Right.

Now, apparently we’re going to upset the Vaiṣṇavas.

Abhinavagupta says that the attainments associated with Vaiṣṇava and certain other religious traditions are more like the favour of a king.

Rājānugraha.

Royal patronage.

Now imagine a king grants you something.

A magnificent palace.

A title.

A great estate.

Wonderful.

But the gift doesn’t make you the supreme king himself.

You still occupy a particular position within the kingdom.

And that’s the analogy.

Abhinavagupta argues that certain forms of divine favour can bestow extraordinary spiritual attainments without necessarily culminating in the highest Śaiva liberation.

So he doesn’t analyse all of these traditions in detail here.

Because the particular śaktipāta he’s classifying has:

Mokṣa-paryantatva.

Liberation as its ultimate end.

Now, this is a sectarian theological claim.

Abhinavagupta is explaining reality from within the hierarchy of Trika scripture.

He’s not presenting a neutral comparative study in which every tradition is granted an equally authoritative account of its own liberation.

But he then makes a subtle qualification.

Śiva’s power is present everywhere.

Śiva-śakty-adhiṣṭhānaṃ tu sarvatra.

All these different manifestations still arise through Śiva’s power.

The difference is in the character of the power operating there.

And he invokes a threefold distinction.

Jyeṣṭhā.

The highest or eldest power.

Ghorā.

The formidable power.

Ghoratarā.

The still more formidable power.

The highest liberating orientation belongs to Jyeṣṭhā.

Other manifestations may involve powers that sustain restricted or differentiated experience.

Now, that’s interesting.

Because he’s not saying there are religious traditions completely outside Śiva.

There can’t be.

He’s saying that the same divine power operates through different degrees and kinds of manifestation.

Some conducive to the supreme recognition.

Others associated with lower attainments or continued limitation.

And then he makes an important observation.

A person doesn’t necessarily remain within one religious system forever.

They can move.

They can develop.

They can transcend earlier orientations.

Let’s investigate.

12. You might have to work your way through several traditions
Now imagine someone begins within a Vaiṣṇava tradition.

They practise.

Receive instruction.

Develop devotion.

Perhaps undergo initiation.

Then they move into another Śaiva system.

Then another.

And eventually become qualified for the highest teaching of the Ṣaḍardhaśāstra.

The Trika scriptural tradition.

Now, Abhinavagupta explicitly allows such a progression.

Someone may pass through the prescribed stages of spiritual initiation.

Samayī and the subsequent grades.

And through the five streams of Śaiva revelation.

Pañca-srotas.

Eventually they may reach the highest qualification.

Parama-adhikāritā.

The capacity to receive the supreme teaching.

But there’s another possibility.

Someone might skip stages.

Ullaṅghana-krama.

A sequence involving the crossing over of intermediate levels.

And someone else might not require a sequence at all.

Akrama.

Without progression.

Now, that’s rather beautiful.

Because even though Abhinavagupta possesses an elaborate hierarchy of practices and scriptures, he doesn’t insist that every person must follow the same predetermined route.

Some progress gradually.

Some leap across stages.

Some awaken without an intervening sequence.

And why?

Once again:

Icchā-vaicitrya.

The diversity of divine will.

Now, this leads to an important claim about gurus.

And it’s one that reveals just how seriously Abhinavagupta takes the hierarchy of knowledge.

13. A teacher of a lower system isn’t necessarily qualified to teach the higher
Right.

Suppose you’ve got someone who possesses complete knowledge of a particular religious tradition.

They’re a genuine authority within that system.

Excellent.

But do they therefore possess the knowledge required to teach the highest Trika revelations?

Not necessarily.

Abhinavagupta says that teachers established in lower scriptural systems aren’t automatically qualified even to view the maṇḍala of a higher system.

That is quite a severe hierarchy.

But here’s the reverse.

A teacher fully established in the highest teaching possesses the qualifications for the lower systems.

Because the higher understanding encompasses their principles.

And Abhinavagupta says the superior teacher can actually enliven the lower teachings.

Prāṇayati.

Breathes life into them.

Why?

Pūrṇatvāt.

Because of fullness.

Now that is an interesting expression.

The highest understanding isn’t merely another specialised subject sitting alongside the lower ones.

It comprehends their place within a more complete account of reality.

And the person possessing that understanding is:

Sarvādhikārī.

Qualified in every domain.

At least according to Abhinavagupta’s doctrinal hierarchy.

Now he gives this teacher several names.

Daiśika.

Spiritual instructor.

Guru.

Teacher.

Ācārya.

Authoritative preceptor.

Dīkṣaka.

Initiator.

Cumbaka.

A teacher associated with the power to draw or attract the disciple into the spiritual process.

But whatever title is used, the essential criterion remains:

Pūrṇa-jñāna.

Complete knowledge.

Now here’s the distinction I particularly want you to remember.

Because it’s incredibly important.

A yogin who possesses extraordinary powers is not necessarily qualified to bestow liberation.

Suppose someone can demonstrate a remarkable yogic attainment.

Fine.

If a disciple wants that particular result, and the yogin can immediately produce it through instruction, then that yogin may be an appropriate teacher for that purpose.

But if the goal is liberation?

Then the decisive qualification is knowledge.

Not extraordinary experiences.

Not powers.

Not charisma.

Jñāna.

And Abhinavagupta says the yogin becomes qualified to instruct in liberation insofar as they possess that liberating knowledge.

Right.

Now, here’s something particularly useful.

Because some people worry about having multiple teachers.

And Abhinavagupta explicitly discusses that.

14. Can you have more than one guru?
Yes.

A person seeking completeness of understanding may approach multiple teachers.

Bahūn api gurūn kuryāt.

They may have many gurus.

Now isn’t that interesting?

Because you might imagine that entering a tradition means you’re forever restricted to one particular teacher.

But Abhinavagupta permits multiple sources of instruction according to their differing degrees of knowledge.

One teacher may help you understand one aspect.

Another may possess deeper knowledge of a particular subject.

Another may lead you further.

And the student can honour them according to the distinctions in their knowledge.

Now, Abhinavagupta does introduce a very strong restriction.

He says abandoning a guru possessing complete knowledge requires expiation.

Prāyaścitta.

And then he anticipates an objection.

“Hang on, Abhinava.”

Go on.

“What if the guru stops teaching?”

Right.

“Or starts saying something contrary to what they previously taught?”

Ah.

“Surely you can leave?”

Abhinavagupta says no — provided we’re genuinely talking about a guru possessing complete knowledge.

His reasoning is that such a teacher is, by definition, free from attachment and similar limitations.

So if the teacher remains silent or acts in a way the disciple doesn’t understand, the explanation is attributed to some limitation or lack of readiness in the disciple.

The disciple should endeavour to continue attending upon the teacher.

Now, that’s what the Sanskrit actually says.

And it’s important not to soften it into a modern statement that Abhinavagupta simply recommends finding whichever teacher suits you best.

He doesn’t.

He takes the authority of a fully realised guru extremely seriously.

But notice the assumption upon which his argument depends.

The teacher must actually possess pūrṇa-jñāna and freedom from the relevant defects.

Merely claiming to be enlightened doesn’t establish that.

And this traditional prescription should not be treated as a reason to remain with a coercive, exploitative or abusive teacher.

The chapter supplies a metaphysical ideal of a perfectly realised guru, not a reliable practical test by which an ordinary student can establish another person’s infallibility.

Now, returning to Abhinavagupta’s argument:

The liberating teacher is qualified by complete knowledge.

The disciple approaches through genuine attraction produced by śaktipāta.

And the purpose of initiation is the communication of liberating recognition.

So we’ve come all the way back to the question with which we began.

What ultimately produces that descent?

Not karma.

Not a lucky balance of good and bad deeds.

Not the mechanical ripening of impurity.

It is:

Nirapekṣa śaktipāta.

The independent descent of divine power.

And Abhinavagupta states it again.

The descent leading to grace isn’t dependent upon karma or other determining constraints.

Now, you might think that’s the end of the chapter.

But there’s something else.

And it’s absolutely fundamental to understanding saṃsāra.

Because we’ve discussed grace.

What about concealment?

Why does Śiva remain hidden?

And how can someone who seems spiritually awakened behave like an idiot?

Ah.

Excellent.

That’s our next problem.

15. Tirobhāva — how Śiva hides himself again
Now we encounter one of the five cosmic acts.

Tirobhāva.

Concealment.

The obscuring of the true nature of consciousness.

Remember our previous discussion?

Śiva can freely conceal his own essential nature.

Svarūpācchādana.

And he can freely reveal it.

Well, Abhinavagupta now investigates what concealment looks like in practice.

And he says it can produce an experience characterised by profound suffering and delusion.

Gāḍha-duḥkha-moha.

Dense suffering and confusion.

Now, here’s something rather surprising.

Even an awakened person may behave like someone who is deluded.

“WHAT?”

Yes.

But listen carefully.

Abhinavagupta distinguishes the outward behaviour from the person’s inward orientation.

Someone who has genuinely awakened may nevertheless act in a foolish way.

Prabuddho’pi mūḍhavat ceṣṭate.

Even the awakened behaves like the deluded.

But inwardly, in the Heart, the person disapproves of that foolish conduct.

Hṛdayena mūḍhaceṣṭāṃ nindati.

Now consider that.

The external action and inward recognition are not necessarily identical.

Someone might act foolishly without having fundamentally lost the recognition of consciousness.

But Abhinavagupta then gives the opposite example.

And it’s even more interesting.

A deluded person may perform spiritual practices.

Mantra.

Worship.

Devotion.

Mantrārādhana.

They might perform all the outward activities associated with spiritual awakening.

But inwardly, they reject or condemn what those practices signify.

And because of that, the practices don’t produce the same liberating orientation.

Now imagine someone chanting mantras all day.

Beautiful pronunciation.

Perfect rhythm.

Very impressive.

But inwardly they’re thinking:

“This is all pointless.”

“There’s nothing here.”

“I don’t believe any of it.”

And their actions and inner convictions are working against one another.

Abhinavagupta says that this self-contradictory condition can deepen suffering and delusion.

The person becomes suspicious even of their own spiritual conduct.

And that anxiety draws them into:

Duḥkha-moha-paṅka.

The mud of suffering and confusion.

What an image.

Paṅka.

Mud.

Mire.

The person is sinking into the mire of their own contradictory condition.

Now, here’s the important distinction.

In the awakened person, foolish conduct may occur but is inwardly rejected.

In the deluded person, spiritual conduct may occur but is inwardly rejected.

And according to Abhinavagupta, these two situations have profoundly different implications.

Because in the first case, the fundamental orientation toward recognition remains.

In the second, the orientation toward concealment remains.

Now, there’s a major qualification.

He says:

Na tu utpanna-śaktipātasya tirobhāvo’sti.

For one in whom śaktipāta has arisen, there is no concealment of that fundamental kind.

Now that’s a powerful claim.

It means that, within this metaphysical system, once genuine liberating śaktipāta has arisen, the fundamental orientation it establishes isn’t simply annulled by a subsequent fluctuation in outward behaviour.

Now, don’t confuse that with saying that awakened people are incapable of causing harm.

Nor does it mean that their conduct should be exempt from moral scrutiny.

The passage concerns the metaphysical status of the descent and concealment of divine power.

But the question remains:

Why does concealment happen at all?

And here Abhinavagupta returns to his earlier argument.

16. Even concealment cannot ultimately be explained by karma
Now, Abhinavagupta acknowledges that concealment is associated with karma and the experience of suffering and delusion.

But he rejects the idea that karma is an independent ultimate cause determining Śiva’s freedom.

Because we’ve already established that karma cannot stand outside the supreme Lord and dictate when his power may operate.

So concealment too must ultimately be understood through:

Icchā-vaicitrya.

The diversity of divine will.

Now, Abhinavagupta introduces another subtle point.

The consequences of concealment can vary in duration.

Someone may experience the relevant suffering within their present body.

Another may undergo a different arrangement of karmic consequences.

And he refers to people who have both served and dishonoured religious obligations.

Such as the commitments associated with initiation.

The prescribed conduct.

Caryā.

The guru.

The deity.

Deva.

The sacred fire.

Agni.

And those who have previously belonged to the Śaiva teachings but subsequently abandoned them.

Now, again, this is a traditional tantric account of the consequences of particular religious actions.

Not a reason to conclude that anyone who leaves a religious group deserves suffering.

But it matters for faithfully understanding the chapter.

Abhinavagupta is discussing how the consequences of religious conduct and the overarching freedom of the Lord are related.

And here’s the really important thing.

Concealment isn’t necessarily permanent.

Someone who remains in the concealed condition may eventually experience śaktipāta.

During life.

Or, according to the tradition, through the compassion of relatives, a guru or other persons after death.

And this too depends upon the diversity of divine will.

Now, isn’t that remarkable?

Even after all the classifications of grace, concealment, teachers and initiation, Abhinavagupta refuses to establish an independently existing mechanism that confines Śiva’s freedom.

The supreme Lord can conceal.

The supreme Lord can reveal.

The supreme Lord can manifest different circumstances, different degrees of recognition and different paths toward liberation.

And finally, Abhinavagupta brings the whole discussion together through something we’ve encountered before.

The five divine acts.

17. The five acts of Śiva — the entire bloody business is happening in you
Right.

Let’s recall them.

Sṛṣṭi.

Manifestation.

Sthiti.

Maintenance.

Saṃhāra.

Withdrawal or dissolution.

Tirobhāva.

Concealment.

Anugraha.

Grace.

Five activities.

Pañcakṛtya.

Now, ordinarily you might imagine these as five enormous cosmic operations being performed somewhere outside yourself.

Śiva creates a universe.

Maintains it.

Destroys it.

Hides himself.

Then chooses to reveal himself to certain individuals.

Very dramatic.

But Abhinavagupta has spent eleven chapters explaining why that picture is incomplete.

Because the reality of the universe is consciousness itself.

And every apparently limited individual is a manifestation of that same reality.

So now the practitioner is instructed to recognise the five divine activities within their own Self.

Svātmany anusandadhat.

Contemplating them in one’s own nature.

Now think about what happens in a single experience.

An object appears.

Sṛṣṭi.

It remains present.

Sthiti.

It disappears.

Saṃhāra.

Its appearance as something independent of consciousness expresses concealment.

Tirobhāva.

And the recognition that the entire process is a manifestation of one’s own conscious nature is grace.

Anugraha.

Now, that’s an interpretive illustration of the five acts, rather than a literal five-step exercise prescribed in this chapter.

But it gets at the philosophical significance.

The divine powers aren’t merely happening somewhere out in the cosmos. They are intrinsic to the freedom of consciousness itself.

And then comes Abhinavagupta’s final instruction.

It’s one of the most beautiful in the chapter.

He says, in essence:

Recognising the five divine activities within your own Self, understand yourself as Parameśvara.

And:

Na khaṇḍitam ātmānaṃ paśyet.

Do not see yourself as fragmented.

Now that is absolutely magnificent.

Khaṇḍita.

Broken into pieces.

Divided.

Fragmented.

And the instruction is not to apprehend your own nature as something divided into independent parts.

Not the little individual over here.

The supreme Lord somewhere else.

Grace over there.

Karma over here.

Ignorance in one compartment.

Liberation in another.

All mysteriously operating as though they belonged to entirely independent realities.

No.

The same consciousness freely manifests its powers.

And you’re to recognise that consciousness as your own essential nature.

Now, think about the whole argument.

You began by asking:

“Why does grace happen to one person and not another?”

And Abhinavagupta has gradually dismantled the assumptions underlying the question.

The individual isn’t an independently existing substance outside Śiva.

Karma isn’t an authority standing above Śiva.

Impurity isn’t some foreign power capable of limiting the absolute.

And grace isn’t a commodity distributed according to a cosmic accounting system.

The contracted individual is Śiva appearing through self-concealment.

The awakened individual is Śiva appearing through self-revelation.

And both are expressions of the same unrestricted freedom.

Svātantrya.

Now, after all that, Abhinavagupta gives a final verse.

And this time it’s genuinely attested.

18. The concluding verse — Śiva can hide, reveal, and recognise himself again
ATTESTED SANSKRIT — CHAPTER ELEVEN

यथा निरर्गलस्वात्मस्वातन्त्र्यात्परमेश्वरः।

आच्छादयेन्निजं धाम तथा विवृणुयादपि॥

अप्रबुद्धेऽपि वा धाम्नि स्वस्मिन्बुद्धवदाचरेत्।

भूयो बुद्ध्येत वा सोऽयं शक्तिपातोऽनपेक्षकः॥

This closing passage is attested in the Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism edition of Chapter 11. The GRETIL transcription ends after the preceding prose and omits these verses.

Now let’s translate the actual meaning before we get carried away.

Yathā nirargala-svātma-svātantryāt parameśvaraḥ.

Just as the supreme Lord, through the unrestricted freedom of his own Self…

Nirargala.

Without obstruction.

Without restraint.

Absolutely unrestricted.

Ācchādayen nijaṃ dhāma tathā vivṛṇuyād api.

…may conceal his own radiance, so too may he reveal it.

Now that’s magnificent.

The same freedom.

Concealment.

Revelation.

No second power required.

Then:

Aprabuddhe’pi vā dhāmni svasmin buddhavad ācaret.

Even when his own radiance is unawakened or unrecognised, he may act as though awakened.

And finally:

Bhūyo buddhyeta vā so’yaṃ śaktipāto’napekṣakaḥ.

Or he may awaken again.

And this descent of power is independent.

Anapekṣaka.

Not dependent upon something else.

Now, here’s my slang rendering.

Right.

The supreme Lord is absolutely free.

No restrictions.

No external authority.

No cosmic manager telling him what he’s allowed to manifest.

And through that freedom, he can conceal his own radiance.

He can appear as a limited individual.

Someone completely convinced they’re separate from everything else.

Someone who experiences themselves as incomplete.

Someone who doesn’t recognise the consciousness through which they’re experiencing anything at all.

And then?

He can reveal himself.

Just like that.

Or gradually.

He can manifest someone who acts as though awakened even when recognition isn’t fully present.

Or he can awaken again.

And none of this requires some external mechanism standing above his freedom.

Because the descent of power isn’t fundamentally dependent upon karma.

Not upon merit.

Not upon the ripening of impurity.

Not upon an accumulation of religious achievements.

It’s the supreme freedom of consciousness expressing itself.

Śiva hides himself.

Śiva reveals himself.

Śiva recognises himself.

And the whole bloody operation is Śiva.

End of Chapter Eleven — Śaktipātaprakāśanam.

Now consider how remarkable the conclusion is.

At the start, we wanted a causal explanation of grace.

Some specific reason.

A mechanism.

A rule.

Something that would let us point at a particular person and say:

“Ah, yes. This is why that person became liberated.”

But Abhinavagupta has insisted that such explanations, when treated as ultimate, compromise the unrestricted freedom of Śiva.

Karma can explain karmic experience within manifestation.

Religious practice can prepare understanding.

A genuine teacher can communicate recognition.

Initiation can provide a means of liberation.

But none of these stands outside supreme consciousness as an independently governing power.

And the highest realisation isn’t the acquisition of something that was previously absent.

It’s the recovery of one’s own nature.

Svarūpa-pratyāpatti.

Now here’s the most extraordinary part.

Even the question:

“Why hasn’t Śiva revealed himself to me?”

Is being asked within the very consciousness whose nature is Śiva.

And the apparent seeker, the question, the process of investigation and the possibility of recognition all arise within that one reality.

You might spend years studying Sanskrit.

Learning mantras.

Contemplating the tattvas.

Investigating the meaning of svātantrya.

And through that process, recognition may become clearer.

But Abhinavagupta’s ultimate point is that no quantity of practice forces Paramaśiva to become what he already is.

The freedom to conceal and the freedom to reveal belong to the same consciousness.

And that consciousness is not something separate from the very awareness through which you’re hearing this.

Absolute bloody magnificence.
