# Śiva Sūtras 2.2

प्रयत्नः साधकः

Prayatnaḥ sādhakaḥ.

Effort is effective in accomplishment.

Stop Trying So Hard, but Also Don’t Stop Trying.

Right.

Now we’re discussing effort.

Which is already slightly concerning.

Because every spiritual tradition seems to alternate between two completely contradictory instructions.

“Make an enormous effort.”

“Stop making an effort.”

“Practise constantly.”

“There’s nothing to attain.”

“Apply yourself.”

“Let go.”

And the poor practitioner is sitting in the middle, thinking:

CAN SOMEBODY PLEASE TELL ME WHAT I’M SUPPOSED TO BE DOING WITH MY EYEBROWS?

Fortunately, Vasugupta is talking about a rather specific kind of effort.

Prayatna — effort, exertion, endeavour.

Sādhaka — that which accomplishes, or the effective means of attainment. The word also commonly denotes a practitioner, but here Kṣemarāja emphasises the efficacy of the effort.

Now, when Kṣemarāja explains this, he doesn’t simply recommend forcing the mind into submission through sheer muscular determination.

He describes an effort rooted in the spontaneous arising of the desire to contemplate the mantra’s true nature.

This is extraordinarily subtle.

Because we’re dealing with the relationship between icchā, jñāna and kriyā.

Will.

Knowledge.

Action.

Imagine you’re contemplating Śiva.

Perhaps repeating a mantra.

Perhaps reflecting on the nature of awareness.

Suddenly there’s a genuine movement towards recognition.

Not merely the thought, “I should meditate because my schedule says so.”

But a living impulse towards the source of awareness.

An interest so immediate that the mind naturally turns towards it.

Kṣemarāja says the practitioner must firmly seize this arising.

And he illustrates the process with imagery taken from the Tantrasadbhāva.

A bird sees food and moves towards it with spontaneous intensity.

An arrow, released from its bow, travels towards its target.

The point isn’t to strain continuously.

It’s that a particular, concentrated movement of attention can arise with remarkable naturalness.

And the practitioner follows it.

This is the difference between distracted, mechanical effort and living contemplative engagement.

Consider trying to remember somebody’s name.

You sit there.

“What’s his name?”

“Starts with an M.”

“No, perhaps a B.”

You force it.

Nothing.

Then something shifts.

A connection appears.

Suddenly the name becomes obvious.

The breakthrough isn’t merely the product of pushing harder.

But neither is it completely unrelated to the preceding attention.

There was a directed engagement, and then an opening.

That’s only an analogy, not an equation between memory recall and divine recognition.

But it captures something about the interplay between effort and spontaneous illumination.

Kṣemarāja is particularly interested in directing the mind towards bindu, here understood as the undivided light of consciousness.

The effective effort does not consist in collecting more mental objects.

It moves towards the conscious source.

And the ultimate result is identification with the deity of the mantra.

The practitioner, the contemplation, and its supreme object cease to be apprehended as fundamentally separate.

So the advice is not:

“Just relax, mate, you’re already Śiva.”

Nor is it:

“Clench every spiritual muscle until enlightenment pops out.”

It’s something closer to:

When the movement towards recognition arises, enter it wholeheartedly.

Don’t get distracted.

Don’t substitute a theory of recognition for recognition itself.

Don’t spend the entire meditation writing an imaginary academic paper about what the meditation would mean if you were actually doing it.

The Sanskrit is prayatna.

Not procrastinationa.

Which, regrettably, is not an attested tantric category.

Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.
