# Śiva Sūtras 3.20

त्रिषु चतुर्थं तैलवदासेच्यम्

Triṣu caturthaṃ tailavad āsecyam.

The fourth should be poured into the three like oil.

Pour Turya Over Your Entire Life Like Divine Olive Oil.

Ladies and gentlemen.

This might be one of the most beautiful instructions in the entire Śiva Sūtras.

And also one of the funniest when translated literally.

Because Vasugupta has apparently decided that supreme consciousness is a substance with which the other states should be thoroughly basted.

Tailavat.

Like oil.

Wonderful.

Let’s unpack it.

Triṣu — into the three.

Caturtham — the fourth.

Tailavat — like oil.

Āsecyam — should be poured, sprinkled, infused.

Now, what are the three?

Waking.

Dreaming.

Deep sleep.

Jāgrat. Svapna. Suṣupti.

And what’s the fourth?

Turya.

The underlying nondual consciousness.

We’ve encountered this several times.

But now the instruction becomes exceptionally concrete.

The fourth is to permeate the other three.

Not merely appear during meditation.

Not merely be recognised at the beginning of a dream.

Not merely become momentarily apparent when the mind falls silent.

It must become established throughout the changing conditions of experience.

And Kṣemarāja explains this with extraordinary precision.

He says that oil gradually spreads through and permeates what receives it.

In the same way, the bliss of turya is to become increasingly pervasive throughout the three states.

Now imagine pouring oil onto cloth.

At first, it touches one small region.

Then it spreads.

It penetrates the fibres.

Gradually, a larger portion becomes saturated.

The oil doesn’t have to destroy the cloth to permeate it.

The cloth remains.

But its condition changes.

And that’s the analogy.

The waking state doesn’t necessarily disappear.

Dreaming doesn’t have to be abolished.

Deep sleep doesn’t have to be replaced by some permanently excited mental condition.

Instead, the underlying conscious reality is recognised throughout their changing forms.

And now comes a particularly subtle distinction.

Kṣemarāja explicitly contrasts this with the teaching of Book I.

Remember:

जाग्रत्स्वप्नसुषुप्तभेदे तुर्याभोगसम्भवः।

Jāgratsvapnasuṣuptabhede turyābhogasambhavaḥ.

The enjoyment of turya is possible through the distinctions of waking, dreaming and deep sleep.

That was 1.7.

And then:

त्रितयभोक्ता वीरेशः।

Tritayabhoktā vīreśaḥ.

The experiencer of the three is the lord of the heroes.

That was 1.11.

But the method described in Śāmbhavopāya concerns a more immediate, powerful recognition.

Here in Āṇavopāya, Kṣemarāja describes a gradual process.

The practitioner begins with moments of recognition.

Perhaps at the transition between mental states.

Perhaps when thought subsides.

Perhaps during deep absorption.

But those moments are not yet sufficient.

The recognition must extend into the intervening experience.

The ordinary movement of awareness.

The countless activities of waking life.

And this is where the metaphor of oil becomes magnificent.

You’re not trying to isolate one sacred moment from all the other moments.

You’re allowing recognition to pervade the entire structure of experience.

Now think about what that means in practice.

You sit in meditation.

There’s a moment of extraordinary clarity.

The usual sense of separate selfhood relaxes.

You recognise awareness as the ground of experience.

Beautiful.

Then you get up.

You walk outside.

Somebody drops a plate.

A dog starts barking.

A motorbike drives past.

And suddenly:

“FOR FUCK’S SAKE, CAN EVERYONE PLEASE BE QUIET, I’M TRYING TO BE ŚIVA.”

Well.

The oil hasn’t spread very far, has it?

The recognition has remained confined to one particular circumstance.

The task is to discover the same fundamental reality amidst the changing contents of experience.

And here’s an important correction.

The sūtra isn’t instructing the practitioner to remain physically awake throughout deep sleep.

It’s describing the pervasion of the three states by the recognition of turya within the tradition’s contemplative framework.

It is not a recommendation to deprive yourself of sleep or strain to maintain ordinary alertness while unconscious.

The metaphysical issue is the continuity of consciousness, not the elimination of physiological sleep.

And now Kṣemarāja introduces a crucial detail.

He refers to the initial and final boundaries of the three states.

The moments of emergence.

The moments of subsidence.

But says the practitioner should also permeate their middle.

The intervening condition.

Why?

Because it’s relatively easy, within this contemplative framework, to value the special transition.

The moment of silence.

The moment before a thought.

The moment of waking.

The extraordinary gap.

But what about the rest of experience?

What about the ongoing activity?

What about the entire middle of your life?

That’s where the recognition must become established.

Kṣemarāja explains this as an Āṇavopāya-specific form of sustained attentiveness, distinguishing it from the more immediate attainment discussed in the first section. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And this is why the metaphor is so extraordinary.

The point isn’t to acquire a fourth experience and then abandon the other three.

It’s to recognise the fourth as fundamental throughout them.

The cloth remains.

The oil permeates it.

And gradually the whole thing becomes saturated.

Which, if you think about it, is an absolutely beautiful image for spiritual integration.

Not escaping the world.

Not rejecting experience.

Not constructing a separate sacred compartment.

But recognising the conscious reality through every condition of life.

And now Vasugupta gives the method.

How do you actually enter this fourth condition?

Well, apparently you have to plunge into it with your mind.

Then relinquish the very mental operations you used to approach it.

Which is a perfectly normal sort of paradox for Kashmir.
