# Śiva Sūtras 2.10

विद्यासंहारे तदुत्थस्वप्नदर्शनम्

Vidyāsaṃhāre tadutthasvapnadarśanam.

When pure knowledge subsides, the dreamlike appearances arising from its fading become visible again.

You Realise You’re Śiva, Then Immediately Forget Again Like an Absolute Idiot.

Right.

This final sūtra is absolutely devastating.

And honestly, perhaps the most relatable statement in the entire second book.

Because after all this extraordinary metaphysics, after the divine mantra, after the awakening of Mātṛkā, after the sacrifice of the body, after the devouring of differentiated knowledge—

Vasugupta says:

You might forget.

That’s it.

You’ve recognised the truth.

You’ve understood the fundamental nature of consciousness.

You’ve experienced the dissolution of limited identification.

You’ve seen the world as the manifestation of Śakti.

And then…

Someone sends you an annoying message.

Suddenly you’re back.

“I cannot BELIEVE this person.”

“How dare they.”

“Do they not appreciate who they’re dealing with?”

And just like that, the individual identity has returned to centre stage.

The cosmic drama has resumed.

Śiva has once again become a bloke arguing with his phone.

Now, obviously, this is a modern psychological illustration.

Kṣemarāja’s actual discussion is more technical.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Vidyā — knowledge, here pure liberating knowledge.

Saṃhāra — withdrawal, subsidence, disappearance.

Taduttha — arising from that, or resulting from it.

Svapna — dream.

Darśana — seeing, appearance.

Kṣemarāja describes what happens when the practitioner’s attention to pure knowledge weakens.

The awareness of supreme reality is no longer maintained.

The impressions of that awareness gradually fade.

And the differentiated mental world reasserts itself.

The svapna here isn’t necessarily a literal dream during sleep.

It is the dreamlike manifestation of limited, differentiated cognition.

The world once again appears in terms of subject and object, possession and loss, attraction and aversion.

And the yogi can become caught up in those distinctions.

Now here’s something very important.

Kṣemarāja is not saying Śiva has literally stopped existing.

Or that universal consciousness has somehow disappeared.

That would contradict the basic doctrine.

Rather, the recognition of pure consciousness has subsided for the practitioner.

The underlying reality has not changed.

The mode of awareness has changed.

This is the heart of the issue.

You see clearly.

Then you become distracted.

Then you remember.

Then you become absorbed in something else.

Then you recognise again.

A continual alternation of recognition and contraction.

And Kṣemarāja is concerned with what happens when awareness lacks the steadiness necessary for sustained recognition.

He invokes the Mālinīvijayatantra and Spandakārikā to explain that even a genuine opening can be followed by renewed involvement in differentiated experience.

The latent impressions of recognition may remain, but attention is no longer established in their source. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And this is why the sūtra is a warning.

Not because the world is inherently evil.

Not because ordinary experience is something to hate.

But because the yogi may become identified once more with the limitations appearing within experience.

Consider what happens when someone understands a profound philosophical truth.

For a moment, everything is clear.

You see how all the elements fit together.

You understand the problem.

You feel an extraordinary freedom from the confusion that previously occupied you.

And then, a few hours later, you find yourself behaving as though none of that understanding had ever happened.

The knowledge was real at the level at which it occurred.

But it had not become stable.

This is a useful analogy for what Kṣemarāja is discussing, though the actual sūtra concerns a much more profound yogic recognition than an intellectual insight.

The important point is stabilisation.

Not merely having recognised.

But remaining established in recognition.

And here Kṣemarāja provides the transition to the next section.

If the practitioner cannot maintain the attentive recognition characteristic of Śāktopāya, another form of practice becomes appropriate.

One employing more concrete supports.

More articulated procedures.

More deliberate engagement with the faculties of the limited individual.

And thus, the third book emerges.

Āṇavopāya.

The way of the aṇu.

The limited individual.

Where the teaching begins working with the very structures through which consciousness experiences itself as finite.

Which is a rather beautiful conclusion.

Because even when the practitioner becomes distracted from the highest recognition, the tradition doesn’t simply abandon them.

There remains another way.

The capacity for practice belongs to the same Śakti.

The limited individual has not fallen outside consciousness.

They have merely returned to a contracted mode of awareness.

And that contracted mode can itself become the starting point for liberation.

The same Śiva.

The same Śakti.

Another method.

Another opportunity.

Another chance for consciousness to recognise what it never ceased to be.

The Great Reveal — What Was Śāktopāya Actually Teaching?
Let’s revisit the extraordinary progression of these ten statements.

In the first book, Vasugupta established the supreme nature of consciousness and described the immediate recognition characteristic of Śāmbhavopāya.

But Śāktopāya provides a more articulated account of how mental activity itself can participate in liberation.

The teaching is not:

“Stop thinking and become Śiva.”

Nor is it:

“Think hard enough and manufacture Śiva.”

It’s something rather subtler.

Recognise the conscious power from which thought arises, and through which its differentiation becomes manifest.

Consider the structure.

2.1 — Cittaṃ mantraḥ.


The mind becomes mantra through contemplation of its supreme conscious source.

2.2 — Prayatnaḥ sādhakaḥ.


Spontaneous, directed effort makes this contemplation effective.

2.3 — Vidyāśarīrasattā mantrarahasyam.


The hidden reality of mantra is the living power of nondual consciousness.

2.4 — Garbhe cittavikāso ’viśiṣṭavidyāsvapnaḥ.


The practitioner must not confuse limited accomplishments and visionary phenomena with supreme knowledge.

2.5 — Vidyāsamutthāne svābhāvike khecarī śivāvasthā.


When supreme knowledge arises spontaneously, Khecarī, the state of Śiva, is revealed.

2.6 — Gurur upāyaḥ.


The guru, as genuine instruction and the vehicle of divine grace, is a means of recognition.

2.7 — Mātṛkācakrasambodhaḥ.


The practitioner awakens to the divine nature of the entire wheel of phonemic powers.

2.8 — Śarīraṃ haviḥ.


Limited bodily identification becomes an offering into the fire of consciousness.

2.9 — Jñānam annam.


Differentiated knowledge is consumed within nondual recognition.

2.10 — Vidyāsaṃhāre tadutthasvapnadarśanam.


When that recognition subsides, dreamlike differentiation returns, providing the reason for further practice.

And there’s an absolutely beautiful symmetry here.

The first section began:

चैतन्यमात्मा।

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

The second section began:

चित्तं मन्त्रः।.

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

And the third section will begin:

आत्मा चित्तम्।

Ātmā cittam.

The individual self is mind.

Three different points of departure.

Three distinct approaches to the problem of recognition.

The supreme standpoint of Śiva.

The transformative power of Śakti.

And the limited standpoint of the individual.

All directed towards the recognition of the one conscious reality.

The Final Teaching — You Cannot Escape Śakti
Here’s the extraordinary thing about Śāktopāya.

Everything that appears to separate you from your true nature is itself an expression of the power of that nature.

Thought.

Language.

Desire.

Mental activity.

Differentiation.

The sense of being an individual.

All belong, in the final analysis, to the manifestation of Śakti.

And therefore, the path to recognition doesn’t always require rejecting them.

It can involve understanding them so completely that their conscious source becomes apparent.

The same language that articulates limitation can become mantra.

The same mental activity that constructs difference can become contemplative recognition.

The same Mātṛkā that structures differentiated knowledge can disclose the universal I-consciousness.

The same body that appears to define your individuality can become an offering into its conscious source.

The same knowledge that constitutes bondage can become food for liberation.

And when recognition disappears?

Even that forgetting occurs within the power of consciousness.

It has not created a second reality independent of Śiva.

The extraordinary conclusion is that the path and the apparent obstacle are not made from two fundamentally different substances.

They are different modes of the same divine manifestation.

Śakti is not a ladder somebody built so you could climb out of the universe.

She’s the power through which the universe appears.

The power through which you experience yourself as limited.

And the power through which that limitation can be recognised as a contraction of the infinite.

The Goddess isn’t waiting at the end of the path.

She’s been making the path, walking the path, and appearing as the confused individual wondering where the path leads.

An astonishing amount of work for one woman.

But apparently infinite power does have its advantages.
