# Śiva Sūtras 3.43

नैसर्गिकः प्राणसम्बन्धः

Naisargikaḥ prāṇasambandhaḥ.

The connection with vital energy is natural.

Even When You’re Liberated, Your Body Keeps Breathing. Shocking, I Know.

Now this is an extraordinarily subtle continuation.

And we need to understand why Vasugupta places it here.

The previous sūtra described the liberated yogi as still clothed in the elements.

The body remains.

And now the question arises:

Why?

If the fundamental conditions of bondage have been overcome, why does the embodied condition continue?

Kṣemarāja answers:

Naisargikaḥ prāṇasambandhaḥ.

The connection with prāṇa is natural.

Let’s unpack it.

Naisargika — natural, belonging to nature, arising spontaneously.

Prāṇa — vital energy, life-breath.

Sambandha — connection, relationship.

But here’s the important point.

Kṣemarāja isn’t merely making the ordinary biological observation that living organisms breathe.

He’s interpreting prāṇa through the metaphysics of Śakti.

And this becomes absolutely fascinating.

The supreme consciousness possesses svātantrya.

Freedom.

Through that freedom, it manifests the universe.

And part of this manifestation involves adopting the standpoint of a contracted subject.

A perceiver.

A living individual.

And prāṇa, within the tantric framework, belongs to the manifestation of that living subjectivity.

Now, Kṣemarāja describes the Goddess of consciousness as wishing to manifest the wonderful diversity of the universe.

And in doing so, she assumes a contracted aspect associated with vital activity.

The subject and its objects become manifest through this process.

The relation between consciousness and vital energy therefore belongs to the divine freedom of manifestation.

It’s not necessarily an alien imprisonment imposed upon Śiva from outside.

And this is where the doctrine becomes extremely elegant.

The same power that makes embodied life possible can continue to operate after the yogi has recognised its source.

The body remains alive.

Breathing continues.

The faculties function.

But the yogi is no longer fundamentally identified with them.

Now Kṣemarāja brings in a very interesting tantric correspondence.

The three principal subtle channels.

Iḍā.

Piṅgalā.

Suṣumṇā.

And these are connected with symbolic structures involving:

Moon.

Sun.

Fire.

The known.

The knower.

Knowledge.

And the three temporal dimensions:

Past.

Present.

Future.

Now, we have to keep two levels distinct.

These are traditional tantric correspondences concerning the subtle body and cosmic manifestation.

They should not be mistaken for anatomical structures verified by modern physiology.

But within the tradition, their metaphysical significance is substantial.

They express how the power of consciousness becomes articulated through vitality, temporality and differentiation.

And Kṣemarāja goes even deeper.

He relates prāṇa to the processes of emission and return.

Visarga.

Emission.

Āpūraṇa.

Filling, inward replenishment.

And he connects these with the continuous movement of life.

Now imagine breathing.

An outgoing movement.

An incoming movement.

An apparent alternation.

A rhythm.

Within the tantric model, this rhythm symbolises the larger movement of manifestation and withdrawal.

The same structure encountered throughout the text.

Sṛṣṭi.

Manifestation.

Sthiti.

Maintenance.

Laya.

Withdrawal.

Now the cosmic process is understood through the rhythm of vitality.

And Kṣemarāja cites the Svacchandatantra on the relationship between prāṇa and apāna, as well as the association of the phoneme ha with spontaneous vital movement.

He also cites Bhaṭṭa Kallaṭa’s Tattvārthacintāmaṇi, where consciousness is described as becoming vital energy. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, here’s why this matters.

The body’s continued vitality doesn’t invalidate liberation.

The natural connection with prāṇa can remain.

But the yogi understands that connection through the supreme conscious reality.

And this is a very different perspective from assuming that liberation requires the abolition of ordinary biological life.

The body can breathe.

The senses can function.

The world can appear.

And recognition can remain.

The important distinction is not whether the vital processes continue.

It’s whether consciousness is still fundamentally misidentified with them.

Which brings us to the penultimate sūtra.

And this one is exceptionally technical.

Because Vasugupta is going to discuss the subtle channels, the central reality, and the highest form of contemplative awareness.

And Kṣemarāja is going to make an extraordinary claim.

That the yogi established in the innermost conscious reality no longer depends upon the distinctions between the left, right and central channels.

In other words:

After an entire book discussing the metaphysical plumbing system, we’re finally going beyond the pipes.

Thank God.
