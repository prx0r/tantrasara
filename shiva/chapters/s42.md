# Śiva Sūtras 3.10

रङ्गोऽन्तरात्मा

Raṅgo ’ntarātmā.

The inner Self is the stage.

The Stage Is Your Inner Self. The Universe Has Gone Full Broadway.

Right.

So we’ve got our actor.

Śiva.

Very talented.

Infinite range.

Can play literally anybody.

But where does the performance occur?

Vasugupta says:

Raṅgaḥ.

The stage.

And:

Antarātmā.

The inner self.

Now this needs careful interpretation, because it would be tempting to say:

“The stage is pure consciousness.”

Which sounds beautiful.

But Kṣemarāja’s explanation is more specific.

Here antarātman refers to the inner individual constitution — particularly the subtle and causal dimensions of embodied experience.

This isn’t simply another name for supreme consciousness.

The distinction matters.

Remember puryaṣṭaka?

The eightfold subtle constitution comprising the mental faculties and subtle sensory principles.

Kṣemarāja connects the stage with this subtle embodiment.

He also mentions the causal condition associated with voidness and vital energy.

In other words, the stage is the inner structure through which the individual performance becomes possible.

Think about how you experience the world.

You’re not merely receiving a sequence of completely uninterpreted objects.

Your mental faculties organise experience.

Memory contributes continuity.

Desire contributes motivation.

The senses contribute differentiated contents.

The intellect establishes judgements.

The ego appropriates experiences.

And the entire inner apparatus participates in the construction of your lived world.

Now Kṣemarāja isn’t proposing a modern neuroscientific theory.

He’s describing a tantric metaphysical structure.

But the theatrical metaphor becomes extraordinarily illuminating.

The actor manifests different roles through the stage of the inner being.

And the subtle body provides the conditions through which those roles are enacted.

Take a waking experience.

You see somebody you recognise.

Immediately, memory comes into play.

You remember past interactions.

You anticipate future possibilities.

You feel attraction or aversion.

You construct a particular identity in relation to them.

All of this belongs to the inner apparatus through which experience unfolds.

And then you dream.

The same individual may appear in a completely different setting.

Their behaviour changes.

Your own role changes.

The entire situation is transformed.

Yet the experience arises through the powers of consciousness operating in a different condition.

The stage has changed its scenery.

But the deeper actor remains.

Now here’s the point.

The ordinary person becomes absorbed in the stage and the roles.

The realised yogi recognises their underlying nature.

Kṣemarāja explains that the actor manifests the world’s drama through the successive activities of the inner senses, while the subtle body constitutes the stage upon which the performance unfolds. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

This also explains why the inner faculties aren’t simply enemies to be destroyed.

They’re the instruments of manifestation.

And within Āṇavopāya, they can become instruments of recognition.

The individual starts from the stage.

The bodily and mental structures.

And through their contemplation, moves towards recognition of the actor.

Now, think of the relationship between these last two sūtras.

Nartaka ātmā.

The Self is the dancer.

Raṅgo ’ntarātmā.

The inner self is the stage.

The dancer acts.

The stage provides the locus of the drama.

And now all we need is an audience.

But because this is Kashmir Śaivism, the audience is going to be something completely unexpected.
