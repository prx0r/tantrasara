# Śiva Sūtras 3.5

Nāḍīsaṃhāra-bhūtajaya-bhūtakaivalya-bhūtapṛthaktvāni.

Withdrawal of the subtle channels; mastery of the elements; isolation from the elements; separation from the elements.

Look at the length of this thing.

Vasugupta has suddenly remembered Sanskrit allows compounds.

He’s gone from two-word philosophical explosions to what appears to be the password for a particularly secure monastery.

But the sūtra is important because it introduces a series of yogic operations.

Kṣemarāja understands these through the interconnected disciplines of breath regulation, concentration, withdrawal and absorption.

Let’s work through them.

Nāḍīsaṃhāra — Withdrawal of vital movement
Nāḍī refers to a subtle channel through which prāṇa, vital energy, is understood to operate in traditional yogic physiology.

Saṃhāra means withdrawal or reabsorption.

Now, according to the relevant tantric model, ordinary vital activity operates through differentiated channels and functions.

Among the familiar terms are:

Prāṇa — one principal form of vital movement.

Apāna — another, associated with downward-moving or eliminative functions.

Udāna — an upward-associated vital function, especially important in these subtle-body accounts.

And the central channel:

Suṣumṇā.

Kṣemarāja describes the reabsorption of vital movement into the middle channel, involving the relation between prāṇa and apāna and the central function of udāna.

He cites detailed breath-regulation methods from the Svacchandatantra.

These include inhalation, exhalation, retention and the meditative unification of vital movement.

The historical text really does prescribe breath practices. It’s not merely using the vocabulary metaphorically.

But the subtle-channel system is a traditional yogic framework, not an established anatomical network, and forceful breath retention is not something to improvise from an audiobook.

For our purposes, the philosophical point is sufficient.

Ordinary vital activity is experienced through multiple differentiated functions.

The contemplative aim is their unification and withdrawal towards a subtler centre.

Bhūtajaya — Mastery of the elements
Bhūta means an existent or element; here the gross elements.

Jaya means conquest or mastery.

The five familiar elements are:

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

Air.

Space.

In the tantric account, the practitioner employs specific dhāraṇās, concentrations, associated with these elements.

Kṣemarāja cites the Svacchandatantra on their bodily locations and the extraordinary powers traditionally attributed to such practices.

These include concentrating on air at the foot, fire near the navel, earth at the throat, water around the uvula and space at the crown of the head.

It’s an unusual arrangement if you’re expecting a modern anatomy textbook.

But this is ritual-yogic physiology, not ordinary physiology.

And the text’s claims about mastery and supernatural accomplishments should be understood as traditional claims rather than demonstrated physical abilities.

Bhūtakaivalya — Withdrawal from the elements
Kaivalya conveys isolation or separateness.

Here Kṣemarāja associates the process with pratyāhāra.

Withdrawal of attention from sensory objects.

Ordinarily, the mind rushes towards its objects.

A sound arises.

Attention follows.

A smell arises.

Attention follows.

Your phone vibrates.

The entire nervous system declares a state of emergency.

The practitioner deliberately withdraws from this outward dispersion.

Not because sensory experience is inherently evil.

But because the mind is ordinarily dominated by its constant engagement with differentiated objects.

The withdrawal creates a condition in which the source of awareness can become the focus.

Bhūtapṛthaktva — Separation from the elements
And here we arrive at the deepest point.

Pṛthaktva means separation or distinctness.

But this isn’t a denial of embodiment.

It is the recognition that consciousness is not exhausted by the elements constituting embodied experience.

Earth, water, fire, air and space belong to the field of manifestation.

The conscious reality in which they appear is not reducible, within this metaphysical system, to any one of them.

Kṣemarāja associates this culmination with samādhi and recognition of the independent nature of consciousness.

His discussion draws extensively on the Svacchandatantra, moving through prāṇāyāma, dhāraṇā, pratyāhāra and the transcendence of elemental identification. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And notice what we’ve done.

We began with ordinary embodied existence.

Then moved through its vital functions.

Then its elemental constitution.

Then withdrawal from identification with those elements.

And finally towards the conscious reality underlying the entire process.

This is Āṇavopāya in action.

Starting from the limited individual.

Employing definite techniques.

And moving towards recognition.

But Vasugupta immediately issues another warning.

Because apparently the practitioner can get extremely good at these exercises and still completely miss the point.
