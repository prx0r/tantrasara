# Śiva Sūtras 3.27

कथा जपः

Kathā japaḥ.

Conversation is mantra-recitation.

Every Conversation Is a Mantra. You’ve Been Chanting the Entire Time, You Absolute Donut.

OH, THIS IS GLORIOUS.

Absolutely glorious.

Two words.

Katha.

Speech, conversation, discourse.

Japa.

Repetition or recitation of mantra.

And Vasugupta announces:

Kathā japaḥ.

Conversation is japa.

Now, if you take that out of context, it’s completely ridiculous.

Imagine somebody spending three hours complaining about the weather.

And announcing:

“Actually, I have just performed three hours of supreme tantric mantra practice.”

Well.

Not quite.

Kṣemarāja makes a very important qualification.

We’re talking about the speech of the yogi established in recognition of supreme I-consciousness.

And this is where the whole philosophical journey becomes extraordinarily beautiful.

Remember the beginning of Book II?

चित्तं मन्त्रः।

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

Now, in Book III:

कथा जपः।

Kathā japaḥ.

Speech is mantra-recitation.

The same basic transformation is being expressed at a more complete level.

The mind no longer simply produces differentiated cognitions that reinforce bondage.

It becomes an instrument of recognition.

And now even ordinary speech can arise from the uninterrupted awareness of the supreme Self.

But here’s the really important part.

Kṣemarāja connects this with the Mahāmantra.

The great mantra of universal I-consciousness.

Aham.

I.

Not the little ego.

Not the ordinary personality.

But the primordial I-consciousness of Śiva.

And within the Trika understanding, this I-consciousness is inseparable from the manifestation of the universe.

Now imagine the yogi speaking.

The words arise.

They may concern ordinary things.

Food.

Travel.

A philosophical discussion.

A question.

An answer.

But throughout the movement of speech, the yogi remains established in the universal conscious source.

So the verbal activity no longer obscures that source.

Instead, it becomes an expression of the uninterrupted recognition of Śiva.

And Kṣemarāja cites the Kālikākrama to explain that the yogi’s speech constitutes japa because it arises from the continual apprehension of the great mantra’s deity as one’s own Self.

This is very different from mechanically repeating a formula.

The highest japa is inseparable from recognition.

And then he cites the Vijñānabhairava.

Which is where things become particularly interesting.

Because the text describes spontaneous mantra in relation to the movement of breath.

Haṃsa.

The subtle mantra associated with the natural movement of respiration.

In a traditional account, the outgoing and incoming breaths are correlated with the syllables sa and ha, producing the continuous mantra of life.

And the text gives the traditional daily count:

21,600 repetitions.

Now, this is a conventional yogic calculation based on an assumed average breathing rhythm, not a universal physiological measurement applicable to every human being.

But its symbolic significance is extraordinary.

The living being is understood as continually participating in an unspoken mantra.

Breathing itself becomes associated with the divine power of manifestation.

And Kṣemarāja relates this to the higher understanding of japa as uninterrupted contemplation of the supreme reality. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now consider the progression.

Ordinarily, a mantra seems to be something you deliberately repeat.

You sit down.

You count the syllables.

You focus your attention.

You try to maintain concentration.

But in the condition described here, the yogi’s awareness of the divine is no longer restricted to deliberate recitation.

Speech itself becomes an expression of recognition.

And this is magnificent.

Because it means the sacred is no longer confined to special verbal formulas.

Not because the formulas have become meaningless.

But because the conscious source of all speech has been recognised.

The mantra is no longer merely an activity performed at particular times.

The underlying I-consciousness is apprehended as continuously present.

And here’s the funniest thing.

You can be talking about something completely ordinary.

And the philosophical meaning is transformed by the standpoint from which the speech arises.

Imagine a realised yogi asking:

“Could you pass the salt?”

An entirely ordinary sentence.

No Sanskrit.

No bells.

No elaborate mudrās.

Not even a particularly mystical tone of voice.

But within Kṣemarāja’s understanding, the speech of such a yogi is not separate from the continuous contemplation of Śiva.

The salt is still salt.

The request still means what it means.

The linguistic function remains perfectly ordinary.

But the awareness underlying the act is not limited by the ordinary identification with separate selfhood.

And thus ordinary discourse can be understood as japa.

Now, we should not flatten the teaching into:

“Everything you say is a mantra, therefore say whatever you like.”

That would be a rather spectacular misunderstanding.

The condition of recognition comes first.

The transformation of speech follows from it.

And this is why the text doesn’t simply teach a new religious ritual.

It teaches a transformation of the conscious standpoint from which activity occurs.

The body continues as a vow.

Speech becomes japa.

And now Vasugupta announces what the realised yogi gives to others.

And it’s not money.

Not supernatural powers.

Not a complicated initiation certificate.

Self-knowledge.

The highest gift.
