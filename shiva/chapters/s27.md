# Śiva Sūtras 2.5

विद्यासमुत्थाने स्वाभाविके खेचरी शिवावस्था

Vidyāsamutthāne svābhāvike khecarī śivāvasthā.

When supreme knowledge arises spontaneously, Khecarī manifests as the state of Śiva.

No, Khecarī Doesn’t Mean Shoving Your Tongue Up Your Nose.

Right.

We’ve reached Khecarī.

And unfortunately, we’ve got to clear something up immediately.

Because if you search for Khecarī Mudrā, you’re likely to encounter extremely flexible yogis doing extraordinary things with their tongues.

In certain haṭhayoga traditions, khecarīmudrā refers to a technique involving the tongue and the upper palate.

But that is not what Kṣemarāja means here.

And he explicitly distinguishes this sūtra’s Khecarī from physical or specialised yogic procedures promising extraordinary powers, including flight.

Quite.

It’s always a relief when a medieval tantric philosopher tells you that you don’t have to rearrange your mouth to attain supreme consciousness.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Vidyā — knowledge, here supreme knowledge.

Samutthāna — arising, emergence.

Svābhāvika — natural, spontaneous, belonging to one’s own nature.

Khecarī — she who moves through kha, space or the sky.

Śivāvasthā — the state of Śiva.

Now the word kha is doing something extremely important.

In ordinary Sanskrit, it can mean space or sky.

But in this tantric context, Kṣemarāja interprets it as the boundless space of consciousness.

So Khecarī is the power moving freely within that space.

And when supreme knowledge arises naturally, the yogi enters the state of Śiva.

Not the state of an individual who has acquired another special experience.

The state of recognising the nature of consciousness itself.

Imagine the mind as a bird.

Ordinarily, it flies towards objects.

A sound.

A memory.

A desire.

A fear.

It moves between limited things.

And its sense of freedom is conditioned by those things.

But now the bird discovers something astonishing.

Its real freedom does not consist merely in choosing which object to fly towards.

The openness through which every movement is possible was never absent.

This is the metaphorical significance of Khecarī.

Not a bird imprisoned in a box getting a slightly larger box.

Not even a bird acquiring a particularly impressive pair of wings.

But the recognition of the boundless conscious space in which its movements arise.

And Kṣemarāja links this with the spontaneous emergence of bliss.

Ānanda.

Not simply ordinary pleasure.

Not an exciting sensation that eventually disappears and leaves you wanting another one.

But the delight associated with consciousness recognising its own freedom.

And notice what happened between 2.4 and 2.5.

At 2.4, the practitioner became fascinated by exceptional phenomena appearing within Māyā.

At 2.5, those limited accomplishments are subordinated to the spontaneous arising of supreme knowledge.

It’s a complete reorientation.

Instead of asking:

“What amazing thing can I experience next?”

The yogi discovers the ground of experience itself.

And that, within Kṣemarāja’s interpretation, is the state of Śiva.

Not supernatural tourism.

Not a spiritual upgrade package.

Recognition of the freedom that makes every experience possible.

Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.
