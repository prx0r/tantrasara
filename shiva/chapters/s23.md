# Śiva Sūtras 2.1
## BOOK II — ŚĀKTOPĀYA

चित्तं मन्त्रः

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

Congratulations, Your Mind Is Now a Mantra.

Ladies and gentlemen.

He’s done it again.

Two words.

Two bloody words.

And the entire philosophy of mantra has just been turned upside down.

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

Now, hang on.

Didn’t we just spend the entire first book explaining that ordinary cognition produces bondage?

Wasn’t differentiated knowledge responsible for the whole miserable business of individual limitation?

And now we’re being told that the mind is a sacred instrument of liberation?

What’s going on?

Has Vasugupta forgotten what he wrote?

Has the manuscript been assembled in the wrong order?

Is Śiva having an administrative disagreement with himself?

No.

This is the central brilliance of Śāktopāya.

The same consciousness that appears contracted into ordinary mental activity can be recognised in its divine nature.

But Kṣemarāja immediately makes an important qualification.

He isn’t saying every random mental fluctuation automatically functions as a liberating mantra.

Your thoughts about what to eat for dinner are not necessarily the supreme mantra.

Neither is mentally rehearsing a conversation in which you absolutely destroy somebody who disagreed with you three years ago.

Although, admittedly, that can feel rather powerful.

The relevant citta is consciousness actively engaged in contemplating the supreme reality.

Let’s take the Sanskrit apart.

Cit — consciousness, awareness.

Citta — mind or mental consciousness, particularly in its active, modified condition.

Mantra — a sacred formula, but also understood traditionally through the acts of contemplation and protection.

Kṣemarāja gives a characteristic tantric explanation connecting mantra with:

Manana — inward contemplation.

Trāṇa — protection or deliverance.

This is a traditional interpretive derivation, not a claim that the two words mechanically combine according to ordinary Sanskrit morphology.

But the philosophical meaning is fantastic.

The mantra is that through which contemplation becomes liberating.

Liberating from what?

From the fundamental misidentification that makes you experience yourself as a finite subject surrounded by an entirely separate world.

Here’s the really important bit.

Kṣemarāja insists that the efficacy of mantra does not ultimately reside in an arrangement of letters considered separately from consciousness.

The living power of mantra is realised through identification with the conscious reality of its deity.

And this is an absolutely enormous claim.

Imagine chanting:

ॐ नमः शिवाय।.

Oṃ namaḥ śivāya.

You can pronounce those sounds mechanically.

You can repeat them while making breakfast.

You can count them on a string of beads.

You can construct an exceptionally complicated spreadsheet documenting your progress.

Very impressive.

Ten thousand repetitions.

Beautiful chart.

Excellent consistency.

But what if your fundamental understanding remains:

“I am a small individual over here, desperately attempting to contact a distant divine being over there”?

Then the deepest nondual meaning of the mantra has not yet been recognised.

For Kṣemarāja, the culmination involves recognising the non-separation of the practitioner, mantra and deity.

The sound is not merely sent towards Śiva.

The very awareness in which the mantra appears is recognised as Śiva’s own conscious power.

That’s the transformation.

And notice how this relates to the previous book.

At 1.4:

ज्ञानाधिष्ठानं मातृका।.

Jñānādhiṣṭhānaṃ mātṛkā.

Mātṛkā is the foundation of differentiated knowledge.

Language participates in the structure of limitation.

But now:

चित्तं मन्त्रः।.

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind itself becomes mantra when its activity is directed towards recognition of its supreme source.

The problem was never that consciousness possessed the ability to articulate experience.

The problem was the failure to recognise the true nature of that articulation.

The same Śakti binds.

The same Śakti liberates.

An extraordinary business model.

She’s manufacturing the locks and distributing the keys.

And the keys, apparently, were made from the same material as the locks.

Kṣemarāja’s point: The mantra’s highest power is not mere pronunciation but contemplative identification with the divine consciousness embodied in it. He supports this reading with tantric scriptural passages and the Spandakārikā. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Book II: Śāktopāya — The Way of Śakti.

How to Turn Your Own Mind into a Divine Weapon, Discover That the Alphabet Is Literally a Goddess, and Finally Stop Being Such a Cosmically Confused Little Bastard.

Previously, on the Śiva Sūtras…
Right.


When we last encountered our spiritual protagonist Vasugupta, he’d made twenty-two rather explosive statements about the nature of reality.

We discovered that consciousness is the Self.

That limited knowledge is bondage.

That the entire universe is the body of Śiva.

That enlightenment involves an extraordinary sense of wonder.

And that, under certain rather ambitious tantric interpretations, a sufficiently accomplished yogi may possess supernatural powers.

All perfectly reasonable.

But then Vasugupta ended the first book with something particularly intriguing.

महाह्रदानुसन्धानान्मन्त्रवीर्यानुभवः।.

Mahāhradānusandhānān mantravīryānubhavaḥ.

Through union with the Great Lake comes the experience of the potency of mantra.

The Great Lake.

The unfathomable reservoir of consciousness.

The divine source of every sound, every thought, every manifestation.

And now, naturally, we’ve got a question.

If the power of mantra comes from consciousness…

What the bloody hell actually is a mantra?

Is it a magic word?

A sequence of Sanskrit syllables?

A cosmic password?

Does Śiva have some sort of metaphysical voice-recognition software?

Do you chant Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya fourteen thousand times and suddenly receive administrative privileges over the universe?

Well.

Vasugupta is about to disappoint everyone who’s purchased a very expensive singing bowl.

Because the answer is considerably more interesting.

Welcome to Śāktopāya.

The way of Śakti.

Whereas Śāmbhavopāya centres on the immediate upsurge of recognition through divine will, Śāktopāya works through the power of awareness, understanding, contemplation and the transformation of cognition.

It’s not merely thinking about Śiva.

It’s discovering what thinking actually is when illuminated by its own conscious source.

And this is where Kṣemarāja, writing his Śivasūtravimarśinī, really gets going.

Ten sūtras.

One extraordinarily sophisticated theory of mantra, language, consciousness and liberation.

All ten Sanskrit formulations below follow the traditional Śāktopāya sequence; the explanations follow Kṣemarāja rather than treating the brief aphorisms as self-explanatory.
