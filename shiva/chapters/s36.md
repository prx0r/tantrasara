# Śiva Sūtras 3.4

शरीरे संहारः कलानाम्

Śarīre saṃhāraḥ kalānām.

In the body, the dissolution of the constituent powers or parts.

Right, Time to Uninstall the Universe from Your Body.

We’ve arrived at our first major concrete contemplative instruction.

And Kṣemarāja explains it using bhāvanā.

Creative contemplation.

Or more precisely, an intentionally cultivated contemplative apprehension.

Now, unlike the sudden recognition characteristic of Śāmbhavopāya, we’re working with structured practices.

The practitioner deliberately employs the mind to transform the way manifestation is understood.

And the practice here concerns the dissolution of the principles of manifestation into their sources.

Saṃhāra.

Withdrawal.

Reabsorption.

Dissolution.

Remember the thirty-six tattvas?

The entire hierarchy extending from Śiva down to earth?

Well, now we’re moving in reverse.

The universe has manifested through successive degrees of differentiation.

The contemplative process reverses that movement.

And the body becomes the field of practice.

Now, Kṣemarāja distinguishes three levels of embodiment.

The gross body, constituted by the gross elements.

The subtle body, including the mental faculties and subtle elements.

And a higher causal level, involving subtler conditions of consciousness and vital power extending towards Samanā.

The practitioner contemplates the progressive dissolution of the manifest principles.

Earth is reabsorbed into its causal principle.

And so on through the hierarchy.

The differentiated structures of manifestation are traced back towards their ultimate source.

But the point isn’t merely to memorise the thirty-six tattvas backwards like an exceptionally committed Sanskrit student.

Although, to be fair, that would be quite impressive.

The point is the transformation of how the practitioner understands the relationship between the manifest and its source.

Imagine looking at a golden ornament.

A bracelet.

You can identify its particular form.

Its weight.

Its decoration.

Its shape.

But you can also recognise that the bracelet’s existence depends upon the gold.

The bracelet is not an independent substance standing apart from the gold.

Now imagine reversing the process conceptually.

You cease attending to the bracelet as a separate form.

You recognise the underlying material reality.

Then you consider that the same reality can appear through many different ornaments.

The distinction between the forms remains intelligible.

But their substantial unity becomes apparent.

This is only an analogy.

The tattva system is far more elaborate, and Śiva is not merely an inert substance underlying objects.

But it illustrates the direction of contemplation.

From differentiation towards source.

From appearance towards its ground.

From limited identity towards consciousness.

And now Kṣemarāja brings in a rather dramatic method.

The body consumed by fire.

He cites the Vijñānabhairava, which describes contemplating a fire arising from the right foot and burning the body, culminating in the appearance of peaceful divine luminosity.

Yes.

A tantric visualisation of your entire body being incinerated.

Lovely.

Very relaxing.

But the fire is imagined within contemplation, not physically applied.

Nobody needs to set themselves on fire.

Nobody needs to damage the body.

The aim is to dissolve the mental fixation upon the body as the ultimate identity.

And this is where the philosophical and practical dimensions meet.

Ordinarily you perceive:

“I am this organism, situated in this particular place, surrounded by a separate universe.”

But through the contemplative dissolution of the embodied principles, that identification is progressively loosened.

The body, subtle faculties and differentiated categories are considered in relation to their source.

Ultimately, that source is consciousness.

Kṣemarāja connects this practice to dhyāna, meditation, and cites the Mālinīvijayatantra on the methods characteristic of Āṇavopāya, including recitation, meditation, phonemic contemplation and visualisation. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

The extraordinary point is that you begin with the very structures through which limitation is experienced.

The body.

The mind.

The elements.

And use them to contemplate what exceeds their limitations.

The prison becomes the meditation hall.

And this is when the text starts getting into yogic physiology.

Because apparently dissolving the universe wasn’t quite enough.

Now we’ve got to deal with the plumbing.
