# Śiva Sūtras 1.18

लोकानन्दः समाधिसुखम्

Lokānandaḥ samādhisukham.

The bliss of the world is the happiness of samādhi.

The Entire World Has Become Your Meditation.

Oh, this is beautiful.

Consider what many people imagine meditation to be.

You sit down.

Close your eyes.

Withdraw from the horrible noisy world.

Eventually, if you’re lucky, you get a special experience of peacefulness.

Then somebody starts drilling next door.

And your enlightenment has been cancelled.

The neighbour’s power tools have defeated the Absolute.

A tragic outcome.

But Vasugupta is describing something more radical.

Loka — the world, understood through the field of knowable objects and their experiencers.

Ānanda — bliss.

Samādhi — absorption, integration, collectedness.

Sukha — happiness or delight.

For the yogi established in recognition, the very appearance of the world is inseparable from the delight of conscious reality.

This doesn’t mean every worldly event becomes conventionally pleasant.

Pain can still be pain.

Loss can still be loss.

The claim is that the underlying conscious reality is not separated from its manifestations by the ordinary division between meditation and daily life.

The yogi doesn’t necessarily have to escape the world to recover their fundamental nature.

The world itself becomes an occasion for recognition.

Kṣemarāja connects this to resting in the position of the perceiver while the relation between experiencer and experienced remains manifest. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Imagine sitting beside a river.

A bird calls.

The water moves.

The air touches your skin.

A thought appears.

Normally, you might interpret these as separate things happening to a particular individual.

But in the nondual recognition described here, their entire manifestation is apprehended within the indivisible reality of consciousness.

The river is not merely an obstruction to meditation.

The bird is not interrupting your spiritual attainment.

The world is not an unfortunate distraction from Śiva.

It is Śiva’s own manifestation.

This is why Trika is so radically affirmative.

It is not primarily trying to get rid of existence.

It is trying to recognise its deepest nature.

And now we arrive at a rather unexpected turn.

Because Vasugupta is about to start talking about supernatural powers.

Naturally.
