# Śiva Sūtras 3.34

तद्विमुक्तस्तु केवली

Tadvimuktas tu kevalī.

But the one liberated from those is kevalī — established in their own pure, independent nature.

Congratulations, You’re Alone. Not Lonely. Metaphysically Alone.

Now this is absolutely magnificent.

Because we’ve just learned that the yogi can experience pleasure and pain without identifying their deepest nature with either.

And Vasugupta immediately announces:

Kevalī.

The one who stands alone.

Oh dear.

That sounds rather depressing.

You’ve attained the highest spiritual realization imaginable, and apparently there’s nobody else at the party.

Just you.

And Śiva.

Who is also you.

And the entire universe.

Which is also the manifestation of your own Śakti.

What an extraordinarily confusing social gathering.

But let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Tad — those; referring to pleasure and pain.

Vimuktaḥ — released, liberated, freed.

Tu — but, indeed, marking a distinction.

Kevalī — one established in aloneness, purity, or independence.

And here we need to understand kaivalya.

Because this is a term that appears in multiple Indian philosophical traditions, but it doesn’t always mean the same thing.

In classical Sāṃkhya-Yoga, kaivalya is associated with the isolation of puruṣa from prakṛti.

The conscious principle is distinguished from the material processes with which it had been confused.

But Trika doesn’t accept that ultimate metaphysical separation.

Because for Trika, the world of manifestation is not fundamentally independent of Śiva.

It’s Śiva’s own Śakti.

So Kṣemarāja’s kevalī is not an isolated consciousness hiding from an independently existing universe.

The yogi is established in the pure conscious nature of the Self, no longer bound by identification with the subtle body and its pleasure-pain experiences.

And this is what makes the doctrine so subtle.

The yogi isn’t simply detached from objects because objects are horrible.

The yogi recognises their fundamental conscious nature as not dependent upon those objects.

Now Kṣemarāja makes the point exceptionally strongly.

The liberated yogi is not internally touched even by the residual impressions of pleasure and pain.

Saṃskāra.

The latent impressions left by experience.

And that’s interesting.

Because ordinary pleasure and pain don’t simply disappear the moment the immediate experience ends.

They can leave tendencies.

Memories.

Habits.

Expectations.

A pleasant experience may produce desire for repetition.

A painful experience may produce aversion.

And the individual becomes governed by those residual dispositions.

But Kṣemarāja describes a yogi whose true conscious identity is no longer bound by even those impressions.

Their nature is cinmātra.

Pure consciousness.

Now this is a traditional description of the highest yogic condition, not a clinical claim that liberated persons become incapable of remembering painful experiences.

And it certainly shouldn’t be used to deny someone’s suffering.

The distinction is between the functioning of experience and the ultimate identity of the experiencer.

Kṣemarāja invokes the Kālikākrama, which describes the yogi breaking through the great delusion of duality constructed through the cognitions of pleasure, pain and related mental states.

The problem is not simply that an unpleasant event occurs.

It’s the metaphysical misunderstanding through which the limited cognitive subject is mistaken for the ultimate Self. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now here’s the extraordinary point.

Kevalī doesn’t mean a lonely individual.

It means the yogi is no longer fundamentally dependent on the limitations that define individual subjectivity.

They’re established in the conscious reality from which the appearance of individuality proceeds.

And that reality is not empty of manifestation.

It’s the source of manifestation.

So we’re not talking about a person who has become metaphysically stranded in an infinite void.

We’re talking about consciousness no longer mistaken for the limited forms appearing within it.

Now imagine someone obsessed with maintaining a particular state of happiness.

Every experience is evaluated according to whether it produces pleasure.

They seek pleasant things.

Avoid unpleasant things.

And organise their entire lives around protecting the conditions of satisfaction.

Which, to be fair, is a fairly accurate description of much ordinary human behaviour.

But the difficulty is that pleasure and pain are changing phenomena.

If your identity depends upon preserving pleasant experience, you’re vulnerable to every change in its conditions.

The happiness arrives.

You cling to it.

The happiness disappears.

You suffer.

Then you begin searching for another happiness.

And the entire process continues.

But the kevalī, in Kṣemarāja’s account, has recognised a reality not exhausted by this sequence.

And that changes the meaning of freedom.

Freedom is not simply obtaining permanent pleasant sensations.

It’s the recognition of the conscious reality underlying changing sensations.

And now comes the rather devastating contrast.

Because Vasugupta is about to show us the opposite condition.

The individual who has not recognised their true nature.

The one who remains absorbed in pleasure and pain.

The one whose activity is governed by ignorance.

The one who keeps generating karma.

In other words:

The rest of us.

Lovely.
