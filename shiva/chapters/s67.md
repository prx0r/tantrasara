# Śiva Sūtras 3.35

मोहप्रतिसंहतस्तु कर्मात्मा

Mohapratisaṃhatas tu karmātmā.

But the one compacted in delusion is constituted by karmic activity.

You’ve Been Doing Karma. Karma Has Been Doing You.

Now this is a wonderful contrast.

And notice that little word again.

Tu.

But.

On the one hand, the liberated yogi.

Kevalī.

On the other hand, the bound individual.

Karmātmā.

The one whose identity is bound up with action.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Moha — delusion, bewilderment, ignorance.

Pratisaṃhata — compacted, gathered densely, thoroughly enveloped.

Karma — action, deed, karmic activity.

Ātmā — self, here the individual understood through involvement in karma.

Now, mohapratisaṃhata is a magnificent word.

A compact mass of delusion.

Not merely:

“I happen to be mistaken about one particular thing.”

No.

The whole structure of ordinary self-identification is saturated with misrecognition.

The individual believes themselves to be a finite subject possessing independent agency.

They experience the world as fundamentally separate.

They act through attraction and aversion.

And those actions produce karmic consequences.

Kṣemarāja explains that the bound person becomes the receptacle of pleasure and pain precisely because they are enveloped in ignorance.

And they are continually implicated in good and bad actions.

Śubha.

Auspicious, good.

Aśubha.

Inauspicious, bad.

Now, here’s something important.

Kṣemarāja isn’t saying morally good and bad actions are indistinguishable.

Quite the opposite.

He explicitly discusses their consequences.

But both belong to the condition of the individual agent acting under limited identification.

The person experiences:

“I am the doer.”

“I performed this action.”

“I must receive the result.”

And the entire karmic machinery becomes involved.

Now, remember the three malas.

Āṇavamala — the primordial sense of limitation.

Māyīyamala — the experience of differentiation.

Kārmamala — the limitation associated with agency and karmic action.

Here we’re seeing the consequences of the third.

The individual isn’t merely experiencing a separate world.

They are acting within it through the conviction of limited doership.

And therefore the consequences of action become part of their conditioned existence.

Kṣemarāja cites the Kālikākrama to explain how ignorance and conceptual construction prevent the individual from recognising the tattvas, beginning with Śiva, as manifestations of consciousness.

Then good and bad conditions arise through the person’s dependence upon ignorance.

And suffering follows from inauspicious states and deeds. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now think about the contrast with the previous sūtra.

The kevalī experiences the body and mind without treating their changing states as the ultimate Self.

The karmātmā identifies with the limited subject and its actions.

One recognises the underlying reality.

The other remains caught within its contracted manifestation.

And the important difference isn’t necessarily visible from the outside.

Two people might perform the same ordinary action.

They might both walk down a road.

Speak to another person.

Eat a meal.

But Kṣemarāja’s philosophical distinction concerns the conscious standpoint from which action occurs.

Not merely the outward movement.

One identifies with the finite agent as ultimate.

The other recognises the universal conscious source.

Of course, this doesn’t remove conventional responsibility.

A person claiming spiritual liberation cannot simply declare themselves exempt from the consequences of harming others.

The metaphysical recognition of Śiva is not an ethical loophole.

But the tradition’s point is that the ordinary structure of karmic bondage depends upon limited self-identification.

And now comes the extraordinary turn.

Because even the person compacted in delusion hasn’t ceased to be Śiva in their fundamental nature.

Which means the possibility of recognition remains.

And Kṣemarāja introduces śaktipāta.

The descent of divine grace.

The intervention of Śiva’s own power through which the individual’s innate freedom becomes disclosed.

And suddenly the whole situation changes.

The compact mass of delusion doesn’t have to remain compact forever.

The limitation can be recognised.

Difference can be transcended.

And then, according to the tradition, something rather spectacular becomes possible.

The creation of another world.

Oh, for God’s sake.

We’re back to supernatural powers.

Apparently Śiva cannot resist showing off.
