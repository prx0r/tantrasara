# Śiva Sūtras 3.1

आत्मा चित्तम्

Ātmā cittam.

The Self is mind.

Congratulations, You’re a Mind Now. Quite the Demotion.

EXCUSE ME?

Vasugupta.

My friend.

My brother in Bhairava.

WHAT?

The first sūtra of the entire text was:

चैतन्यमात्मा।

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

We’ve spent thirty-two sūtras establishing the supreme identity of consciousness.

And now you’ve changed your bloody mind?

Ātmā cittam.

The Self is mind.

Well, which is it?

Are we infinite consciousness, or are we a bundle of thoughts wandering around worrying about the price of vegetables?

Fortunately, Kṣemarāja anticipated this objection.

And his explanation is absolutely crucial.

He’s not contradicting the first book.

He’s changing the standpoint.

In the first book, we considered the true nature of the Self.

In this book, we consider the Self as it appears in the contracted condition of individuality.

That’s the distinction.

Let’s break down the Sanskrit.

Ātman — Self.

Citta — mind, mental consciousness.

But Kṣemarāja is very specific about citta.

Here it encompasses the antaḥkaraṇa, the internal instrument of cognition.

And that instrument consists of three major functions.

Buddhi — determining intelligence.

The faculty that makes judgements.

“This is a tree.”

“That is a dog.”

“That person is being unreasonable.”

Ahaṃkāra — the ego-making or I-making principle.

The faculty of appropriation.

“This is MY tree.”

“That is MY dog.”

“That person has insulted ME.”

Manas — the ordinary mind.

The faculty of mental construction, fluctuation, and engagement with sensory objects.

“What shall I do about this?”

“What if the tree falls?”

“What if the dog doesn’t like me?”

“What if everybody secretly hates me?”

And suddenly we’ve got the entire human predicament.

Buddhi identifies the object.

Ahaṃkāra appropriates the experience.

Manas starts producing a twelve-part Netflix documentary about it.

And the whole thing takes place within consciousness.

But because consciousness has contracted into identification with this mental apparatus, the limited individual experiences:

I am this mind.

Not:

“I am the universal consciousness in which these mental activities manifest.”

Just:

“I am this collection of thoughts, desires, judgements and personal history.”

And that’s the condition of the aṇu.

The little one.

The apparently finite individual.

But here’s the extraordinarily important move.

Kṣemarāja says the Self doesn’t genuinely become a different ultimate substance.

In its true nature, it remains consciousness.

Its identification with citta belongs to its āṇavadaśā — the condition of limitation.

So there’s no actual contradiction between:

Caitanyam ātmā.

And:

Ātmā cittam.

The first describes the Self in its fundamental nature.

The second describes the Self under the condition of contraction.

Now think about what that means.

The individual isn’t some metaphysical mistake that exists outside Śiva.

Individuality is itself a manifestation within Śiva’s consciousness.

The limitation is real as an experienced condition, but it does not establish an ultimately independent second reality.

The infinite has become involved in its own finite appearance.

And this is why saṃkoca, contraction, is so important in Trika.

Consciousness hasn’t been physically compressed into a tiny container.

It’s not as though somebody took an infinitely large Śiva, shoved him into a human skull, and slammed the lid.

Rather, the conscious subject appears under the conditions of limited knowing and agency.

Like an actor becoming completely absorbed in a character.

The actor doesn’t cease to be the actor.

But from within the performance, the character’s world becomes the apparent horizon of experience.

And the tragedy begins when the actor forgets the distinction.

Kṣemarāja explicitly states that there is no ultimate wandering of the Self, whose nature remains consciousness. The appearance of transmigration belongs to its contracted identification with the mental apparatus. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

The philosophical revelation: Your ordinary selfhood is not the complete truth of what you are. But neither is it something standing outside consciousness. It is consciousness appearing under conditions of limitation.

And now we know who our protagonist is.

Śiva.

Playing a person.

Who thinks he’s a mind.

And is about to become terribly confused.


Textual note: The ten Sanskrit sūtras are attested in the standard 77-sūtra recension. The explanatory reading follows Kṣemarāja’s Śivasūtravimarśinī, especially his distinctions concerning mantra, limited siddhis, Khecarī, Mātṛkā and the subsidence of pure knowledge. Traditional supernatural and cosmological claims have been presented as the tradition’s claims, not verified physical phenomena. The humorous narration is original rather than a quotation of Kṣemarāja.

Next comes the longest and most extraordinary part of the text.

Book III — Āṇavopāya
Forty-five sūtras.

The limited individual, the return of bondage, the construction of experience, the role of the senses, the breath, embodiment, liberation in daily life, and one of the most beautiful sequences in all Kashmir Śaivism:

नर्तक आत्मा।

Nartaka ātmā.

The Self is the dancer.

रङ्गोऽन्तरात्मा।.

Raṅgo ’ntarātmā.

The inner Self is the stage.

प्रेक्षकाणीन्द्रियाणि।.

Prekṣakāṇīndriyāṇi.

The senses are the spectators.

Which means that after two books of studying the metaphysical nature of consciousness, Vasugupta is about to explain that the entire business of being alive is essentially Śiva putting on a theatrical production for himself.

And you’ve been taking your role rather seriously.



## BOOK III — ĀṆAVOPĀYA



Previously, on the Śiva Sūtras…
Right.


We’ve survived two books.

In Book I, Vasugupta told us that consciousness is the Self, the universe is the body of Śiva, and reality is an extraordinary expression of divine freedom.

Beautiful.

In Book II, we discovered that the mind can become mantra, the Sanskrit alphabet is a manifestation of Śakti, knowledge is food, and apparently the entire human body is a sacrificial offering.

Also beautiful.

Slightly concerning, but beautiful.

And now we’ve arrived at Book III.

Āṇavopāya.

The means belonging to the aṇu.

The limited individual.

And this is where things get really interesting.

Because thus far, we’ve been considering recognition from relatively exalted standpoints.

Śāmbhavopāya — the way associated with Śiva’s immediate power of will.

Śāktopāya — the way associated with the transformative power of knowledge.

But now Vasugupta has decided to address a rather awkward situation.

What if you’re not a supreme yogi?

What if the spontaneous recognition of Bhairava doesn’t arise?

What if you’ve read an entire book about the divine potency of mantra, but the moment somebody speaks rudely to you, you become a furious little monkey again?

What if you understand svātantrya intellectually, but your actual daily experience consists mainly of confusion, desire, frustration, and checking whether somebody has replied to your messages?

Well.

Congratulations.

You are the target audience.

Āṇavopāya begins with the limited perspective and employs more definite supports: contemplation, breath, concentration, bodily practices, the senses, and the transformation of ordinary experience.

But don’t mistake this for a completely different philosophy.

We’re not abandoning nonduality.

We’re asking how the individual who experiences limitation can begin to recognise the freedom that was always their deepest nature.

And Kṣemarāja is going to give us an extraordinary explanation.

The individual mind.

The thirty-six tattvas.

The five limiting powers.

The dissolution of the cosmos within the body.

Subtle channels and extraordinary yogic claims.

The world as the radiance of consciousness.

And then, perhaps most beautifully of all, the entire universe as a theatrical performance.

Śiva the actor.

The subtle body the stage.

The senses the audience.

And a cosmic drama in which the actor has become so absorbed in the role that he’s forgotten he’s the one performing it.

Absolutely magnificent.

One textual point before the curtain rises: we’re following Kṣemarāja’s 45-sūtra third section, making 77 sūtras overall. Another recension inserts Visargasvābhāvyād abahiḥsthites tatsthitiḥ before Bījāvadhānam, resulting in 46 sūtras in this section. We’ll stay with the 45-sūtra sequence so the numbering remains consistent.

Now then.

Lights down.

Curtain up.

The Lord of the Universe is about to explain why you think you’re a person.
