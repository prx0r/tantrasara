# Śiva Sūtras 3.22

प्राणसमाचारे समदर्शनम्

Prāṇasamācāre samadarśanam.

When the vital power flows outward in an integrated way, there is equal vision of the one conscious reality.

You Come Out of Samādhi, and the Entire World Is Still Śiva. Excellent.

Now this is magnificent.

Because we’ve just entered the supreme condition through inward immersion.

And a very obvious question arises.

What happens when we come out?

Do we lose it?

Does the world become alien again?

Do we immediately return to the ordinary sense of separation?

Do we have to spend the rest of our lives sitting perfectly still, hoping nobody disturbs us?

Well, Vasugupta has a much more interesting answer.

Samadarśanam.

Equal vision.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Prāṇa — vital breath or vital energy.

Samācāra — proper, balanced or integrated movement; here, the gradual outward extension of vital activity.

Sama — equal, uniform, undivided in this context.

Darśana — seeing, vision, apprehension.

Now, this is not simply a sūtra about breathing slowly.

Kṣemarāja’s commentary is much more specific.

He explains that after the yogi enters the supreme state, vital activity naturally begins to extend outward again.

The practitioner doesn’t remain permanently absorbed in a condition without ordinary engagement.

The senses operate.

The breath moves.

The mind functions.

The world appears.

But now the outgoing activity is pervaded by the recognition of supreme consciousness.

And therefore samadarśana arises.

The yogi perceives the same fundamental reality throughout experience.

Kṣemarāja describes this as the uniformity of cidānanda.

Consciousness and bliss.

And this is important.

The yogi doesn’t merely have an extraordinary experience and then return to a fundamentally separate ordinary life.

The recognition becomes operative as experience unfolds again.

Now imagine someone meditating.

The mind becomes quiet.

The sense of limitation temporarily subsides.

There’s an extraordinary experience of spaciousness.

And then they open their eyes.

There’s a table.

A chair.

Some dust floating in a beam of sunlight.

An irritating noise outside.

And the question is:

Has the divine disappeared because objects have returned?

From the Trika standpoint, no.

Because the objects themselves are manifestations of consciousness.

The apparent distinction between meditation and ordinary experience doesn’t establish two fundamentally different ultimate realities.

The supreme conscious nature is not restricted to the absence of objects.

And Kṣemarāja makes precisely this point.

He explains that even as the intellect and prāṇa extend towards external objects, the realised yogi remains established in universal consciousness without the contracted standpoint returning.

He cites the Pratyabhijñā tradition and a passage associated with Ānandabhairava concerning equal vision across divine beings, social distinctions and things. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now let’s be careful.

Equal vision doesn’t mean empirical differences cease to matter.

A tiger and a cup of tea remain very different things.

And treating them identically could lead to a rather unpleasant afternoon.

It doesn’t mean morality disappears.

Nor that harmful actions become acceptable merely because everything is Śiva.

The philosophical point is that the underlying conscious reality is not ultimately divided by the differentiated forms through which it manifests.

And here’s the extraordinary result.

The yogi can participate in the world without losing recognition of its source.

The senses function.

The body functions.

Speech occurs.

Action occurs.

But their apparent differentiation no longer conceals the universal conscious reality.

This is integration.

And it’s a profound contrast with the idea that liberation consists solely in withdrawing from the world.

The inward movement is important.

But so is the outward return.

The contemplative state and ordinary activity are not ultimately alien realities.

Now, this is where things become particularly relevant to the problem of stabilising recognition.

Because Vasugupta is not claiming that everyone who has experienced turya will automatically remain established in it.

Quite the opposite.

The next two sūtras address what happens when the recognition becomes interrupted.

And they are absolutely brilliant.

Because we’re about to encounter something deeply familiar.

You recognise the truth.

You forget.

You remember again.

You become distracted.

You recognise again.

And Vasugupta is going to explain the entire cycle through the relationship between the middle of experience and its conscious source.

Which is exactly the sort of thing you’d expect from a man who has already informed us that the universe is an elaborate one-man theatrical production.
