# Śiva Sūtras 3.41

तदारूढप्रमितेस्तत्क्षयाज्जीवसङ्क्षयः

Tadārūḍhapramites tatkṣayāj jīvasaṅkṣayaḥ.

For one whose awareness is established in that supreme state, the ending of desire brings the dissolution of conditioned individuality.

Your Limited Self Has Been Cancelled. Nobody Actually Died.

Now this is absolutely central to the ending of the entire Śiva Sūtras.

Let’s unpack it carefully.

Tad — that; referring to the supreme condition of turya.

Ārūḍha — ascended to, established in.

Pramiti — knowledge, conscious apprehension.

Tat-kṣayāt — through the destruction of that; referring back to desire.

Jīva — the living, conditioned individual.

Saṅkṣayaḥ — complete dissolution, cessation, exhaustion.

Now, Kṣemarāja explicitly explains both instances of tad.

The first refers to the supreme conscious state.

The second refers to the desire described in 3.40.

So the sequence is:

Awareness becomes established in the supreme reality.

The fundamental craving arising from incompleteness subsides.

And the limited condition of the jīva comes to an end.

But here’s the crucial question.

What exactly is being destroyed?

Not the physical body.

Not the existence of consciousness.

Not the possibility of ordinary experience.

What ceases is the identification of the Self with the limited puryaṣṭaka as the ultimate knowing subject.

That is Kṣemarāja’s explanation.

The jīva as the contracted, desire-driven individual ceases to define the Self.

And now we encounter an absolutely beautiful expression.

Ātmārāmatā.

Delight in one’s own Self.

Resting in the fullness of the Self.

The person no longer requires an external object to establish their fundamental completeness.

And Kṣemarāja contrasts this with the continual outward movement of the bound individual.

Now, something fascinating happens.

He cites a tantric passage comparing liberation to waking from a dream.

When you awaken, the objects of the dream are no longer experienced as the actual environment in which you’re situated.

The dream city.

The dream characters.

The dream catastrophe.

Their former apparent authority is withdrawn.

And similarly, the yogi who recognises the supreme reality no longer apprehends saṃsāra through the same fundamental delusion.

But this doesn’t mean that the physical universe disappears for everyone else.

Nor that the enlightened person ceases to perceive ordinary objects.

The analogy concerns the transformation of their conscious standpoint.

The world is no longer experienced as a realm fundamentally separate from Śiva.

And here’s a remarkable detail.

Kṣemarāja’s cited material actually uses the term nirvāṇa.

Yes.

Nirvāṇapada.

The state of nirvāṇa.

Now, we should not automatically equate this with the Buddhist understanding of Nibbāna.

The metaphysical frameworks are substantially different.

In the Buddhist traditions, nirvāṇa is associated with the cessation of greed, hatred and delusion, without positing an eternal universal Śiva-self.

In Trika, Kṣemarāja interprets liberation through recognition of the supreme conscious Self, inseparable from divine freedom and Śakti.

The resemblance in vocabulary is real.

But so is the philosophical difference.

And this is an excellent example of why we need to read the actual Sanskrit rather than assuming a word has exactly the same meaning everywhere.

Kṣemarāja describes the yogi as established in kaivalya because they are no longer carried along by the senses and subtle elements.

The individual no longer remains a helpless passenger within the machinery of conditioned existence. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now here’s the extraordinary result.

The body may continue.

The senses may function.

The world may appear.

But the fundamental sense of being an incomplete individual driven outward by desire has been overcome.

And this takes us to one of the most beautiful metaphysical corrections in the entire work.

Because if the jīva has been dissolved, why is the liberated person still walking around?

Surely their body should vanish?

Shouldn’t they immediately ascend into some luminous cosmic dimension?

Well.

Vasugupta has thought of that.

And the answer is wonderful.

The body remains like a cloak.
