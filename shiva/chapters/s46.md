# Śiva Sūtras 3.14

यथा तत्र तथान्यत्र

Yathā tatra tathānyatra.

As there, so elsewhere.

Doesn’t Matter Where You Go, Śiva’s Already There.

Now, this is a magnificent little statement.

Four words.

And Kṣemarāja uses it to explain the universality of the freedom just described.

Let’s unpack it.

Yathā — just as, in whatever manner.

Tatra — there.

Tathā — so, likewise.

Anyatra — elsewhere.

As there, so elsewhere.

Now the question is:

What exactly is being compared?

Kṣemarāja answers by referring to the yogi’s manifestation of freedom.

Just as the yogi experiences the divine power of manifestation in their own body, so that power is understood to operate elsewhere.

The practitioner who is steadily established in recognition doesn’t confine freedom to one particular location.

And the traditional interpretation includes an extraordinary claim.

The realised yogi is said to be capable of manifesting divine freedom beyond the limits of their own bodily situation.

That is part of the commentary’s metaphysical and yogic claim, rather than merely a poetic statement about feeling peaceful while travelling.

But it also has a deeply illuminating nondual significance.

Because if consciousness is the fundamental reality of the manifest universe, then its nature isn’t ultimately restricted to one particular physical location.

Think about the ordinary individual.

They experience themselves as situated here.

Within this body.

At this time.

Surrounded by things located elsewhere.

And these distinctions are perfectly meaningful within empirical experience.

But the Trika claim is that the universal conscious reality is not ultimately divided by those spatial distinctions.

The limited standpoint is localised.

The supreme reality is not.

And the realised yogi recognises that distinction.

Now consider the practical implication without exaggerating the supernatural claims.

Suppose someone has a profound meditative experience.

They sit quietly.

Their mind becomes still.

There’s an extraordinary sense of openness.

The usual sense of separation temporarily relaxes.

And they conclude:

“Wonderful. This is where I experience Śiva.”

Then they leave the meditation room.

Traffic.

Noise.

People.

Problems.

And suddenly:

“Ah, unfortunate. Śiva has apparently remained inside the meditation hall.”

But if the fundamental nature of consciousness is not confined to that place, why would recognition be restricted to those particular conditions?

The same reality is present through the changing circumstances of experience.

The task is the stabilisation of recognition, not the construction of an exclusively sacred geographical enclosure.

And this is precisely where Kṣemarāja connects the sūtra to the yogi’s sustained attention.

The manifested freedom is described as present wherever the yogi remains established in that recognition.

He cites the Svacchandatantra and the Spandakārikā, emphasising the natural, nonartificial character of this freedom. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, compare this with the first book’s teaching that the entire seen world is the body of the realised Self.

The same metaphysical movement appears again.

The limited individual ordinarily identifies consciousness with a particular organism.

But recognition reveals its universal nature.

And therefore the yogi no longer fundamentally confines the Self to the individual body.

Now, this doesn’t mean ordinary physical constraints magically disappear for everyone who understands the doctrine.

You still need to eat.

You still need to sleep.

You still need to respect other people’s boundaries.

And attempting to walk through a wall because you’ve read a Sanskrit aphorism is not an advisable method of testing nondual metaphysics.

But within the tradition, the deepest freedom of consciousness is not limited by the ordinary spatial identity of the finite subject.

As there, so elsewhere.

The same Śiva.

The same Śakti.

The same fundamental reality.

And now we’ve reached the final sūtra of this instalment.

And after all this talk about divine freedom, cosmic dancing and the universality of consciousness, Vasugupta has one last instruction.

It’s astonishingly simple.

But perhaps one of the most practically important in the entire third section.

Pay attention to the seed.
