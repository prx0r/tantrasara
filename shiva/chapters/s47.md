# Śiva Sūtras 3.15

बीजावधानम्

Bījāvadhānam.

Attention to the seed.

The Whole Universe Comes from One Seed, and You Keep Getting Distracted.

Oh, that’s lovely.

Absolutely lovely.

We’ve travelled through fifteen sūtras.

We’ve examined the limited mind.

The conditions of bondage.

The five kañcukas.

The dissolution of the universe through contemplation.

The regulation of vital movement.

The dangers of supernatural powers.

The awakening of innate knowledge.

The cosmic theatre.

The attainment of divine freedom.

And now Vasugupta brings us back to something exceptionally direct.

Bījāvadhānam.

Attention to the seed.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Bīja — seed, germ, source.

Avadhāna — attention, attentiveness, concentration.

And Kṣemarāja gives us a beautifully precise interpretation.

The seed is Parā Śakti.

The supreme power.

The source of the entire manifested universe.

And its nature is sphurattā — the luminous, vibrant manifestation of self-aware consciousness.

Now, let’s think about that.

Ordinarily a seed is something small.

You plant it.

It grows.

And eventually an entire tree emerges.

The tree develops branches.

Leaves.

Flowers.

Fruit.

And within those fruits, further seeds.

The seed appears almost insignificant compared with the vast complexity that unfolds from it.

But it contains, in a particular causal sense, the potential for the subsequent development.

Now, Kṣemarāja is using the seed as a metaphysical principle.

Not suggesting that there is a tiny physical object somewhere inside your skull containing miniature galaxies.

The seed is the divine power from which manifestation proceeds.

And that power is not independent of consciousness.

It is Parā Śakti.

The supreme creative power of Śiva.

Now, here’s the beautiful part.

Kṣemarāja explains that even after the attainment of freedom, the yogi should not become udāsīna — indifferent or inattentive.

Instead, they must repeatedly direct the mind towards the seed.

The commentary uses the expression:

भूयो भूयश्चित्तनिवेशनम्।

Bhūyo bhūyaś citta-niveśanam.

Repeatedly placing or establishing the mind.

That’s Kṣemarāja’s own explanatory wording.

And it is absolutely crucial.

Because it explains why the text doesn’t simply end after announcing the attainment of freedom.

You might imagine that once the yogi has recognised divine consciousness, there’s nothing left to discuss.

Congratulations.

Done.

Roll credits.

Everybody goes home.

But Kṣemarāja insists upon continuing attention to the supreme source.

Why?

Because the teaching concerns the stable establishment of recognition, not merely a passing experience.

Remember the end of Book II?

विद्यासंहारे तदुत्थस्वप्नदर्शनम्।

Vidyāsaṃhāre tadutthasvapnadarśanam.

When pure knowledge subsides, dreamlike differentiation reappears.

That was our warning.

You can recognise the truth and then become absorbed once again in limited cognition.

And now, in Book III, we find the corresponding instruction.

Return your attention to the seed.

Again.

And again.

Not necessarily by repeating a particular verbal formula endlessly.

But by sustaining contemplative attention towards the fundamental power of consciousness.

Kṣemarāja identifies the seed as the cause of the universe and cites the Netratantra on the supreme Goddess as the source of the gods and divine powers, characterised symbolically through fire and moon. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now this is where the symbolism becomes magnificent.

Fire and moon.

Power and luminosity.

The varied differentiations through which divine manifestation is described.

The supreme Śakti is the source from which the innumerable powers proceed.

But the practitioner is instructed to attend not merely to their countless differentiated expressions.

Rather, to the source.

The seed.

And notice how this relates to everything we’ve studied.

At 3.1, the Self in its contracted condition was identified with mind.

At 3.2, limited knowledge was bondage.

At 3.3, Māyā was associated with the failure to discern the principles of manifestation.

Then the practitioner began the work of contemplation.

The body was dissolved into its causal principles.

The senses and subtle faculties became instruments of practice.

The whole universe was recognised as the manifestation of consciousness.

The yogi attained the understanding of divine freedom.

And now the mind, which was initially bound by its own limited operation, becomes directed towards the very source of manifestation.

The mind that was absorbed in the branches turns towards the seed.

And here there’s a wonderful relationship with Śāktopāya.

Remember:

चित्तं मन्त्रः।

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

Not every wandering thought automatically functioning as a liberating formula, but mental activity transformed through contemplation of the supreme reality.

And now:

बीजावधानम्।

Bījāvadhānam.

Attention to the seed.

The mind becomes directed towards Parā Śakti.

The source of the entire universe.

The power through which manifestation arises.

The consciousness in which the entire process appears.

Now, consider an ordinary practical analogy.

You sit down to meditate.

You recognise the presence of awareness.

A thought appears.

You follow it.

A memory appears.

You follow that.

A plan appears.

You begin constructing tomorrow.

A worry appears.

You begin solving a problem that doesn’t currently exist.

And approximately twenty minutes later, you’re mentally having an argument with a man you haven’t seen since secondary school.

Remarkable.

You’ve travelled through an entire personal universe without leaving your cushion.

But now the instruction is:

Return to the source.

Not by violently suppressing every mental event.

Not by trying to construct an artificial blankness.

But by directing attention towards the conscious reality through which all those events become manifest.

Again.

And again.

And again.

The point is not that each return creates consciousness.

Consciousness was never absent in the first place.

The practice concerns recognition.

And the stabilisation of that recognition.

This is where Trika becomes extremely practical.

Because the metaphysics is enormous.

Thirty-six tattvas.

Divine powers.

Phonemic cosmology.

Subtle bodies.

Worlds within worlds.

But the fundamental orientation is beautifully simple.

The entire manifestation arises through Śakti.

And the practitioner turns towards its source.

Not as some distant object.

But as the conscious power fundamental to their own existence.

And now the cosmic theatre becomes almost unbearably beautiful.

The dancer is Śiva.

The stage is the inner being.

The spectators are the senses.

The drama is the manifestation of the universe.

And the seed is the supreme power through which the entire production becomes possible.

The actor has been performing for so long that he’s become absorbed in the role.

He thinks he’s the character.

He worries about the plot.

He fights with the other characters.

He clings to the scenery.

He becomes terrified that the performance might end.

And then something shifts.

He recognises the actor.

He recognises the stage.

He recognises the spectators.

He recognises the divine power through which the whole performance appears.

And the drama can continue.

But the fundamental misunderstanding is beginning to dissolve.

The performance no longer conceals its source.

Bījāvadhānam.

Attention to the seed.

What a way to finish this first stretch of Āṇavopāya.

The Great Reveal — What Did Vasugupta Actually Just Do?
Fifteen sūtras.

And the progression is astonishingly coherent.

Think about where we began.

आत्मा चित्तम्।

Ātmā cittam.

The Self is mind.

The apparently limited individual identifies with the mental apparatus.

Knowledge reinforces the distinction between subject and object.

Māyā sustains the misrecognition of the principles of manifestation.

And the individual remains entangled in conditioned existence.

But then the practices begin.

शरीरे संहारः कलानाम्।

Śarīre saṃhāraḥ kalānām.

The manifested principles are contemplated in their withdrawal towards their source.

The body becomes a field of practice.

The subtle faculties and vital powers become instruments of concentration.

And the yogi begins moving beyond the ordinary identification with differentiated manifestation.

But there is a danger.

मोहावरणात्सिद्धिः।

Mohāvaraṇāt siddhiḥ.

Extraordinary powers can remain within delusion.

The practitioner must not mistake unusual experiences for recognition of the supreme.

Then the deeper knowledge arises.

मोहजयादनन्ताभोगात्सहजविद्याजयः।

Mohajayād anantābhogāt sahajavidyājayaḥ.

The conquest of delusion culminates in the mastery of innate knowledge.

And now the world becomes transformed in meaning.

It is recognised as the radiance of consciousness.

Then the extraordinary theatrical sequence begins.

The Self is the dancer.

The inner self is the stage.

The senses are the spectators.

And refined intelligence discloses the subtle inner nature of consciousness.

Finally:

सिद्धः स्वतन्त्रभावः।

Siddhaḥ svatantrabhāvaḥ.

Freedom is attained.

Not merely personal autonomy.

But recognition of the supreme divine freedom of consciousness.

And this freedom is not restricted to a single particular locus.

यथा तत्र तथान्यत्र।

Yathā tatra tathānyatra.

As there, so elsewhere.

But the yogi must not become inattentive.

The supreme source remains the object of contemplative orientation.

And therefore:

बीजावधानम्।

Bījāvadhānam.

Attention to the seed.

The Most Beautiful Thing About This Section
There’s something extraordinary about the structure of the three books.

Book I began:

चैतन्यमात्मा।

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

Book II began:

चित्तं मन्त्रः।

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

Book III began:

आत्मा चित्तम्।

Ātmā cittam.

The Self is mind.

Three statements.

Three different standpoints.

But not three ultimately separate realities.

From the highest standpoint, the Self is universal consciousness.

From the standpoint of Śakti, mental activity can become a vehicle of divine recognition.

From the standpoint of the limited individual, the Self appears contracted into identification with the mind.

And the entire practice involves recognising the relationship between these conditions.

The point is not to create a supreme Self somewhere beyond the individual.

Nor is it to turn the individual ego into an infinitely powerful superhuman.

It’s to recognise the conscious reality through which the individual, its mind, its body, and the entire universe become manifest.

And the most beautiful part?

The limited individual is never actually outside Śiva.

Even the ignorance belongs to the manifestation of consciousness.

Even the effort belongs to Śakti.

Even the practices are expressions of divine power.

And even the forgetting occurs within the reality that can never be fundamentally absent.

The dancer has never ceased to be the dancer.

But he has become absorbed in his performance.

The recognition isn’t a new actor entering the stage.

It’s the original actor recognising his own nature.

And the play continues.
