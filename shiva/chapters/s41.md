# Śiva Sūtras 3.9

नर्तक आत्मा

Nartaka ātmā.

The Self is the dancer.

Śiva Is Literally Doing a One-Man Show, and You’re Every Character.

OH, THIS IS BEAUTIFUL.

Absolutely beautiful.

Two words.

Nartaka.

Dancer.

Ātmā.

Self.

The Self is the dancer.

Now, given everything we’ve just learned, consider what this means.

The universe is the manifestation of consciousness.

The apparent objects are its radiance.

The individual is consciousness experienced under conditions of contraction.

And so what is the relationship between Śiva and the manifest universe?

A performance.

A dance.

A cosmic drama.

The Self is the dancer.

And here’s the really extraordinary part.

The dancer doesn’t simply stand outside the performance controlling it from a distance.

The dance is the dancer’s own activity.

The movement is not an independently existing substance.

It’s the dancer expressing their power.

And this is precisely the kind of relationship Trika describes between Śiva and Śakti.

Śiva is not an inert observer watching Śakti do all the work.

Śiva’s very nature is inseparable from the freedom of his power.

Prakāśa and vimarśa.

The luminosity of consciousness and its reflexive self-awareness.

The manifestation of reality is not foreign to this unity.

It’s its expression.

Now imagine an actor on a stage.

He plays a king.

The king has a kingdom.

A family.

An enormous palace.

A terrible enemy.

And an extraordinarily complicated political situation.

The actor performs every emotion.

Anger.

Love.

Fear.

Triumph.

Loss.

And for the duration of the drama, an entire world becomes manifest through the performance.

But the actor is not ultimately identical with the limited role.

The king can die.

The actor remains.

The kingdom can collapse.

The actor remains.

The play can end.

The actor remains.

And now Vasugupta takes this analogy and applies it to consciousness.

The waking world.

The dream world.

The apparent absence of differentiated experience in deep sleep.

These are different modes of the cosmic performance.

And the Self is the dancer moving through them.

Kṣemarāja explicitly explains the sūtra through the manifestation of different roles across the states of consciousness.

The yogi recognises the underlying Self as the actor displaying these changing conditions through its own powers.

He supports the interpretation with references to the Naiśvāsatantra, Bhaṭṭa Nārāyaṇa’s Stavacintāmaṇi and the Īśvarapratyabhijñā. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And here’s the profound complication.

Ordinarily, the actor becomes identified with the role.

The individual says:

“I am this person.”

“This is my story.”

“These are my successes.”

“These are my failures.”

“These are the terrible things that happened to me.”

And the role becomes the apparent totality of existence.

Imagine an actor who becomes so absorbed in playing Hamlet that he forgets he’s an actor.

He wanders around after the performance convinced that Denmark is in crisis.

He’s still fighting with Claudius.

Still mourning his father.

Still contemplating the skull.

Somebody tries to offer him a sandwich.

“BEGONE, WRETCH, THE KINGDOM IS LOST.”

Mate.

You’re in a theatre.

The performance finished twenty minutes ago.

That’s an imperfect but helpful analogy for the structure of contraction.

The actor becomes absorbed in a particular form of its own manifestation.

Recognition is the disclosure of the actor’s deeper nature.

But there is something even more subtle here.

Because in an ordinary theatre, the actor and the audience are separate people.

The stage is an independent physical structure.

The script has been written by somebody else.

And the actor is constrained by circumstances that exist independently of him.

But in the Trika metaphysical vision, the supreme reality is the source of the entire performance.

The actor.

The activity.

The manifested world.

The powers through which it is experienced.

All belong to the same consciousness.

And that means the cosmic theatre is not merely an analogy for pretending.

It’s an analogy for manifestation through freedom.

The performance is real as manifestation.

But its deepest reality is not separate from its source.

And the actor is none other than Śiva.

Now here’s something worth appreciating.

The sūtra doesn’t say:

“The Self is a spectator sitting motionless while the universe dances.”

It says:

The Self is the dancer.

An active principle.

A manifesting power.

And this is what makes the Trika understanding of consciousness so distinctive.

The divine is not merely pure existence stripped of all activity.

It possesses svātantrya.

Freedom.

Its manifestation is līlā — play.

Not necessarily frivolous amusement, but spontaneous divine expression.

And the realised yogi recognises the relationship between the changing performance and the conscious actor.

Which means that ordinary life doesn’t have to be rejected as spiritually meaningless.

The performance can continue.

The actor can still play the role.

But now the role is understood through its source.

Imagine doing something ordinary.

Walking.

Eating.

Talking.

Listening.

Instead of identifying entirely as the individual agent moving through a separate world, the yogi recognises the activity as a manifestation of conscious power.

The dance continues.

But the dancer is recognised.

And now, naturally, we need a stage.

Because even Śiva can’t put on a production without somewhere to stand.

Apparently the cosmic theatre has a technical rider.
