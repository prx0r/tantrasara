# Śiva Sūtras 3.17

स्वमात्रानिर्माणमापादयति

Svamātrānirmāṇam āpādayati.

The yogi brings about manifestation according to the measure of their own consciousness.

Congratulations, the Universe Is Now Made to Your Measurements.

Right.

We’ve emerged from the Great Lake.

Feeling refreshed.

Lovely immersion.

Very peaceful.

And Vasugupta informs us that the realised yogi can now manifest forms according to the power of consciousness.

Naturally.

Let’s unpack the compound.

Sva — one’s own.

Mātrā — measure, dimension, determining aspect.

Nirmāṇa — production, formation, construction, manifestation.

Āpādayati — brings about, accomplishes, causes to arise.

The idea is that the yogi manifests according to svamātrā, the measure belonging to their own consciousness.

But Kṣemarāja doesn’t leave mātrā as a vague expression.

He gives a fascinating explanation.

It is a contracted or condensed aspect of the essence of consciousness.

Cidrasāśyānatā.

A kind of solidification of the essence of consciousness.

Not literal physical solidification, of course.

This is a metaphysical description.

But the imagery is extraordinary.

Imagine consciousness as unlimited, self-luminous reality.

And now imagine forms appearing through particular differentiations of that reality.

Objects.

Subjects.

Bodies.

Worlds.

The undivided reality manifests determinate forms.

Kṣemarāja connects this to the appearance of both vedya and vedaka.

The knowable.

And the knower.

Now this is important.

We’re not simply saying that consciousness produces an external object which a separate subject subsequently encounters.

The entire relation between subject and object belongs to manifestation.

The knower and the known are both included in the field of Śakti’s expression.

And here’s where the text becomes especially beautiful.

Kṣemarāja cites the Svacchandatantra to explain that one reality can appear in gross and subtle forms according to its limiting conditions.

Then he turns to the Pratyabhijñā tradition.

And finally to the relationship between water and ice.

Now, think about ice.

Ice has a definite structure.

It can appear as a cube.

A sheet.

A beautifully sculpted swan at an unnecessarily extravagant wedding.

And you can describe those forms as distinct objects.

But none of them is a substance fundamentally alien to water.

The differentiation of form doesn’t establish an independent material reality.

That’s the direction of the analogy.

Within Trika, the entire manifest universe is understood as the expression of consciousness through its own powers.

And the realised yogi is said to recognise, and in the traditional account exercise, the creative freedom underlying these manifestations.

Kṣemarāja even invokes the Spandakārikā on the yogi who experiences the entire universe as divine play while remaining established in recognition.

And he connects this with jīvanmukti.

Liberation while living. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, let’s be precise.

Kṣemarāja’s commentary really does speak in terms of manifestation according to will.

We shouldn’t quietly rewrite his claim into:

“You can imagine things creatively.”

That would be a much weaker statement.

But neither should we mistake the traditional claim for an experimentally established ability to create physical objects by thought.

The commentary’s metaphysical point is that the manifesting power of consciousness is not ultimately external to the realised Self.

And here’s the wonderful reversal.

In ordinary life, the individual experiences a world that seems to confront them from outside.

The world is already there.

The person is thrown into it.

They encounter objects.

They react to circumstances.

And the entire situation appears governed by powers that are not their own.

But in the supreme recognition described here, the yogi apprehends the world as manifestation of their own universal conscious nature.

Not of their private personality.

Not of their ordinary ego.

But of consciousness understood through the freedom of Śiva.

And this is where the water-and-ice analogy becomes especially powerful.

The ice doesn’t have to cease existing for its reality as water to be recognised.

Likewise, the world doesn’t have to physically disappear for its nondual nature to become apparent.

The form can remain.

The recognition changes.

You were staring at the ice.

Now you understand the water.

And suddenly Vasugupta makes an even more extraordinary declaration.

Because if the yogi is established in this knowledge, apparently the entire cycle of rebirth comes to an end.

Now that’s quite an administrative achievement.
