# Śiva Sūtras 3.45

भूयः स्यात्प्रतिमीलनम्

Bhūyaḥ syāt pratimīlanam.

Again and again, there is the complete reunion of inward and outward awareness in the supreme Self.

The Greatest Plot Twist: You Were Śiva the Entire Bloody Time.

And that’s it.

The final sūtra.

The seventy-seventh statement.

Vasugupta has spent the entire text explaining the nature of consciousness, the structure of bondage, the powers of Śakti, and the means of liberation.

And he ends with:

Bhūyaḥ syāt pratimīlanam.

Now we need to be exceptionally careful with this one.

Because the word pratimīlana is doing enormous philosophical work.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Bhūyaḥ — again, repeatedly, abundantly.

Syāt — may be, should be, comes to be.

Pratimīlanam — closing together, folding together, reunion or complete integration.

And Kṣemarāja gives it a very particular interpretation.

He connects pratimīlana with two complementary movements.

Nimīlana.

Closing inward.

The withdrawal of consciousness from differentiated manifestation into its own nature.

And:

Unmīlana.

Opening outward.

The manifestation of the universe through that same consciousness.

Now think about what that means.

Ordinarily, spiritual practice can appear to involve a movement away from the world.

You withdraw.

You close your eyes.

You turn attention inward.

You seek the source.

And in some traditions, that inward withdrawal becomes the principal image of liberation.

But Trika gives us something more comprehensive.

Because the supreme reality isn’t merely a state of withdrawal.

Śiva possesses the freedom of manifestation.

And therefore the outward opening is not ultimately separate from the inward source.

Nimīlana and unmīlana are not two different absolute realities.

They’re complementary movements of one consciousness.

And pratimīlana is the integration of both.

Now, Kṣemarāja’s explanation is extraordinary.

He says the universe arises from the essential nature of consciousness.

Then, for the yogi established in supreme yoga, the universe is folded back into that conscious reality.

But this doesn’t necessarily mean the physical world disappears.

The crucial movement is the disappearance of the residual impression of duality.

Bhedasaṃskāra.

The latent impression of difference.

That impression falls away.

And the yogi recognises the universe as not fundamentally separate from consciousness.

Now here’s the beautiful thing.

Kṣemarāja explains that the inward closing and outward opening become aspects of the same supreme recognition.

When awareness is directed inward, the yogi recognises consciousness.

When the universe appears outwardly, the yogi recognises that appearance as the manifestation of consciousness.

The distinction between inward and outward no longer conceals their fundamental unity.

And this is precisely why pratimīlana is such a perfect ending.

Because the entire text has been concerned with the relation between the Self and manifestation.

At the beginning:

चैतन्यमात्मा।

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

Then:

ज्ञानं बन्धः।

Jñānaṃ bandhaḥ.

Limited knowledge is bondage.

And now, at the end:

भूयः स्यात्प्रतिमीलनम्।

Bhūyaḥ syāt pratimīlanam.

Repeated integration into the supreme conscious reality.

The apparent separation between subject and object has been overcome.

But not through the permanent annihilation of the world.

Rather, through recognition of the world as the manifestation of the Self.

And Kṣemarāja makes the central point absolutely explicit.

He says the yogi’s Śiva-nature is not something new.

Nāpūrvam.

Not newly produced.

Rather:

Svabhāva eva.

It is their own essential nature.

And this is the ultimate revelation.

The yogi doesn’t become Śiva by creating an entirely new metaphysical identity.

The yogi recognises the Śiva-nature that was always fundamental.

Māyā had obscured it through the power of differentiated mental construction.

The practitioner had experienced themselves as limited.

The body appeared to define the Self.

The senses appeared to encounter a separate world.

The mind became absorbed in differentiated cognition.

Desire produced the movement outward.

And the individual wandered through conditioned existence.

But the supreme nature of consciousness had never ceased to be the reality underlying the entire process.

And Kṣemarāja explicitly says that the methods described throughout the work serve to reveal this already-existing Śiva-nature.

Not manufacture it.

Reveal it.

And now comes a magnificent metaphor from the Svacchandatantra, quoted by Kṣemarāja.

The image is of fire.

Fire emerging from wood.

Once kindled, the fire becomes brilliantly luminous.

And the liberated Self is compared to this emergence.

The fire isn’t created as an entirely alien substance imposed from outside.

The analogy is used to explain the disclosure of a nature previously concealed by limiting conditions.

And Kṣemarāja cites the Svacchandatantra on the yogi who has become free from the impurities and limiting powers.

Even while remaining within manifestation, the liberated person is no longer bound by it in the former manner.

Then he concludes the entire commentary by explaining the final sūtra’s deepest point.

The Śiva-nature of the yogi was not previously absent.

It was obscured through the self-concealing power of Māyā.

And the practices have brought it to manifestation in recognition. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now stop and think about the structure of the whole journey.

The first sūtra:

Consciousness is the Self.

The final sūtra:

The inward and outward movements of manifestation are reunited in consciousness.

We’ve gone from the declaration of the supreme reality to its recognition throughout the entire universe.

And that is the full movement of the Śiva Sūtras.

Not away from existence.

Not towards an entirely new substance.

Not from an independent human being into a separate divine being.

But from misrecognition to recognition.

From contraction to the disclosure of freedom.

From the limited aṇu to the recognition of the universal Self.

And the final instruction isn’t:

“Destroy the world.”

It’s not:

“Suppress every thought.”

It’s not:

“Abandon every relationship.”

It’s not:

“Become an exceptionally impressive individual.”

It’s something far more radical.

Recognise the conscious reality through both the inward withdrawal and outward manifestation of the universe.

And there’s an exquisite irony here.

Because the whole text has been explaining how to become what, according to the tradition, you never actually ceased to be.

You were Śiva.

You experienced limitation.

You searched for Śiva.

You studied Śiva.

You meditated upon Śiva.

You worshipped Śiva.

You asked teachers about Śiva.

And eventually, the distinction between the seeker and the sought was overcome.

The ultimate metaphysical hide-and-seek.

With one player.

And an infinite number of disguises.

THE GRAND FINALE — ALL SEVENTY-SEVEN ŚIVA SŪTRAS
What Vasugupta Actually Just Told Us
Right.

The book is finished.

Seventy-seven sūtras.

Three sections.

And an astonishingly unified philosophical argument.

But now that we’ve followed everything, we can finally stand back and see the whole structure.

Because there’s something extraordinarily elegant about it.

The Śiva Sūtras don’t simply begin with a human being and tell them how to become divine.

They begin by declaring the nature of the divine Self.

Then they explain why that nature appears concealed.

And finally they show how recognition can occur through several distinct modes of practice.

The movement is not from one independent substance to another.

It’s from contracted awareness to recognition of its universal nature.

And the three upāyas approach this from different standpoints.

॥ शिवम् ॥
Iti śivam.
Thus, Śiva. Thus, the auspicious conclusion.
End of the Śiva Sūtras — all 77 sūtras completed.
