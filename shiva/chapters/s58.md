# Śiva Sūtras 3.26

शरीरवृत्तिर्व्रतम्

Śarīravṛttir vratam.

The continuance or activity of the body is the vow.

Being Alive Is Your Religious Practice. Don’t Make It Weird.

Now this is beautiful.

Because we’ve reached an extraordinary transition.

Earlier, the practitioner was engaged in definite practices.

Meditation.

Concentration.

Mantra.

Contemplation.

The discipline of the body and senses.

But now the yogi has become established in recognition of Śiva.

So what becomes of religious practice?

Vasugupta says:

Śarīravṛttir vratam.

The body’s continuation is the vow.

Let’s unpack it.

Śarīra — body.

Vṛtti — activity, functioning, continuation, maintenance.

Vrata — vow, religious observance, sacred discipline.

And Kṣemarāja gives a specific explanation.

The liberated yogi remains embodied.

They continue to experience the circumstances of bodily existence.

And the continuation of that body becomes their sacred observance.

Now this is not an invitation to do whatever you like and call it Tantra.

That’s a misunderstanding worth preventing immediately.

The sūtra does not mean that every impulse of the body automatically constitutes enlightened conduct.

Nor does it abolish responsibility for harmful action.

Kṣemarāja is describing the condition of a yogi whose identity is already established in the supreme reality.

Their life has become inseparable from their recognition of Śiva.

And therefore the ordinary continuation of embodiment is no longer fundamentally separate from worship.

Think about the conventional structure of a religious vow.

You undertake a particular discipline.

Perhaps fasting.

Perhaps recitation.

Perhaps a form of worship.

You establish a special period or activity dedicated to the divine.

There’s ordinary life.

And then there’s the vow.

But Kṣemarāja describes a state in which that separation has been transformed.

The yogi remains devoted to the supreme consciousness through the very continuation of embodied existence.

Living itself becomes a sacred expression.

And the commentary explicitly mentions the traditional understanding that the remaining circumstances of embodiment are allowed to run their course.

The yogi doesn’t have to destroy the body.

Or treat bodily survival as a spiritual failure.

Kṣemarāja also invokes a tantric analogy involving fire, vital energy and the Self, in which the embodied condition is related to the attainment of supreme Śiva.

And he draws on the Trikasāra tradition to explain how ordinary bodily and ritual activity can be transformed through recognition. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, here’s what I find particularly beautiful.

The yogi has ceased to regard the body as the ultimate Self.

But that doesn’t make the body worthless.

The body becomes an expression within consciousness.

And therefore its continued activity can be understood through its divine source.

This is an enormously affirmative position.

It’s not:

“My body is disgusting, and I must escape it.”

Nor:

“My body is the supreme and complete identity.”

It’s:

“My body is a manifestation of consciousness, and its activity can occur without obscuring the recognition of that consciousness.”

And this creates an extraordinary possibility.

Eating can become an expression of sacred life.

Walking.

Resting.

Breathing.

Speaking.

Acting.

Not because each activity automatically produces liberation.

But because the liberated yogi is no longer fundamentally estranged from the divine nature of manifestation.

The life itself becomes permeated by recognition.

And here’s the lovely reversal.

Earlier, bodily identification was a central aspect of bondage.

Now bodily continuation is a vow.

Same body.

Different standpoint.

The body that once seemed to imprison consciousness becomes the field through which its freedom is expressed.

And this prepares us for one of the most wonderful sūtras in the entire work.

Because Vasugupta now extends the same transformation to speech.

And the result is absolutely hilarious when translated literally.

Conversation becomes mantra-recitation.

Which means that apparently the enlightened yogi can go down to the market, ask for some vegetables, and still be engaged in worship.

Now that’s efficient.
