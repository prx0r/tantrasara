# Śiva Sūtras 3.21

मग्नः स्वचित्तेन प्रविशेत्

Magnaḥ svacittena praviśet.

Being immersed, one should enter with one’s own mind.

Use Your Mind to Enter the State Beyond Your Mind. Brilliant. Thanks.

Right.

Let’s clarify the problem.

We want to enter turya.

The fourth state.

We’ve been instructed to infuse it into waking, dreaming and deep sleep.

And now Vasugupta tells us to enter with our own mind.

But hang on.

Wasn’t the mind the instrument of limitation?

Didn’t Book III literally begin:

आत्मा चित्तम्।

Ātmā cittam.

The Self is mind.

Wasn’t that the problem?

And now we’re supposed to use the mind to transcend the limitations of mind?

Yes.

Exactly.

Welcome to Āṇavopāya.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Magnaḥ — immersed, submerged.

Sva — one’s own.

Cittena — with the mind or consciousness, instrumental singular.

Praviśet — one should enter, from pra-viś, to enter into.

Now, Kṣemarāja explains that the practitioner must enter through an inwardly directed, nonconceptual awareness.

And here’s the important bit.

He specifically discusses relinquishing reliance on the grosser procedures of practice.

Breath regulation.

Meditation.

Concentration.

The deliberate operations employed in earlier stages.

Why?

Because once the practitioner has approached the conscious source through these methods, the final entry isn’t accomplished simply by multiplying techniques.

Imagine using a ladder to climb onto a roof.

The ladder is extremely useful.

You need it.

You climb.

Step by step.

But when you’ve reached the roof, you don’t continue climbing the ladder indefinitely.

At some point you have to step off.

Otherwise you’re going to end up standing on the top rung, clutching the ladder, asking whether there’s a more advanced ladder available.

This is the situation Kṣemarāja is describing.

The methods prepare and orient the practitioner.

But the recognition of consciousness itself cannot be reduced to another mechanical operation.

Now, he quotes the Netratantra concerning the movement beyond gross and subtle forms of prāṇāyāma towards the supreme spanda.

And this is enormously important.

Because spanda doesn’t simply mean ordinary physical vibration.

Nor is it merely the sensation of energy moving around the body.

It’s a technical term associated with the dynamic, self-revealing nature of consciousness.

The living pulse of awareness.

And Kṣemarāja places this supreme reality beyond the gross and subtle movements of vital energy.

So even the highly refined experiences associated with breath are not the ultimate goal.

The practitioner moves beyond identification with them.

Then, through inward awareness, enters the supreme condition.

And here he uses the term avikalpaka.

Without conceptual differentiation.

Not unconscious.

Not stupid.

Not a blank stone.

But awareness no longer structured around the ordinary conceptual divisions of subject and object.

And now comes the really beautiful phrase.

Antarvimarśa-camatkāra.

The astonishing delight of inward reflexive awareness.

We’re back to vimarśa.

Consciousness knowing itself.

And camatkāra.

Wonder.

The extraordinary delight in that recognition.

Remember the theatre?

The actor.

The stage.

The spectators.

Now the practitioner is entering the conscious reality underlying the entire performance.

Not by finding one more object to observe.

But by releasing the identification that made the body, breath and mental faculties seem to constitute the ultimate experiencer.

And Kṣemarāja explicitly describes the dissolution of the notion that the body and vital energy are the true knowing subject.

He cites the Svacchandatantra and Vijñānabhairava, as well as a hymn attributed to Pradyumnabhaṭṭa, in support of this inward transformation. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now think about the apparent paradox.

The mind is used to direct attention.

But what is ultimately recognised is not merely another mental object.

It’s the consciousness through which the mind itself appears.

You can investigate a thought.

You can analyse a sensation.

You can contemplate a mantra.

You can examine the structure of an experience.

But eventually the investigation points towards the very reality making the investigation possible.

And the relation between observer and observed changes.

The mind has become a vehicle of recognition.

Not by producing Śiva.

But by becoming transparent to its own conscious source.

And there’s something rather funny about this.

You’ve spent thirty or forty sūtras trying to discover the Self.

You have investigated the tattvas.

Mastered terminology.

Examined phonemes.

Practised concentration.

Contemplated the divine powers.

And now Vasugupta is essentially telling you:

“Right. Now stop clinging to all the machinery.”

“But I spent YEARS learning the machinery.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve made diagrams.”

“Lovely.”

“I’VE GOT A THIRTY-SIX-TATTVA SPREADSHEET.”

“Very impressive. Now enter.”

And that’s the teaching.

The intellect can help orient recognition.

But it cannot substitute for recognition itself.

The map has been useful.

Now, apparently, we’re going swimming again.
