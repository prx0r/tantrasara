# Śiva Sūtras 3.38

त्रिपदाद्यनुप्राणनम्

Tripadādyanuprāṇanam.

The three states are to be enlivened by their primary source, the fourth.

Now this is a very beautiful sūtra.

And it deliberately returns us to an earlier teaching.

Remember 3.20?

त्रिषु चतुर्थं तैलवदासेच्यम्।

Triṣu caturthaṃ tailavad āsecyam.

The fourth should be poured into the three like oil.

And now:

Tripadādyanuprāṇanam.

The enlivening of the three by their source.

But Kṣemarāja makes an important distinction.

At 3.20, the discussion centred on the pervasion of waking, dreaming and deep sleep by turya.

Here he interprets the three through the dynamics of:

Sṛṣṭi — manifestation.

Sthiti — maintenance.

Laya — withdrawal.

And the primary condition underlying all three is:

Turya.

The fourth.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Tri — three.

Pada — state, position, condition.

Ādi — first, primary, fundamental.

Anuprāṇana — enlivening, vitalising, animating.

The instruction concerns the infusion of the three conditions with the awareness of their supreme source.

Now, here’s the truly fascinating part.

Kṣemarāja associates the three with distinct orientations of experience.

The outward movement towards objects.

The involvement with those objects.

And their inward withdrawal.

Manifestation.

Maintenance.

Dissolution.

Consider what happens when you encounter something beautiful.

A piece of music, for example.

First, the music begins.

A sound emerges.

Your attention turns towards it.

Sṛṣṭi.

Manifestation.

Then the music continues.

You become absorbed in it.

Perhaps the melody becomes extraordinarily moving.

Sthiti.

Maintenance.

Then the phrase ends.

The final note fades.

The experience subsides.

Laya.

Withdrawal.

Now here’s the extraordinary claim.

These three movements can become occasions for recognising the fourth.

Turya.

The underlying conscious reality.

And Kṣemarāja says that even when this reality is concealed by Māyā, it can flash forth for an instant.

Like lightning.

Vidyutvat.

Like a flash of lightning.

Now this is a gorgeous image.

Because sometimes an experience produces a sudden opening.

A moment of extraordinary joy.

A feeling of fullness.

A profound sense of wonder.

And for an instant, the ordinary structures of mental limitation can seem to relax.

Kṣemarāja says the practitioner can use such moments to enliven their recognition of the supreme state.

But the aim is not merely to chase increasingly intense pleasures.

That would simply return us to rāga.

Wanting.

Clinging.

Searching for completeness in another object.

Rather, the yogi attends to the underlying conscious reality disclosed through the experience.

And now Kṣemarāja cites the Vijñānabhairava.

This is particularly important because the examples are surprisingly diverse.

He invokes practices involving intense delight, the joy of meeting a beloved person after a long separation, the pleasure of eating and drinking, the experience of music, and even the heightened states associated with sexual enjoyment.

Yes.

The traditional text really does include sensual and erotic examples.

This is Tantra.

We’re not going to pretend it exclusively concerns sitting quietly with a nice cup of herbal tea.

But the philosophical point isn’t simply indulgence.

It’s the recognition of the conscious fullness that can become apparent through powerful experiences.

And Kṣemarāja also invokes the Spandakārikā on intense states such as anger, joy, uncertainty and rapid movement.

The common principle is that an unusual intensity of experience may disclose a deeper conscious power when approached through the appropriate contemplative understanding. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now let’s be precise.

This is not a claim that every intense emotion automatically reveals enlightenment.

Nor that sexual pleasure, anger or excitement are inherently liberating.

The relevant point is the conscious standpoint of the practitioner and the possibility of recognising the source of experience.

Here’s the beauty of it.

Imagine listening to a piece of music that moves you profoundly.

For a moment, the usual sense of being a separate subject evaluating an external object may become less prominent.

The experience becomes extraordinarily full.

Now, you can chase that fullness as something produced exclusively by the external music.

Or you can investigate the conscious reality through which the fullness is experienced.

And Kṣemarāja’s argument points towards the latter.

The delight becomes an occasion for recognising the source.

But it must not remain merely an isolated flash.

Remember the lightning.

The momentary opening.

The practitioner is instructed to cultivate the continuity of recognition.

To enliven the successive phases of experience with the conscious reality of turya.

This is why the word anuprāṇana is so beautiful.

It’s not simply suppressing experience.

It’s enlivening it.

The ordinary processes of manifestation, maintenance and withdrawal become pervaded by recognition.

And now we reach the next sūtra.

Which extends the method even further.

Not merely to particular mental states.

Not merely to extraordinary moments of joy.

But to the body.

The senses.

And the external world.

Because apparently no part of experience is permitted to remain outside this recognition.

The entire universe is getting spiritually saturated.
