# Śiva Sūtras 3.3

कलादीनां तत्त्वानामविवेको माया

Kalādīnāṃ tattvānām aviveko māyā.

Māyā is non-discernment concerning the principles beginning with Kalā.

Māyā Has Given You Five Absolutely Terrible Subscription Plans.

Now we’re getting into the serious machinery of Kashmir Śaivism.

Because Kṣemarāja is not merely going to say:

“The world is an illusion, mate.”

That would be an extremely inadequate account of Trika.

Instead, he explains Māyā through the system of tattvas.

The principles or categories of manifestation.

Thirty-six of them.

A complete metaphysical architecture describing how universal consciousness manifests as limited subjectivity and the world of differentiated experience.

And at a crucial point in that architecture, we encounter Māyātattva.

The sixth tattva.

Followed by five limiting powers called kañcukas.

Literally, coverings or sheaths.

And these are magnificent.

Because each represents a contraction of something that, in the supreme condition of Śiva, is unlimited.

Let’s meet them.

1. Kalā — Limited agency.

Not to be confused with kāla, time.

Kalā here is the limitation of the power of action.

In the supreme condition, Śiva possesses unrestricted freedom of manifestation.

But the individual experiences:

“I can only do certain things.”

“I have limited abilities.”

“I want to accomplish this, but I can’t.”

The infinite power of action is experienced under limitation.

Congratulations.

You’ve been promoted from the universal agent to a person who can’t find his keys.

2. Vidyā — Limited knowledge.

In its supreme condition, consciousness is not restricted by the epistemic limitations of an individual standpoint.

But the contracted being knows particular things.

And doesn’t know other things.

“I know what’s in front of me.”

“I don’t know what’s happening in the next room.”

“I don’t know what tomorrow will bring.”

“I don’t know why the Wi-Fi has stopped working.”

An infinite field of possible knowing has been contracted into a particular cognitive perspective.

3. Rāga — Wanting, attachment, incompleteness.

This one’s especially nasty.

Because the limited individual experiences desire through a sense of lacking something.

“I need that.”

“I’ll be complete when I get this.”

“If that person loves me, everything will finally be okay.”

The fullness of consciousness is experienced as incomplete.

So now the individual begins searching outside themselves for something that will remove the sense of deficiency.

Which is a rather unfortunate undertaking.

Because if the deficiency is rooted in the primordial contraction of consciousness, no particular external acquisition can, by itself, completely undo it.

You can buy another item.

Achieve another goal.

Receive another compliment.

And the basic structure of wanting may simply reappear.

The cosmic shopping cart has no checkout button.

4. Kāla — Time.

Now we’ve got time.

Past.

Present.

Future.

The individual experiences reality through temporal succession.

“I was.”

“I am.”

“I will be.”

The universal freedom of consciousness is now experienced within ordered moments.

You’re no longer simply examining manifestation.

You’re late for something.

Which is perhaps the most humiliating metaphysical contraction of all.

Infinite consciousness.

Late for a bus.

5. Niyati — Restriction, order and necessity.

Finally, there is limitation through ordered circumstances.

You are situated here rather than there.

Events occur within particular relations and conditions.

Your actions have consequences.

You cannot simply disregard the circumstances through which your individual life unfolds.

In the Trika system, niyati involves the restrictions associated with causal and spatial order.

And so the unrestricted nature of Śiva is experienced through a network of definite constraints.

Now consider what has happened.

The supreme consciousness possesses freedom of knowledge and action.

But the individual experiences limited agency, limited knowledge, desire, time and necessity.

This is the architecture of contraction.

And Kṣemarāja’s explanation goes beyond the five kañcukas.

He identifies the principles extending from Kalā down to Pṛthivī, the earth principle, as components of the differentiated embodied condition.

The problem is aviveka.

Non-discernment.

Failing to recognise the real nature and relations of these principles and their dependence on the supreme reality.

And here’s the subtlety.

The tattvas themselves aren’t independent evil substances.

They are principles of manifestation.

The problem is the ignorance under which their differentiated forms are taken to constitute an ultimately separate reality.

Kṣemarāja explicitly raises an objection from the Vijñānabhairava: if knowledge itself is luminous, how can knowledge be bondage?

His answer turns on recognition. Through divine grace, even limited cognition can be known as light; without that recognition, Māyā’s differentiating power sustains bondage. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

So Māyā is not merely someone throwing a blanket over consciousness.

She’s the divine power through which unity is experienced as multiplicity without its underlying unity being recognised.

And that’s why the doctrine is so radical.

The limitation isn’t something that has escaped Śiva’s sovereignty.

It’s a manifestation of that sovereignty.

The universe has not fallen outside the Absolute.

But the individual is experiencing the Absolute through contracted conditions.

And now Vasugupta is going to explain what to do about it.

Apparently the solution is to dissolve the entire cosmos inside your body.

Naturally.
