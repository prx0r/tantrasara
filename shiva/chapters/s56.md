# Śiva Sūtras 3.24

मात्रास्वप्रत्ययसन्धाने नष्टस्य पुनरुत्थानम्

Mātrāsvapratyayasandhāne naṣṭasya punarutthānam.

Through uniting the objects of experience with awareness of one’s true Self, what was lost arises again.

You Forgot You Were Śiva. Here’s How to Remember Without Running Back to Your Cushion.

Right.

This is one of the most practically significant instructions in the entire work.

Because we’ve just seen how the recognition of turya can become interrupted by ordinary mental activity.

And now Vasugupta says it can be restored.

But the method is fascinating.

It’s not necessarily a withdrawal from the objects of experience.

It’s a transformation of the relationship between those objects and consciousness.

Let’s unpack the compound.

Mātrā — measure or measured object; here the objects of cognition.

Sva — one’s own.

Pratyaya — cognition, apprehension, recognition.

Sandhāna — joining, connecting, bringing into union.

Naṣṭasya — of that which was lost.

Punarutthānam — rising again, reappearance, restoration.

The critical term is svapratyaya.

In Kṣemarāja’s interpretation, it concerns the recognition of the true I-consciousness.

Not the ordinary individual ego.

Not merely thinking your personal name.

But awareness of the Self as the conscious reality underlying manifestation.

And when the objects of experience are brought into relation with that recognition, the previously obscured bliss of turya reappears.

Now let’s understand the actual method.

A sound arises.

Ordinarily, you might simply follow the sound outward.

You identify it.

Judge it.

React to it.

Construct a story around it.

But the practitioner can instead apprehend the sound through the recognition of consciousness.

Not:

“This sound is a completely independent thing confronting a fundamentally separate subject.”

But:

“This sound is appearing within the luminous reality of consciousness.”

Now, be precise.

The sound isn’t merely being imagined away.

The object remains an object within the conventional structure of experience.

But its ultimate metaphysical status changes.

It is recognised as a manifestation of consciousness.

And Kṣemarāja’s commentary is very explicit about this.

He cites the Svacchandatantra, instructing the practitioner to investigate the supreme reality through whatever the eyes perceive, speech expresses, mind thinks, intellect determines or ego appropriates.

That’s an extraordinary list.

Because it means the entire apparatus of ordinary cognition can become a field of recognition.

Seeing.

Speaking.

Thinking.

Judging.

Even the activity of ego-appropriation.

The practitioner doesn’t have to begin by annihilating all these faculties.

They can become occasions for investigating the conscious source.

And now comes an even more radical teaching.

Kṣemarāja cites further passages from the Svacchandatantra explaining that the yogi should recognise the supreme reality wherever the mind moves.

Wherever it goes.

Not only during meditation.

Not only inside a shrine.

Not only while repeating a mantra.

Wherever cognition occurs.

And the explanation is profoundly nondual.

If the whole universe is understood as pervaded by Śiva, where can the mind actually go that is ultimately outside Śiva?

That’s the point. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And now imagine this.

You’re walking through a market.

There’s a noise.

You become distracted.

You start thinking about something that needs doing.

A memory arises.

An emotion appears.

Then you remember the teaching.

Instead of treating the thought as evidence that you’ve departed from some sacred reality, you investigate the consciousness in which it appears.

The thought itself becomes an occasion for recognition.

Not because every thought is automatically liberating.

But because the practitioner can recognise its manifestation within the conscious source.

This is extraordinarily important.

Because otherwise spiritual practice can become a strange dualism.

There’s the sacred state.

And the ordinary state.

There’s meditation.

And there’s life.

There’s Śiva.

And there’s everything that distracts me from Śiva.

But if the world is Śiva’s own manifestation, that division cannot be ultimate.

The point is not to manufacture a private spiritual experience insulated from reality.

It’s to recognise reality through all its appearances.

And here we can see why Śāktopāya was so important.

चित्तं मन्त्रः।

Cittaṃ mantraḥ.

The mind is mantra.

Mental activity can become an instrument of recognition when its source is understood.

And now:

मात्रास्वप्रत्ययसन्धाने नष्टस्य पुनरुत्थानम्।

Through union between objects and true Self-awareness, what was lost returns.

Notice the structure.

The practitioner has become distracted by objects.

But the objects themselves can become the means through which recognition is restored.

The supposed obstacle becomes the occasion for liberation.

The very cognition that previously sustained limitation can now disclose its source.

It’s extraordinary.

Imagine somebody searching frantically for the door out of a room.

They examine every wall.

Every corner.

Every possible escape route.

Eventually, somebody points out that the wall they’ve been leaning against contains the door.

The door was not created by the discovery.

But the relationship to the room changes completely.

And that’s the kind of reversal being described.

The practitioner doesn’t have to escape from manifestation to find consciousness.

Manifestation itself can disclose consciousness.

And this is where Kṣemarāja’s interpretation becomes particularly beautiful.

Recognition is recoverable through the very world that seemed to obscure it.

But once that recognition becomes fully established, what happens to the yogi?

Apparently he becomes like Śiva.

Oh, excellent.

We’ve reached the promotion ceremony.
