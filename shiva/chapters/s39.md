# Śiva Sūtras 3.7

मोहजयादनन्ताभोगात्सहजविद्याजयः

Mohajayād anantābhogāt sahajavidyājayaḥ.

Through boundless conquest of delusion comes mastery of innate knowledge.

Defeat Māyā, Unlock the Infinite, and Discover the Knowledge You Never Actually Lost.

Right.

Now we’re getting somewhere.

Because after warning us about siddhis, Vasugupta gives the actual direction of the practice.

Not the accumulation of extraordinary abilities.

But the conquest of delusion and the manifestation of sahajavidyā.

Innate knowledge.

Let’s unpack the compound.

Moha — delusion.

Jaya — conquest, victory.

Ananta — endless, boundless.

Ābhoga — expansion, extension, pervasive reach.

Sahaja — innate, natural, born together with.

Vidyā — knowledge.

Jayaḥ — conquest or mastery.

And this word sahaja is absolutely beautiful.

Because it doesn’t mean you’ve constructed something entirely new.

It points to something intrinsic.

Something belonging to your own nature.

So what is the knowledge being revealed?

Kṣemarāja identifies it with the recognition of the supreme nature of consciousness, particularly through the movement towards Śivavyāpti.

And now we need to introduce two extremely important terms.

Ātmavyāpti.

Śivavyāpti.

Vyāpti means pervasion or permeation.

Ātmavyāpti is the attainment or recognition of one’s own conscious Self, beyond the limiting identifications constituting ordinary individuality.

Śivavyāpti is the fuller recognition of the supreme Śiva-nature, including the universal manifestation and divine powers.

These are technical levels within the doctrinal explanation, not merely two interchangeable synonyms for having a nice meditation.

Kṣemarāja, citing the Svacchandatantra, distinguishes them.

The practitioner first moves beyond identification with the bonds of limited existence.

But the movement does not end with the recognition of an isolated inner consciousness.

It culminates in the universal recognition of Śiva’s nature.

And this is where Trika differs sharply from interpretations that describe liberation simply as the discovery of a passive witnessing principle separate from the world.

For Trika, the supreme consciousness is not merely a witness.

It is svatantra.

Free.

Creative.

Self-revealing.

The source of manifestation.

So the fullest recognition is not simply:

“I am the awareness watching everything.”

It’s the much more radical understanding:

“The reality of awareness is inseparable from the divine power through which everything manifests.”

Now, Kṣemarāja connects this movement with Unmanā.

A state beyond ordinary conceptual mind, associated with the supreme divine condition.

And here’s an important distinction.

Ordinary knowledge proceeds through successive cognitions.

You know one thing.

Then another.

Then another.

But the supreme knowledge described here isn’t merely the accumulation of many individual cognitions.

It is the nondual recognition of the universal conscious reality.

The yogi does not become liberated by collecting every piece of information in the cosmos.

You don’t need to memorise the entire contents of Wikipedia.

Thank God.

You’ve already got enough Sanskrit to learn.

What is required is a transformation of the fundamental standpoint of knowing.

Kṣemarāja describes the transition from limited self-recognition towards the universal condition of Śiva, emphasising that Āṇavopāya can culminate in Śāktopāya and the fuller awakening of innate knowledge. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And now, ladies and gentlemen, something extraordinary happens.

Because Vasugupta is about to explain what the world looks like when this knowledge has arisen.

And the answer is absolutely gorgeous.

The universe becomes the yogi’s own light.

Not an enemy.

Not an illusion to be despised.

Not a foreign territory from which consciousness needs to escape.

The world becomes the radiance of the Self.

Which means we’re about to enter one of the most beautiful passages in the entire Śiva Sūtras.

The cosmic theatre.

And Śiva, the absolute show-off, is taking centre stage.
