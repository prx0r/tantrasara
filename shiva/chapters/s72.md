# Śiva Sūtras 3.40

अभिलाषाद्बहिर्गतिः संवाह्यस्य

Abhilāṣād bahirgatiḥ saṃvāhyasya.

Through desire, the one carried along moves outward.

Congratulations, You’ve Reinstalled Wanting. Welcome Back to Saṃsāra.

Oh, this is absolutely devastating.

Because after everything we’ve learned about the extraordinary nature of consciousness, Vasugupta brings us back to a very simple human predicament.

I want something.

That’s it.

The entire cosmic drama begins again.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Abhilāṣa — desire, longing, wanting.

-āt — because of, arising from.

Bahis — outward.

Gatiḥ — movement, going.

Saṃvāhya — one who is carried along, conveyed or driven onward.

Now that last word is particularly important.

Saṃvāhya.

The one who is carried.

Kṣemarāja interprets this as the bound individual who is carried from one womb or embodied condition to another through the powers constituting conditioned existence.

The paśu.

The bound being.

The karmātmā we encountered in 3.35.

And the powers carrying this individual include the kañcukas, mental faculties, senses, subtle elements and gross elements.

An entire metaphysical conveyor belt.

Absolutely extraordinary.

You’ve got the limited individual standing on the conveyor belt, looking around, asking:

“Where are we going?”

And the machinery replies:

“Another embodied existence.”

“Why?”

“Desire.”

“Can I get off?”

“Have you recognised your true nature?”

“Not really.”

“NEXT WOMB, PLEASE.”

Now that’s the traditional saṃsāric framework, not an empirically demonstrated mechanism of rebirth.

But Kṣemarāja’s philosophical explanation is very precise.

He connects abhilāṣa with āṇavamala.

The primordial limitation of the individual.

And specifically with the feeling:

Apūrṇammanyatā.

The conviction of incompleteness.

Let’s unpack that.

A-pūrṇa — not full, incomplete.

Manyatā — considering, regarding, thinking oneself to be.

The individual experiences:

“I am incomplete.”

And from that fundamental orientation arises desire.

“I need this.”

“I must obtain that.”

“I cannot be fulfilled until something changes.”

And therefore consciousness becomes outwardly directed.

Bahirgati.

The movement towards objects.

Now here’s the extraordinary subtlety.

Trika doesn’t simply say that every ordinary preference is morally evil.

Wanting a meal when you’re hungry is part of embodied life.

The deeper issue is the metaphysical identification through which the subject experiences itself as fundamentally deficient.

The limited individual mistakes the acquisition of objects for the ultimate recovery of completeness.

And this is where the whole system becomes remarkably coherent.

Remember rāga among the five kañcukas?

The limitation associated with desire.

The supreme fullness of consciousness is experienced through the condition of lack.

And that apparent lack drives the individual outward.

The person searches among objects for a completeness that, according to the doctrine, belongs to their own supreme conscious nature.

Now imagine somebody convinced that happiness lies in obtaining one particular thing.

A relationship.

A possession.

A professional achievement.

A certain status.

And perhaps they obtain it.

Wonderful.

For a moment, satisfaction arises.

But then another desire appears.

Or anxiety about losing what they’ve acquired.

Or comparison with somebody who has something better.

And the cycle begins again.

The problem isn’t necessarily the object.

It’s the structure of seeking completeness from something conceived as ultimately external.

Kṣemarāja is talking about this structure at a much deeper metaphysical level.

He describes the limited individual as becoming outwardly oriented precisely because they fail to recognise the inward reality of turya as their own Self.

He cites the Svacchandatantra and Kālikākrama on ignorance, desire, differentiated mental construction and the continuation of suffering. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And here’s the beautiful philosophical reversal.

The object may be perfectly ordinary.

A cup of tea.

A beautiful place.

A person you love.

But the underlying mode of experience can differ radically.

One person encounters the object through the conviction:

“I am incomplete, and this thing must complete me.”

Another encounters it without mistaking the object for the ultimate source of fulfilment.

The difference is not necessarily in the outward action.

It’s in the fundamental identification of the subject.

And now Vasugupta gives us the solution.

Not simply suppressing all desire by force.

Not constructing a new ego whose proudest achievement is having no desires.

But the disclosure of fullness through stable recognition of consciousness.

And when that recognition becomes established, the condition of being the limited individual is said to dissolve.

Which sounds alarming.

Because we’re about to have a sūtra involving the destruction of the jīva.

But don’t worry.

Nobody needs to die.

This is about the end of a particular mode of self-identification.
