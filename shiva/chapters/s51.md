# Śiva Sūtras 3.19

कवर्गादिषु माहेश्वर्याद्याः पशुमातरः

Kavargādiṣu māheśvaryādyāḥ paśumātaraḥ.

The Mothers of bound beings, beginning with Māheśvarī and the others, preside over the phonemic classes beginning with the ka-group.

The Sanskrit Alphabet Has Eight Divine Mothers, and They’re All Watching You.

OH, HERE WE GO.

THE MOTHERS ARE BACK.

And I cannot overstate how interesting this sūtra is.

Because we’ve already encountered the divine power of the Sanskrit alphabet several times.

In Book I:

ज्ञानाधिष्ठानं मातृका।

Jñānādhiṣṭhānaṃ mātṛkā.

Mātṛkā is the foundation of differentiated knowledge.

In Book II:

मातृकाचक्रसम्बोधः।

Mātṛkācakrasambodhaḥ.

Complete awakening to the wheel of Mātṛkā.

And now, in Book III, Vasugupta is telling us that the phonemic powers are associated with divine Mothers who govern the limited beings.

Which raises the obvious question.

WHY IS THE ALPHABET TRYING TO ENSLAVE ME?

Excellent question.

Let’s begin with the Sanskrit.

Ka-varga — the group of consonants beginning with ka.

Ādiṣu — among those beginning with, and the following groups.

Māheśvarī — the goddess Māheśvarī.

Ādyāḥ — those beginning with her, and the others.

Paśu — the bound individual.

Mātaraḥ — mothers.

Now, Kṣemarāja invokes the Mālinīvijayatantra to explain the emergence of phonemic differentiation.

And the argument is absolutely fascinating.

Śakti is one.

But she possesses the freedom to manifest in many forms.

As icchāśakti, she is will.

As jñānaśakti, she is knowledge.

As kriyāśakti, she is action.

These powers are not fundamentally separate divine substances.

They’re expressions of one conscious power.

But through their manifestation, differentiated objects and cognitions arise.

And here’s the important step.

Language articulates these differentiations.

You encounter an object.

You recognise it.

You name it.

You evaluate it.

You develop a relationship towards it.

And the phonemic powers are understood as participating in this entire process.

Kṣemarāja describes Mātṛkā as permeating cognition through subtle and gross verbal articulation.

Now, think about your own experience.

You see somebody.

Immediately a word appears.

“Friend.”

“Enemy.”

“Beautiful.”

“Annoying.”

“Stranger.”

And the word doesn’t simply sit there neutrally.

It may become involved in an entire structure of attraction, aversion, memory and expectation.

“This person is dangerous.”

“This person can make me happy.”

“This person is responsible for my unhappiness.”

And suddenly you’re living inside a universe of emotionally charged conceptual distinctions.

The remarkable thing about Kṣemarāja’s position is that he treats this not merely as a psychological habit but as a manifestation of divine phonemic powers.

The Mothers.

Now, within the tantric system, the Sanskrit letters are organised into groups.

And these groups are associated with goddesses.

One traditional eightfold classification, explained in the source tradition, includes:

Yogīśvarī, associated with the vowel group.
Brāhmī, associated with the ka-group.
Māheśvarī, associated with the ca-group.
Kaumārī, associated with the ṭa-group.
Vaiṣṇavī, associated with the ta-group.
Vārāhī, associated with the pa-group.
Aindrī, associated with the semivowels.
Cāmuṇḍā, associated with the sibilants and related final phonemes.
This is a particular tantric correspondence, not an assertion that every Indian tradition assigns identical goddesses to identical letters.

And Kṣemarāja’s commentary is more intricate than this simple list.

He discusses the division into phonemic seeds and matrices, the multiplicity of the powers of speech, and their relation to the fifty letters and their presiding Rudras.

The whole thing is like discovering that your ordinary alphabet is actually a metaphysical government with several departments and an alarming number of female executives.

And every department reports to Śakti.

But here’s the crucial point.

These goddesses can function as powers of bondage.

The same Mātṛkā whose true nature can be recognised as supreme consciousness also supports differentiated cognitions that conceal that nature.

And Kṣemarāja adds a particularly uncomfortable warning.

Even someone who has attained recognition can become confused again through inattention.

The phonemic powers can reassert their binding influence.

Think about that.

You’ve had the most profound realisation of your life.

You understand that the universe is Śiva.

You’ve recognised the nature of awareness.

Everything is luminous.

Everything is extraordinary.

And then somebody says:

“You’re wrong.”

Immediately, a concept arises.

Wrong.

Then another.

Me.

Then another.

Disrespect.

Then:

I MUST DEFEND MYSELF.

And before you know it, you’re writing an eight-thousand-word reply proving why your spiritual interpretation is superior.

The Mothers have got you.

Again.

Now, this doesn’t mean the goddesses are literally malicious entities in Kṣemarāja’s metaphysical account.

They are powers of Śakti.

But when their differentiating activity operates without recognition of the underlying unity, the individual becomes bound by the distinctions they manifest.

Kṣemarāja specifically connects their operation with emotional states such as amazement, joy, fear, attachment and aversion.

And he explains how the power of speech can conceal unrestricted consciousness beneath the apparent identity of a dependent, embodied individual. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

This is why the sūtra belongs exactly here.

At 3.18, we discovered that the continuity of innate knowledge ends rebirth.

And now Kṣemarāja says:

Careful.

If that recognition subsides, the differentiated powers of language can once again sustain limitation.

The problem isn’t the existence of words.

It’s the failure to recognise the conscious power manifesting through them.

And this creates one of the most beautiful reversals in the entire work.

Mātṛkā first appears as the foundation of bondage.

Then as the object of liberating awakening.

Then as a power that can reintroduce limitation when the practitioner becomes inattentive.

She hasn’t fundamentally changed.

Your relationship with her has.

The Goddess can appear as the source of your confusion.

Or as the revelation of your own deepest nature.

Same Śakti.

Different recognition.

And Vasugupta now gives us an absolutely extraordinary practical metaphor for keeping that recognition alive.

He’s going to pour the fourth state of consciousness into the other three.

Like oil.

Yes.

We’re now lubricating enlightenment.
