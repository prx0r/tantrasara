# Śiva Sūtras 3.31

स्थितिलयौ

Sthitilayau.

Maintenance and dissolution.

Congratulations, You’re Not Just Creating the Universe. You’re Also Maintaining and Deleting It.

That’s it.

Two words.

And Vasugupta has decided that the entire cosmic process is now your responsibility.

Fantastic.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Sthiti — standing, remaining, maintenance, preservation.

Laya — dissolution, absorption, withdrawal.

Sthitilayau is a dual compound referring to those two processes.

Now this sūtra cannot be properly understood by itself.

And Kṣemarāja explicitly makes that clear.

It continues the previous statement:

Svaśaktipracayo ’sya viśvam.

The universe is the unfoldment of the realised yogi’s own divine power.

So 3.31 adds:

Maintenance and dissolution are also expressions of that same power.

Ah.

Now we’ve got the full cosmic operation.

Sṛṣṭi — manifestation.

Sthiti — maintenance.

Laya — withdrawal.

Creation, preservation and reabsorption.

But let’s be careful with creation.

The Trika doctrine is not necessarily proposing that Śiva manufactures a universe out of something independent of himself.

That would leave us with two fundamental realities.

Śiva.

And the material from which Śiva constructs the universe.

Which would be rather embarrassing for a nondual metaphysics.

Instead, the universe is understood as an ābhāsa.

A manifestation or appearance within and through consciousness.

And Kṣemarāja describes these processes in exactly that framework.

Manifestation is the coming forth of differentiated appearances through kriyāśakti, the divine power of action.

Maintenance is their continuing outward manifestation.

And dissolution is their repose in the conscious subject.

Now here’s the crucial thing.

When an object disappears from experience, it does not follow that consciousness has disappeared.

Imagine looking at a candle.

The flame is present.

You perceive it.

Then somebody blows it out.

The flame disappears.

Your perception of that particular flame ends.

But the consciousness through which its appearance and disappearance were known is not thereby annihilated.

Now don’t take the candle analogy too literally.

Kṣemarāja is not simply explaining ordinary visual persistence.

He’s giving a metaphysical account of the relation between manifestation and the conscious knower.

The point is that both appearance and withdrawal belong to Śakti.

The presence of an object doesn’t establish an independent reality outside consciousness.

And its disappearance doesn’t indicate that consciousness has lost something essential to itself.

The entire movement is understood through the conscious power of manifestation.

Kṣemarāja even invokes the distinction between asti and nāsti.

“It is.”

“It is not.”

The apparent presence and absence of things.

And he connects both with the operations of Śakti.

Now imagine a terrible situation.

Your favourite cup breaks.

Immediately:

“NOOOOO.”

The cup has existed in your experience.

It has persisted through time.

And now the particular arrangement of matter has been destroyed.

And your mind announces:

“A great tragedy has occurred.”

Which may be quite reasonable.

It was a lovely cup.

But from the standpoint of Trika metaphysics, the coming forth, duration and dissolution of phenomena all belong to the dynamic manifestation of consciousness.

The supreme reality is not made complete by the cup’s existence.

Nor is it diminished by its destruction.

And Kṣemarāja extends this to the whole universe.

Manifestation.

Maintenance.

Dissolution.

All are expressions of the same divine power.

He cites the Kālikākrama and a verse concerning the liberated person who recognises pure, independent consciousness as their own I-awareness. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now the extraordinary thing is that 3.30 and 3.31 have together established an entire cosmology.

The world unfolds as Śakti.

It persists through Śakti.

It is withdrawn through Śakti.

Nothing has escaped the sovereignty of the supreme consciousness.

Which sounds magnificent.

But an objection immediately arises.

And it’s a very good objection.

If consciousness is manifesting these different conditions, doesn’t it change?

Doesn’t it become one thing during creation, another during maintenance, and something else during dissolution?

And if it changes in that manner, how can it possibly be the eternal, self-revealing reality we’ve been talking about?

Well.

Vasugupta has an answer.

And it’s absolutely devastating.

Next comes the final fifteen sūtras of the entire text.


And the first two are wonderfully compact:

स्थितिलयौ।

Sthitilayau.

Maintenance and dissolution.

तत्प्रवृत्तावप्यनिरासः संवेत्तृभावात्।

Tatpravṛttāv apy anirāsaḥ saṃvettṛbhāvāt.

Even while those powers operate, the yogi does not lose their nature as the conscious knower.

We’re about to discover what happens when the universe continues manifesting, sustaining and dissolving — while the liberated yogi remains established in recognition.

Then comes the final treatment of pleasure and pain, the disappearance of bondage, the relationship between body and consciousness, the subtle movement of breath, and Vasugupta’s concluding teaching.

Finish the Śiva Sūtras — 3.31–3.45.


The Grand Finale: How Śiva Creates the Universe, Forgets He’s Doing It, Gets Upset About Absolutely Everything, and Finally Remembers That the Whole Bloody Thing Was His Idea.


Previously, on the Śiva Sūtras…
Ladies and gentlemen.

We’ve arrived.

The final fifteen sūtras.

Seventy-seven statements in total. Three great openings of consciousness. An entire metaphysical system compressed into what amounts to a particularly aggressive collection of Sanskrit fortune cookies.

And what a journey it’s been.

In Book I, Vasugupta informed us that we’re actually infinite consciousness.

Marvelous.

In Book II, we discovered that our own mind can become mantra, the alphabet is secretly a goddess, and the entire universe is apparently a rather elaborate manifestation of divine freedom.

Excellent.

Then came Book III.

And the situation deteriorated considerably.

It turns out that this infinite consciousness has somehow become identified with a limited mind, trapped itself in a body, developed an extraordinary number of anxieties, and forgotten the entire arrangement.

Śiva has created saṃsāra.

Entered saṃsāra.

Become confused by saṃsāra.

And apparently started buying books about how to escape saṃsāra.

Absolutely magnificent levels of self-inflicted administrative difficulty.

But then the teaching transformed.

The individual began recognising the divine powers underlying the body, mind and senses.

The world became a cosmic theatre.

The Self was the dancer.

The subtle body was the stage.

The senses were the spectators.

And at 3.30, Vasugupta gave us one of the most extraordinary statements of the entire work:

स्वशक्तिप्रचयोऽस्य विश्वम्।

Svaśaktipracayo ’sya viśvam.

The universe is the unfoldment of his own Śakti.

The universe.

The entire bloody universe.

Not merely an illusion to be discarded.

Not an alien territory fundamentally separate from consciousness.

The manifestation of the free power of Śiva.

But this creates a philosophical problem.

A BIG one.

If the universe is consciousness manifesting its own power, what happens when the universe changes?

When something appears?

When something disappears?

When you’re happy?

When you’re miserable?

When your body dies?

If everything changes, does consciousness change with it?

And if everything is Śiva, why the hell are we still suffering?

These are precisely the problems Kṣemarāja addresses as we approach the conclusion.

And the final sūtras are more sophisticated than they initially appear.

Because we’re going to discover that the disappearance of an experience is not the disappearance of consciousness, that pleasure and pain need not define the Self, and that the very desire to grasp an object can return consciousness to its contracted standpoint.

Then come the breath, the subtle centre, the dissolution of conditioned identity, and the extraordinary final statement.

A folding back.

A closing.

A return.

Except — and this is the ultimate plot twist — the reality to which consciousness returns was never actually absent.

Let’s finish this thing.
