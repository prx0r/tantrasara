# Śiva Sūtras 3.23

मध्येऽवरप्रसवः

Madhye ’varaprasavaḥ.

In the middle, inferior manifestation arises.

You Were Enlightened for Five Minutes, Then Your Brain Started Being a Dick Again.

Oh, this one hurts.

Because it’s absolutely devastatingly relatable.

We’ve just been through an extraordinary sequence.

The yogi enters the Great Lake.

The world is recognised as the expression of Śakti.

Innate knowledge becomes established.

Turya permeates waking, dreaming and deep sleep.

The outward movement of vital activity no longer obscures the conscious source.

Absolutely beautiful.

And then Vasugupta says:

In the middle, inferior manifestation arises.

For God’s sake.

WE WERE DOING SO WELL.

What happened?

Let’s unpack it.

Madhye — in the middle, in the intervening condition.

Avara — lower, inferior, lesser.

Prasavaḥ — production, generation, arising.

And here’s where Kṣemarāja becomes wonderfully precise.

He explains that the practitioner may experience the bliss of turya at the initial and final boundaries of the ordinary states.

But may fail to maintain recognition throughout their middle portions.

And in that intervening condition, ordinary mental activity reasserts itself.

Vyutthāna.

The state of ordinary mental emergence or activity, contrasted with spiritual absorption.

This is not necessarily evil or immoral thought.

It’s the return of limited cognition, particularly when its differentiated contents are no longer recognised through their supreme source.

Now think about how extraordinary this is.

At the transition between states, the practitioner may recognise an opening.

Perhaps as a thought subsides.

Perhaps at the emergence of waking.

Perhaps in a contemplative pause.

But then ordinary mental activity resumes.

And the mind becomes absorbed in its contents.

There’s the perception.

Then the interpretation.

Then the appropriation.

Then the personal story.

Then the emotional reaction.

And suddenly the universe has become a matter of what somebody said to you yesterday.

Śiva has been reduced from universal consciousness to a participant in a completely imaginary argument.

Again.

Now Kṣemarāja’s actual point is not simply that people become annoyed.

That’s our modern illustration.

His concern is the interruption of the yogi’s continuous recognition of turya.

The practitioner can apprehend the fourth state at privileged moments yet fail to sustain it throughout the entirety of experience.

And this is why the word madhya is so important.

The middle.

The interval.

The ordinary continuity of experience.

Not merely its beginnings and endings.

Imagine listening to a symphony.

At the beginning, you become profoundly attentive.

The opening notes are extraordinary.

Then you start thinking about dinner.

Twenty minutes pass.

You become mentally involved in some unrelated matter.

Then the final movement begins.

You suddenly return.

And the ending is magnificent.

Now, you did indeed experience the opening and closing of the symphony.

But you missed much of the performance.

Something analogous is being described here.

Recognition at the boundaries does not necessarily imply uninterrupted recognition throughout the intervening states.

And now comes an important distinction.

Kṣemarāja does not say that the practitioner is permanently lost.

The condition of limitation has reappeared, but the recognition already cultivated has not necessarily become entirely ineffective.

The possibility of returning remains.

He explicitly relates the phenomenon to Book II’s final sūtra, where the subsidence of pure knowledge produces dreamlike differentiated experience.

He also invokes the Mālinīvijayatantra to warn that the yogi can become diverted towards transient enjoyments and extraordinary attainments. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And this is where the text becomes unusually psychologically acute.

Because the mere memory of a profound experience is not identical to living within its recognition.

You can remember what clarity felt like.

You can remember an extraordinary philosophical insight.

You can even describe it with complete precision.

But at the present moment, you may be operating through precisely the limitations that the insight had disclosed.

And then the mind performs another trick.

It begins chasing the previous recognition.

“I need to get back.”

“I’ve lost it.”

“How do I recover the state?”

But the very structure of trying to obtain an experience can reinforce the sense of being a limited subject separated from something desirable.

It’s an exceptionally awkward situation.

You’ve recognised the source.

Then forgotten.

Then turned the memory of recognition into another object of attachment.

Congratulations.

You’ve managed to become attached to freedom.

A spectacular achievement.

But Vasugupta doesn’t leave us here.

Because the very next sūtra explains how recognition returns.

And this one is absolutely beautiful.
