# Śiva Sūtras 3.36

भेदतिरस्कारे सर्गान्तरकर्मत्वम्

Bhedatiraskāre sargāntarakarmatvam.

When difference is overcome, the capacity to create another manifestation arises.

You Beat Duality and Unlock the Universe Editor.

Right.

This is a BIG one.

And we need to handle it properly, because Kṣemarāja is making a genuinely extraordinary claim.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Bheda — difference, division, differentiation.

Tiraskāra — removal, rejection, disappearance, overcoming.

Sarga — creation, manifestation, emanation.

Antara — another, different.

Karmatva — the condition or capacity of acting.

So:

When dualistic difference is overcome, the power of bringing forth another creation is said to arise.

Now, before anybody installs cosmic Minecraft and begins designing a replacement solar system, let’s understand the philosophical progression.

The previous sūtra described the bound individual.

The karmātmā.

Limited by ignorance.

Involved in action.

Subject to karmic consequences.

But Kṣemarāja begins his explanation of 3.36 by introducing the descent of divine grace.

Māheśaśaktipāta.

The descent of the power of the Great Lord.

And through this grace, the individual’s innate svātantrya becomes manifest.

Now, this is where the metaphysics of Trika becomes extraordinarily distinctive.

Because if liberation were simply escape from a separate material universe, the recognition of supreme consciousness would principally concern disengagement from matter.

But in Trika, Śiva is the source of manifestation.

His nature includes the power of creation.

And therefore the recognition of Śiva’s nature is inseparable, in the traditional account, from recognition of that creative freedom.

Kṣemarāja describes the overcoming of dualistic identification with the body, vital energy and other limited conditions.

And then he introduces a hierarchy of conscious subjects.

Sakala.

The ordinary embodied individual.

Pralayākala.

A subtler condition in which certain limitations remain.

And then the higher levels:

Mantra.

Mantreśvara.

Mantramaheśvara.

These are technical categories within the Trika hierarchy of experiencers, associated with progressively less contracted forms of consciousness.

They’re not merely job titles handed out to the best meditators.

Although they sound rather impressive.

“Good morning, what do you do?”

“Mantramaheśvara.”

“Ah. And what does that involve?”

“Cosmic sovereignty.”

“Right. Would you like a receipt?”

Anyway.

Kṣemarāja describes the progressive attainment of these higher conditions as the limitations of difference are overcome.

And the result is the creative capacity described by the sūtra.

Now here’s the part we mustn’t conceal.

Kṣemarāja cites the Svacchandatantra in support of extraordinary powers.

The cited passages claim that the adept can become like Svacchanda, exercise power over divine beings, bestow or remove fear, grant blessings or curses, overcome time’s power, and even bring down mountains.

Yes.

Mountains.

The actual tantric source includes that claim.

Not merely:

“One becomes more psychologically creative.”

Not merely:

“One discovers a fresh perspective on one’s life.”

The commentary really does appeal to supernatural cosmic agency. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, those claims aren’t established physical capabilities.

We shouldn’t pretend they are.

But we also shouldn’t sanitize the historical text into a comfortable modern self-help manual.

The metaphysical reasoning is important.

If universal consciousness possesses unlimited creative freedom, then a yogi identified with that consciousness is said, within the tradition, to participate in its manifesting power.

This is the logic behind the extraordinary claims.

And notice the sequence.

First, bhedatiraskāra.

The overcoming of difference.

Then, sargāntarakarmatva.

The power of another creation.

The power is not presented as simply an advanced technique available to the ordinary ego.

It’s connected with the overcoming of the very dualistic limitation that constitutes ordinary egoic identity.

Which is an important distinction.

Because otherwise the text would simply be saying:

“Remain an ordinary finite person, but acquire increasingly spectacular powers.”

And Kṣemarāja’s entire explanation of bondage would become irrelevant.

The deeper teaching concerns the relationship between knowing, agency and the supreme source.

The extraordinary creative powers are presented as consequences of that recognition in the tantric account.

And now comes a rather fascinating question.

Why should anyone believe consciousness possesses creative power at all?

Forget making mountains fall over.

Forget manifesting additional universes.

What evidence do we have that awareness can generate anything?

Well.

Kṣemarāja points towards something you’ve experienced approximately every night of your life.

Dreaming.

And suddenly the argument becomes very interesting indeed.
