# Śiva Sūtras 1.13

इच्छाशक्तिरुमा कुमारी

Icchāśaktir umā kumārī.

The power of will is Umā, the Maiden.

Śiva’s Girlfriend Turns Out to Be Your Own Will.

Here comes Umā.

Śiva’s consort. The goddess.

And Vasugupta identifies her with icchāśakti — the power of will.

But don’t imagine ordinary personal wanting.

“I fancy some tea.”

“I want a nicer house.”

“I would quite like that person to reply to my message.”

Those are limited expressions of desire within differentiated existence.

The icchāśakti being described here belongs to the realised yogi whose will is united with the supreme freedom of consciousness.

Kṣemarāja associates this power with the divine sovereignty of Parabhairava.

And the word kumārī, maiden, matters.

It points towards the freshness, independence, and unsullied character of this divine power.

Kṣemarāja offers several interpretive possibilities, including her freedom from limiting associations and her identification with the cosmic play of manifestation and withdrawal. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Consider the three fundamental powers of Śakti:

Icchā — will.

Jñāna — knowledge.

Kriyā — action.

In ordinary life, they appear fragmented.

You want something.

You don’t know how to obtain it.

You try to act.

You fail.

You become frustrated.

You make tea.

In the metaphysical description of Śiva, however, will, knowledge and action are powers of the one free consciousness.

They are not independent faculties fighting over resources.

And the underlying freedom in which these powers belong is precisely what was introduced in the first sūtra.

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

Thirteen statements later, we’re discovering what that means for divine agency.
