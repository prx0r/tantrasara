# Śiva Sūtras 3.30

स्वशक्तिप्रचयोऽस्य विश्वम्

Svaśaktipracayo ’sya viśvam.

The universe is the expansion or aggregate of his own power.

The Entire Universe Is Your Own Śakti. Try Not to Get a God Complex.

There it is.

Absolutely magnificent.

Because this sūtra brings together almost everything we’ve studied.

Let’s unpack it.

Sva — one’s own.

Śakti — power, divine energy, the dynamic potency of consciousness.

Pracayaḥ — accumulation, expansion, growth, unfoldment.

Asya — of him, his.

Viśvam — the universe, the whole.

And Vasugupta says:

Svaśaktipracayo ’sya viśvam.

The universe is the expansion of his own Śakti.

Now, before the ego starts getting excited, we need to understand the standpoint.

Because this is not a claim that your ordinary personality is personally responsible for inventing every object in the cosmos.

It’s not:

“I have manifested the universe with my thoughts.”

Nor:

“Everything exists because I personally imagined it.”

That would be a rather unfortunate confusion between limited ego and supreme consciousness.

The relevant sva belongs to the realised yogi whose identity is established in the universal nature of Śiva.

And Kṣemarāja explicitly connects this sūtra with the earlier statement:

शिवतुल्यो जायते।

Śivatulyo jāyate.

One becomes like Śiva.

Because the yogi has attained this recognition, the universe is apprehended as the manifestation of their own divine power.

Not the private power of an isolated human personality.

But the universal Śakti inseparable from Śiva.

And this is why the word pracaya is so important.

The universe is not merely something observed by consciousness.

It is the unfoldment of its power.

Now think about the metaphysical implications.

In some models of consciousness, awareness is described primarily as passive.

It receives appearances.

Observes objects.

Registers experiences.

But in Trika, supreme consciousness is not merely a witness.

It possesses svātantrya.

Freedom.

And this freedom includes the power of manifestation.

Icchā.

Will.

Jñāna.

Knowledge.

Kriyā.

Action.

The entire universe is understood through these divine powers.

And Kṣemarāja explicitly associates the world’s manifestation with kriyāśakti.

The power of action.

Now, here’s the extraordinary move.

The universe isn’t an independent reality that consciousness happens to illuminate.

It is an expression of consciousness through its own manifesting power.

And Kṣemarāja quotes the Kālikākrama tradition to explain that consciousness shines in various forms both inwardly and outwardly.

Internally, through experiences such as pleasure.

Externally, through objects such as a pot.

And in both cases, the ultimate reality is consciousness.

Now let’s understand what that means.

Imagine holding a clay pot.

There’s the pot.

The visual perception.

The tactile experience.

The recognition:

“This is a pot.”

The object appears as something distinct from the perceiver.

And conventionally, that’s perfectly meaningful.

You can fill the pot with water.

You can place it on a table.

You can accidentally drop it.

And discover that nondual metaphysics doesn’t automatically make pottery shatterproof.

But Kṣemarāja is making a different claim.

The very appearance and apprehension of the object belong to consciousness.

The distinction between the object and its cognition does not establish an ultimate independence from the conscious reality through which both are manifest.

And this is where the argument becomes extraordinarily subtle.

Because prakāśa, the light of consciousness, is not merely physical illumination.

It’s the self-revealing nature through which manifestation becomes apparent.

And vimarśa is the reflexive awareness inseparable from that luminosity.

The supreme reality knows itself and manifests its powers.

Therefore the world is not fundamentally alien to consciousness.

It’s the unfoldment of Śakti.

And Kṣemarāja takes this directly back to the doctrine of contraction.

In his exposition, the one conscious reality freely manifests multiplicity and the limitations under which individuals experience themselves as separate.

The limited being is not outside Śiva.

It is Śiva’s consciousness appearing under contraction.

And liberation is the recognition of that conscious source.

He cites the Netratantra and Kālikākrama in explaining this relation between consciousness, its powers, and the apparent world of objects. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, this is where the full philosophical structure becomes extraordinarily beautiful.

At 3.1:

आत्मा चित्तम्।

Ātmā cittam.

The Self is mind.

We began with the limited individual’s identification with the internal cognitive apparatus.

At 3.2:

ज्ञानं बन्धः।

Jñānaṃ bandhaḥ.

Knowledge is bondage.

Differentiated cognition reinforces limitation.

At 3.3:

कलादीनां तत्त्वानामविवेको माया।

Kalādīnāṃ tattvānām aviveko māyā.

Māyā involves the failure to discern the true nature of the principles of manifestation.

And now, twenty-seven sūtras later:

स्वशक्तिप्रचयोऽस्य विश्वम्।

Svaśaktipracayo ’sya viśvam.

The universe is the unfoldment of his own Śakti.

Look at the progression.

The beginning:

“I am a limited individual confronting an external universe.”

The culmination:

“The universe is the manifestation of the divine power inseparable from my true conscious nature.”

But the important thing is that the apparent limited individual has not become a separate creator.

The standpoint has changed.

The underlying reality is recognised.

And this is what makes the teaching so radical.

The universe doesn’t have to disappear for liberation to occur.

The body doesn’t necessarily have to be destroyed.

Ordinary perception doesn’t have to be abolished.

The mind doesn’t have to become incapable of thought.

The senses don’t have to cease functioning.

The entire apparatus of manifestation can continue.

But its fundamental nature is no longer misunderstood.

And that’s the great reversal.

In bondage, the individual experiences the universe as something fundamentally external.

In liberation, the universe is recognised as the expression of consciousness.

The same world.

But not the same understanding.

And this brings us back to the cosmic theatre.

Nartaka ātmā.

The Self is the dancer.

The dancer has been performing through countless forms.

Bodies.

Minds.

Worlds.

Experiences.

Emotions.

The entire drama of birth and death.

The actor appears as the king.

The servant.

The hero.

The villain.

The audience.

And the theatre itself.

And then the actor recognises the nature of the performance.

The universe hasn’t necessarily stopped.

The performance can continue.

But the actor is no longer fundamentally lost in the role.

And now, through this recognition, the entire play is understood as the expression of divine power.

Svātantrya.

Freedom.

Śakti.

Power.

Prakāśa.

Luminosity.

Vimarśa.

Reflexive awareness.

Pratyabhijñā.

Recognition.

The world is not a mistake standing outside consciousness.

It is the extraordinary manifestation of consciousness itself.

And the yogi recognises that the freedom through which it appears is inseparable from their own deepest nature.

Not the freedom of a little ego attempting to control everything.

The freedom of Śiva.

Absolutely magnificent.

The Great Reveal — What Just Happened in Sūtras 3.16–3.30?
Let’s look at this progression, because it’s far too coherent to treat as fifteen unrelated spiritual sayings.

3.16–3.18: Immersion, manifestation and liberation.

The practitioner becomes established in supreme Śakti.

The Great Lake of consciousness is entered.

Manifestation is recognised through the power of the Self.

And the continuity of innate knowledge is said to end the cycle of rebirth.

The movement is from practice towards sustained recognition.

3.19–3.21: The danger of forgetting, and the method of integration.

The phonemic goddesses can reintroduce limitation when recognition subsides.

Therefore turya must be infused into waking, dreaming and deep sleep.

The practitioner enters the supreme condition through inward, nonconceptual awareness, ultimately going beyond reliance on the grosser techniques.

The movement is from intermittent recognition towards continuity.

3.22–3.24: Returning to the world without losing Śiva.

Vital activity unfolds outward again.

Equal vision becomes possible.

But inferior mental activity can reappear in the intervening states.

Through joining the objects of experience with true I-consciousness, the lost recognition can be restored.

The movement is from withdrawal towards recognition within ordinary activity.

3.25–3.29: The transformation of the realised person’s life.

The yogi becomes like Śiva.

Bodily continuation becomes a vow.

Speech becomes mantra-recitation.

Self-knowledge becomes the supreme gift.

And the yogi established in sovereignty over the powers becomes capable of communicating liberating wisdom.

The movement is from personal liberation towards the transformation of action and instruction.

3.30: The universal recognition.

The universe is the unfoldment of Śakti.

What previously appeared as an independent external reality is understood through the freedom of supreme consciousness.

And this returns us to the most fundamental principle of the entire text.

चैतन्यमात्मा।

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

Everything else has been an increasingly detailed exploration of what those two words mean.

The Final Teaching — You Don’t Have to Leave the World to Recognise Śiva
Here’s what makes this sequence so extraordinary.

The yogi first enters the Great Lake.

The supreme source.

The apparently transcendent condition.

But the teaching does not end with immersion.

The practitioner returns to manifestation.

The breath continues.

The body continues.

The senses continue.

The mind continues.

Speech continues.

And the world continues.

But the relationship to all these things has changed.

The yogi no longer regards them as independent realities fundamentally separate from consciousness.

They are recognised as expressions of Śakti.

And this is why the final sequence is so beautiful.

Śarīravṛttir vratam.

The body’s continuation is a vow.

Kathā japaḥ.

Conversation is mantra-recitation.

Dānam ātmajñānam.

The gift is Self-knowledge.

Svaśaktipracayo ’sya viśvam.

The universe is the unfoldment of one’s own divine power.

Look at that.

The body becomes sacred.

Speech becomes sacred.

Instruction becomes sacred.

And the universe itself is recognised as Śakti.

But none of this requires pretending that ordinary empirical distinctions have disappeared.

The body still needs food.

Speech still has meaning.

Teachers still need discernment.

Other people still deserve ethical consideration.

And the universe still appears through countless differentiated forms.

The point is that these forms no longer conceal their conscious source.

In the language of Trika:

The supreme Aham is not ultimately separate from the manifested Idam.

The universal I-consciousness is inseparable from the world appearing as “this.”

And therefore the realised yogi does not necessarily need to flee manifestation.

They recognise its divine nature.

The dancer continues dancing.

The stage remains.

The spectators are watching.

And now the actor knows who he is.

Not the character.

Not the costume.

Not the story.

The conscious reality through which the entire performance becomes possible.

And the most extraordinary part?

According to Trika, that reality was never absent.

Not during bondage.

Not during confusion.

Not during suffering.

Not during forgetting.

The limitation was a contraction of its own power.

The liberation is recognition of its nature.

The entire business has been Śiva.

Playing hide-and-seek.

With Śiva.

Inside Śiva.

Using Śiva’s own Śakti to hide himself from himself.

And then writing seventy-seven sūtras explaining how he did it.

An absolutely outrageous amount of effort for a game he invented.

But apparently infinite freedom does have its hobbies.
