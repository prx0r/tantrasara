# Śiva Sūtras 2.3

विद्याशरीरसत्ता मन्त्ररहस्यम्

Vidyāśarīrasattā mantrarahasyam.

The conscious being underlying the knowledge-formed body of mantra is its secret.

The Secret of Mantra Is That You’ve Been Worshipping the Alphabet Without Knowing Its Mother.

Oh, now we’re getting somewhere.

Vidya.

Śarīra.

Sattā.

Mantra.

Rahasya.

Let’s get the Sanskrit properly unpacked, because this is an absolutely gorgeous compound.

Vidyā — knowledge, particularly liberating or pure knowledge in this context.

Śarīra — body, embodiment.

Sattā — existence, being.

Mantra — sacred formula.

Rahasya — secret, mystery, hidden essence.

Kṣemarāja interprets vidyāśarīra as that whose essential body consists of supreme nondual knowledge.

And the sattā, the conscious being of that knowledge-body, is the luminous power of pure I-consciousness.

This is the secret of mantra.

Now, an ordinary person sees a written mantra.

A series of letters.

Perhaps:

न मः शि वा य

Na — maḥ — śi — vā — ya.

Five syllables in the traditional namaḥ śivāya formulation, with namaḥ sandhi and pronunciation treated according to the chant.

There’s a sequence.

The sounds occur one after another.

Your mouth moves.

Your tongue changes position.

The sound resonates.

And you might naturally conclude that the mantra consists fundamentally of those audible units.

But Kṣemarāja wants us to look deeper.

The syllables belong to Mātṛkā, the divine matrix of phonemes.

And the phonemes are expressions of Śakti.

So what ultimately gives the mantra its sacred identity?

Not merely its acoustic properties.

Not merely a collection of letters.

But the conscious power manifested through them.

This is where the tantric philosophy of speech becomes absolutely outrageous.

Because language isn’t treated simply as a human invention for labelling pre-existing things.

It is understood through a cosmological theory in which the unfolding of speech corresponds to the unfolding of manifestation.

The phenomenal world appears articulated through innumerable distinctions.

And speech expresses, organises, and, within this tantric metaphysics, participates in the very manifestation of those distinctions.

Now, before everyone starts claiming Sanskrit is physically generating hydrogen atoms, we need to be precise.

This is a metaphysical doctrine of consciousness and manifestation.

It isn’t a laboratory demonstration that pronouncing a vowel creates an element of the periodic table.

But within the Trika system, there is a deep relationship between the powers of conscious manifestation and the articulated structure of language.

And Kṣemarāja supports his explanation with an astonishing passage from the Tantrasadbhāva.

He describes Śakti in her sleeping, coiled condition.

The potential universe lies within her.

The powers associated with bindu, the divine point, and visarga, emission, participate in the manifestation of the phonemes.

The letters appear through increasingly differentiated powers.

And the many forms of speech and manifestation arise from the one Śakti.

This passage involves Kuṇḍalinī, subtle sound, the arrangement of letters, divine powers, and the emergence of multiplicity.

It’s not a casual metaphor casually added by Kṣemarāja.

It’s part of the actual scriptural material he deploys to explain the secret of mantra. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Think of a piece of music.

You can analyse its individual notes.

Their frequencies.

Their duration.

Their order.

You can write every note onto a page.

But the living experience of the music is not simply a pile of isolated acoustic measurements.

Now imagine a much more radical theory.

The very possibility of articulation, intelligibility, experience and manifestation belongs to one conscious power.

The notes are expressions of that power.

The listener is an expression of that power.

The experienced unity of the music is also an expression of that power.

That’s the kind of nondual move Kṣemarāja is making with mantra.

And the final secret?

The living divine power is not separate from the consciousness through which the mantra is contemplated.

This is why merely knowing the correct syllables is insufficient for the highest recognition.

You can possess the letters and remain ignorant of their source.

You can pronounce the mantra perfectly and still approach Śiva as an entirely separate deity.

You can become the world’s leading expert in the phonology of Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya and spend your entire life wondering whether you’ve finally reached the divine.

While, according to the doctrine, the conscious power making the entire investigation possible is precisely what you’re seeking.

It’s like studying the chemical composition of ink on a love letter while ignoring the love letter.

Very accurate chemistry.

Very unfortunate relationship.

The philosophical point: For Kṣemarāja, the inner secret of mantra is Śakti as self-aware, universal consciousness, not the mere external sequence of phonemes. The phonemes remain significant, but their highest efficacy depends upon recognition of that conscious source.

And now, having explained the extraordinary secret of mantra, Vasugupta offers a warning.

Because apparently you can get all the way into advanced tantric practice, encounter unusual experiences, and still completely miss the point.

Which is deeply embarrassing.
