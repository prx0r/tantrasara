# Śiva Sūtras 3.44

नासिकान्तर्मध्यसंयमात्किमत्र सव्यापसव्यसौषुम्नेषु

Nāsikāntarmadhyasaṃyamāt kim atra savyāpasavyasauṣumneṣu.

Through intense contemplation of the innermost centre of the vital power, what more need be said of the right, left and central channels?

Stop Obsessing Over Your Nostril Channels. The Real Centre Is Consciousness.

Now.

Look at that Sanskrit.

Vasugupta has clearly decided that if we’re finishing the text, we’re going to make the reader work for it.

He’s gone from:

Caitanyam ātmā.

Two words.

To this magnificent phonemic railway accident.

But the meaning is beautiful.

Let’s break it down.

Nāsikā — ordinarily, the nose or nostril; but Kṣemarāja gives it a specialised interpretation here.

Antar — inner.

Madhya — middle, centre.

Saṃyama — disciplined concentration or restraint; here, intense inner contemplative awareness.

Kim atra — what here? What more is to be said?

Savya — right-side, as Kṣemarāja interprets it in this context.

Apasavya — left-side.

Sauṣumṇa — relating to Suṣumṇā, the central subtle channel.

Now, the first thing to understand is that Kṣemarāja doesn’t simply treat nāsikā as the physical nose.

This is absolutely crucial.

He interprets it as the vital power moving in a winding or curved manner through the subtle channels.

Prāṇaśakti.

And the antar of that vital power refers to its inner conscious reality.

Then the madhya is the innermost centre.

The supreme vimarśa.

The reflexive I-consciousness of Śiva.

Now we’re getting somewhere.

Because the entire system of subtle channels has been associated with the differentiation of vitality.

Right.

Left.

Centre.

The movements of prāṇa.

The rhythms of embodied experience.

But Kṣemarāja is directing attention towards something more fundamental.

The conscious power underlying that entire system.

And he quotes the Kālikākrama on vimarśa as the supreme Śakti of the highest conscious Lord.

The crucial distinction is between working with the differentiated movements of vital energy and recognising the conscious source underlying them.

Now, to be clear, this is a traditional contemplative interpretation, not a reason to force your breathing, hold your breath for dangerous lengths of time, or manipulate the nostrils in some elaborate improvised practice.

Kṣemarāja’s point here is not that physiological strain produces liberation.

It’s the intensity of inner awareness.

And he glosses saṃyama through:

Antarnibhālanaprakarṣa.

The intensification of inward contemplation or awareness.

What a magnificent expression.

The practitioner turns towards the innermost conscious reality.

Not merely the movement of breath.

Not merely the sensation of energy.

Not merely the subtle channels.

But the self-revealing awareness through which those phenomena become manifest.

And here’s where the entire metaphysics comes together.

The conscious reality is not merely another thing located inside the body.

It’s not a tiny glowing object hidden somewhere between your eyebrows.

Nor is it a mysterious anatomical organ discovered through sufficiently advanced breathing exercises.

It’s the underlying self-aware power of consciousness.

And Kṣemarāja says that through continuous awareness of that reality, the yogi attains the supreme samādhi that shines throughout all states.

Nirvyutthāna.

Without return to the ordinary distracted standpoint.

And now we arrive at a particularly beautiful citation.

Kṣemarāja invokes the Vijñānabhairava on the relationship between knower and known.

All embodied beings participate in cognition.

But the yogi’s distinction lies in their attentiveness to the relationship between the conscious subject and its objects.

Not merely the objects.

Not merely an abstract theory of a subject.

But the fundamental relation through which manifestation occurs. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now consider the philosophical significance.

At the beginning of Book III, the individual experienced:

“I am this mind.”

And therefore the world appeared as a separate domain of objects.

But now the yogi has traced the entire structure of embodiment back towards the innermost conscious reality.

The body.

The senses.

The breath.

The subtle channels.

The mental apparatus.

All have been examined as manifestations of Śakti.

And now the supreme source becomes the object — or, more accurately, the reality — of recognition.

The movement is from the most differentiated structures of individuality to the conscious power that makes their manifestation possible.

And here’s the most beautiful part.

The yogi doesn’t necessarily have to abolish all the differentiated processes.

Breathing can continue.

The body can remain.

The senses can operate.

But the ultimate reality is no longer concealed by them.

And this is why Kṣemarāja concludes that the yogi’s supreme samādhi can shine throughout every state.

Not merely in the absence of ordinary experience.

But through its manifestation.

And now.

Ladies and gentlemen.

We’ve arrived.

The final sūtra.

Number seventy-seven.

And Vasugupta has chosen an ending so beautiful, so compressed, and so perfectly aligned with the entire work that it almost makes you want to start reading the whole bloody thing again.

Which, as it turns out, is rather appropriate.

Because the final word is about returning.

Folding back.

The coming together of inward and outward awareness.

The universe returning to its conscious source.

Except that source never ceased to be its reality.

Here we go.
