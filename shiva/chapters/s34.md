# Śiva Sūtras 3.2

ज्ञानं बन्धः

Jñānaṃ bandhaḥ.

Knowledge is bondage.

Knowledge Is Bondage. Yes, Again. Apparently You Didn’t Get It the First Time.

RIGHT.

WE’VE SEEN THIS ONE.

It’s literally the second sūtra of the first book.

Vasugupta has copied and pasted himself.

The man’s producing sacred scripture and apparently can’t be bothered writing a new sentence.

But the repetition is entirely deliberate.

Because the same statement is now being explained from the standpoint of the limited individual.

In Book I, knowledge as bondage was connected with the fundamental contraction of universal consciousness.

Here, Kṣemarāja gets much more specific.

He’s talking about the ordinary mental cognitions associated with:

Pleasure.

Pain.

Delusion.

Judgement.

Appropriation.

The perception of difference.

And the experience of being an individual subject moving through a world of external objects.

Now imagine you’re having a perfectly pleasant afternoon.

You see something beautiful.

The mind responds:

“I like this.”

Then:

“I want this.”

Then:

“I need to keep this.”

Then:

“What happens if I lose this?”

Then:

“Why does that bastard have a better one?”

And within approximately seventeen seconds, an innocent pleasant perception has developed into a complete metaphysical hostage situation.

Now, Kṣemarāja isn’t suggesting that pleasure is intrinsically sinful.

Nor that distinguishing objects is itself an ethical failure.

The issue is that differentiated knowledge becomes entangled with limited identity.

The individual knows through an apparatus already structured by the conditions of limitation.

And so every act of knowing can reinforce the apparent division between the finite knower and the independent known.

The limited person begins from:

“I am here.”

“The world is over there.”

“I must obtain what completes me.”

“I must resist what threatens me.”

And all these experiences produce further involvement in saṃsāra.

Kṣemarāja connects this process with puryaṣṭaka.

The eightfold subtle constitution.

Traditionally comprising the intellect, ego, ordinary mind and five subtle sensory elements.

This subtle constitution, together with karmic tendencies, is implicated in transmigration.

He draws on the Spandakārikā to describe the bound individual experiencing pleasure, pain and successive forms of existence through the activities of the subtle body. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now here’s what makes this so philosophically interesting.

The actual problem isn’t that the mind knows something.

Because from the standpoint of Trika, even the act of limited knowing is a manifestation of consciousness.

The problem is the failure to recognise the conscious reality of the knower and the known.

Suppose I look at a flower.

There’s a cognition.

“Flower.”

There’s a perceiver.

“Me.”

There’s a perceived object.

“That flower.”

Ordinary experience organises those into a relation of difference.

Now, at the conventional level, that’s perfectly functional.

You shouldn’t try to water yourself because you’ve decided the distinction between you and the flower is ultimately nondual.

The flower still needs water.

You probably need breakfast.

But at the metaphysical level, the Trika claim is that the perceiver, perception and perceived object are not ultimately independent of the one self-revealing consciousness.

And without that recognition, the subject mistakes their finite cognitive standpoint for their complete nature.

So the same world can be experienced through two fundamentally different orientations.

One: limited knowledge reinforcing separation.

The other: differentiated cognition recognised as the manifestation of consciousness.

And Vasugupta’s point is that the former constitutes bondage.

Which brings us to a very uncomfortable question.

Why does consciousness fail to recognise its own nature?

Enter Māyā.

Again.

She’s back.

And she’s brought paperwork.
