# Śiva Sūtras 3.8

जाग्रद्द्वितीयकरः

Jāgrad dvitīyakaraḥ.

The awakened yogi sees the apparently second reality — the world — as a ray of their own consciousness.

The Entire Universe Is Apparently Your Own Bloody Sunshine.

Now this is a fascinating sūtra.

Because taken without commentary, it’s fairly cryptic.

Let’s unpack it.

Jāgrat — awake, waking, watchful.

Dvitīya — second, another.

Kara — ray, beam, radiance.

And Kṣemarāja provides the crucial interpretation.

The yogi established in innate knowledge remains awake in the deeper sense of being established in recognition.

And the apparently second reality — the world of objects — appears as the radiance of consciousness.

This is the relationship between:

Aham — I.

And:

Idam — this.

Now we’ve encountered these before, but this is where their significance becomes particularly beautiful.

Ordinarily, experience seems to involve two fundamentally separate realities.

There’s me.

And there’s everything else.

I look at a tree.

The tree is over there.

I am over here.

Subject and object.

But within the Trika metaphysical system, the relationship is more subtle.

The supreme Aham, universal I-consciousness, possesses the freedom to manifest the entire domain of Idam, this-ness or objectivity.

The world is not some entirely independent substance standing opposite Śiva.

It is the manifestation of Śiva’s own Śakti.

So what happens when the yogi recognises this?

The objects don’t necessarily disappear.

The tree is still there.

The river still flows.

The mountains remain.

The mosquitoes still apparently regard your ankles as a charitable buffet.

But the ontological understanding of these appearances is transformed.

They’re recognised as expressions of the same consciousness whose nature has become apparent.

Now think of the sun.

The sun gives off light.

Its rays illuminate the world.

And although the rays are distinguishable in their manifestations, they are not some fundamentally foreign power independent of their source.

This is the metaphor Vasugupta uses.

Kara.

A ray.

The world is the radiance of consciousness.

And this is enormously significant because it means liberation isn’t simply a withdrawal from objectivity.

It’s a transformation in the recognition of objectivity.

You’re not supposed to sit there with your eyes closed forever, desperately trying to avoid the existence of tables.

You’re supposed to recognise the fundamental nature of the reality in which the tables appear.

The world is no longer encountered as an ultimately alien second reality.

And Kṣemarāja cites the Vijñānabhairava to support this.

He describes the recognition of consciousness in whatever is perceived — including colour and pleasure — and the attainment of fullness through absorption into omnipresent consciousness.

He also cites a tantric statement describing the entire world as the powers of Śiva, the possessor of Śakti. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now let’s consider the philosophical implication.

If the world is the radiance of consciousness, then recognition doesn’t have to depend on a privileged sensory object.

You don’t need the perfect sunset.

You don’t need an extraordinary vision.

You don’t need the Himalayas to appear through the mist while somebody plays a rather moving piece of devotional music.

Lovely though that would be.

Any manifestation can become an occasion for recognition.

A colour.

A sound.

The feeling of movement.

The appearance of another person.

The emergence of a thought.

Because the essential point is not the particular content.

It’s the conscious reality through which the content becomes manifest.

And here we arrive at something particularly elegant.

In the first book, Vasugupta said:

दृश्यं शरीरम्।.

Dṛśyaṃ śarīram.

The seen is the body.

Now he’s explaining the same expanded recognition through the imagery of light.

The world is the yogi’s radiance.

The apparent second is not ultimately separate from the first.

And therefore the liberated yogi can remain awake in the midst of ordinary experience.

Not because they’ve stopped perceiving differences.

But because the differences no longer conceal their conscious source.

And that brings us to a revelation so magnificent that Vasugupta apparently decides to abandon metaphysical exposition and become a theatre critic.
