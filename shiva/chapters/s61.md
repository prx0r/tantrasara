# Śiva Sūtras 3.29

योऽविपस्थो ज्ञाहेतुश्च

Yo ’vipastho jñāhetuś ca.

The one established in sovereignty over the powers is a source of liberating knowledge.

Not Everyone Who Knows Sanskrit Is Qualified to Be Your Guru.

Now this is a particularly interesting sūtra.

And it requires careful explanation.

Because the Sanskrit is unusual.

Let’s begin.

Yaḥ — he who.

Avipasthaḥ — interpreted by Kṣemarāja through a special tantric derivation.

Jñā — knowledge, or the knowing power.

Hetuḥ — cause, source, means.

Ca — and, also; Kṣemarāja understands it here in an emphatic sense.

Now, the central difficulty is avipasthaḥ.

Kṣemarāja explicitly gives an interpretive analysis involving avi, a term associated with the bound beings.

And pa, the protector.

The powers presiding over the bound beings are therefore brought into the interpretation.

Remember the goddesses from 3.19?

The Mothers.

The powers of Mātṛkā.

The divine forces associated with differentiated cognition.

Well, now the realised yogi is said to be established in sovereignty over those powers.

He is no longer merely governed by them through unconscious limitation.

Rather, he knows their true source.

And therefore possesses the capacity to communicate liberating knowledge.

Now, this is a crucial distinction.

The ordinary individual is subject to the powers of differentiated cognition.

The realised yogi recognises those powers as manifestations of Śakti.

And this recognition changes their relationship to the entire śakticakra.

The wheel of powers.

The yogi is no longer simply a dependent subject carried along by the processes of cognition.

The powers are understood through their supreme source.

And now the extraordinary implication.

Only someone established in genuine recognition can fully perform the liberating function of the teacher described in the preceding sūtra.

At 3.28, we learned:

Dānam ātmajñānam.

The gift is knowledge of the Self.

But how can someone communicate that recognition if they remain fundamentally confused about their own nature?

And that’s Kṣemarāja’s question.

A person who remains entirely subordinate to the powers of differentiated cognition cannot, simply by possessing information, exercise the same transformative authority attributed to the realised teacher.

Now, this shouldn’t be exaggerated into the claim that anyone who lacks enlightenment is incapable of explaining anything useful.

Obviously people can teach grammar.

History.

Philosophy.

Meditation techniques.

Even if they haven’t attained the supreme condition described here.

The distinction concerns the traditional ideal of the guru as an effective source of liberating recognition.

And here’s where the commentary gets unexpectedly funny.

Kṣemarāja discusses alternative interpretations of the sūtra that derive elaborate meanings from individual syllables.

For example, extracting yogīndra from yo, vijñāna from vi, and further meanings from the remaining parts.

The result becomes an intricate symbolic reading.

Very impressive.

Lots of Sanskrit.

Extremely esoteric.

Possibly the sort of thing somebody could turn into a three-hour lecture with a beautiful slideshow.

But Kṣemarāja doesn’t accept the method in this instance.

He objects that the proposed interpretation doesn’t fit the intended connection between the words sufficiently well.

And he makes a wonderful methodological point.

If you’re permitted to extract virtually any meaning from syllabic resemblance, you can generate countless interpretations of every sūtra.

Which is a rather devastating observation.

Because he’s essentially warning against unconstrained Sanskrit mysticism.

Not every attractive phonetic coincidence is a valid interpretation.

Not every elaborate symbolic explanation is faithful to the text.

And the fact that an interpretation sounds extraordinarily profound does not automatically mean it’s philologically justified.

Which is excellent advice for anybody studying Tantra.

You can construct an entire philosophical system out of a word.

But unless the derivation, context and doctrinal argument support it, you may simply be producing very sophisticated nonsense.

Kṣemarāja explicitly rejects the alternative syllable-by-syllable interpretation in favour of his explanation through the powers of knowledge and the realised yogi’s sovereignty over them. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And that makes this sūtra particularly significant.

Because it’s not merely describing the ideal spiritual teacher.

It’s also demonstrating something about how the tradition interprets its own scripture.

The words are extraordinarily compressed.

The meanings are often esoteric.

But interpretation still requires discipline.

A profound metaphysical doctrine does not excuse careless reading.

And that brings us to the final sūtra of this instalment.

One of the most extraordinary statements in the entire text.

Because after all the discussion of recognition, liberation, embodiment, mantra, and the teacher’s gift, Vasugupta returns to the fundamental nature of the universe.

And announces something absolutely enormous.

The entire world is the expansion of the yogi’s own Śakti.

Now that’s a conclusion.
