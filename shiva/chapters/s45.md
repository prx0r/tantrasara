# Śiva Sūtras 3.13

सिद्धः स्वतन्त्रभावः

Siddhaḥ svatantrabhāvaḥ.

The state of being free and independent is attained.

Svātantrya: The Entire Point of the Bloody Operation.

Now we’re talking.

Because this is where everything comes together.

Siddhaḥ — accomplished, attained, realised.

Svatantra — independent, self-governing, free.

Bhāvaḥ — state, condition, manner of being.

And the key word is svatantra.

Let’s look at its construction.

Sva — one’s own, belonging to oneself.

Tantra — here conveying the principle of control, dependence or governance.

Svatantra therefore means self-dependent, independent, governed by oneself rather than by another.

And the abstract noun:

Svātantrya.

Freedom.

Independence.

Autonomy.

But in Trika, it becomes something much more radical than ordinary personal autonomy.

It is the intrinsic freedom of supreme consciousness.

The power through which Śiva knows, acts and manifests.

And Kṣemarāja explains that this freedom involves sahajajñatva and kartṛtva.

Innate knowledge and agency.

The natural powers of knowing and acting.

Now think about the limited individual.

The aṇu.

Its knowledge is restricted.

Its agency is restricted.

Its experience is structured by time, necessity, desire and differentiated cognition.

And the entire third book began by describing precisely this condition.

But now the yogi, through the recognition of the subtle reality of consciousness, attains the condition of freedom.

What’s being reversed?

The fundamental limitations through which Śiva experienced himself as a finite subject.

The point isn’t simply:

“I can now do whatever I want.”

That’s ordinary individual desire enlarged into a fantasy.

Nor is it:

“I am an exceptionally powerful personality who deserves to control everybody else.”

That would be ahaṃkāra wearing a very expensive Śaiva costume.

The claim is that the yogi recognises the nature of consciousness as intrinsically free.

And this freedom isn’t merely freedom from particular external obstacles.

It’s the freedom through which manifestation itself is understood.

Consider the difference.

Ordinary freedom means:

“I have a choice between several possibilities.”

Divine svātantrya means something more fundamental.

The very existence of manifested possibilities belongs to the freedom of consciousness.

Śiva doesn’t merely select among pre-existing options arranged by some independent cosmic administrator.

Within the Trika system, the appearance of those options is itself an expression of Śakti.

That’s why svātantrya is so central.

It distinguishes Śiva from an inert absolute.

Consciousness is not merely luminous.

It is self-aware, active, and endowed with the freedom of manifestation.

Kṣemarāja explicitly describes the attained freedom as sovereignty involving natural knowledge and agency.

He cites a teaching attributed to Nāthapāda identifying the power of freedom with Kālī, the supreme power.

And he cites the Svacchandatantra on the yogi’s sovereignty over the tattvas, beings, letters and mantras through contemplation of Śiva. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now, the latter includes a traditional claim of extraordinary yogic sovereignty.

That should not be mistaken for a scientifically established power to control the physical universe.

But its philosophical significance is profound.

The yogi’s highest freedom is not another limited object of attainment.

It is the recognition of the divine power that makes manifestation possible.

And this brings us back to the beginning.

चैतन्यमात्मा।

Consciousness is the Self.

But now that statement has acquired a far richer meaning.

Consciousness is not merely a container of appearances.

It is not merely an observer.

It is the free, self-revealing reality through which appearances become manifest.

Svātantrya is inseparable from what Śiva is.

And liberation is recognition of that nature.

Not the construction of an entirely new Self.

Not the acquisition of something fundamentally external.

The recognition of one’s own deepest reality.

And there’s something rather beautiful about the fact that the aṇu — the limited individual — is not abandoned.

The journey begins from limitation.

But the destination is the recognition of freedom.

It’s not the replacement of the individual with a completely different metaphysical substance.

It’s the disclosure of the universal nature underlying individuality.

And now Vasugupta adds another sentence.

A very short one.

But an absolutely enormous claim.
