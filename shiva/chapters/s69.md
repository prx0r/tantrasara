# Śiva Sūtras 3.37

करणशक्तिः स्वतोऽनुभवात्

Karaṇaśaktiḥ svato ’nubhavāt.

The power of creation is known from one’s own experience.

You Literally Generate Entire Worlds in Dreams. Śiva Would Like a Word.

Oh, this is a beauty.

Because Kṣemarāja is about to support his metaphysical argument by appealing to something everyone can investigate.

Their own experience.

Let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Karaṇa — making, producing, creating.

Śakti — power.

Svataḥ — from oneself, from one’s own standpoint.

Anubhavāt — from direct experience.

And the interpretation is:

The creative power of consciousness is already evident through experience.

Now, Kṣemarāja gives two examples.

Saṅkalpa.

Mental construction, imaginative determination.

And:

Svapna.

Dreaming.

And this is where the whole thing becomes extraordinary.

Consider a dream.

You’re asleep.

And suddenly there’s a world.

Buildings.

People.

Mountains.

An entire environment.

Perhaps you’re standing in a city you’ve never visited.

Perhaps you’re speaking to somebody who died years ago.

Perhaps you’re being chased by a six-foot goose wearing a police uniform.

Absolutely terrifying.

But for the duration of the dream, the world appears.

You experience it.

You respond to it.

You may even believe it’s entirely independent of you.

And then you wake up.

And discover that the dream world was not an independently existing physical environment of the same kind as your waking surroundings.

It arose through mental activity.

Now, modern neuroscience and Trika metaphysics would explain this in very different ways.

But Kṣemarāja’s immediate point is about the experience of manifestation.

The mind can present forms, objects and situations that are experienced as a world.

And this shows, within his argument, that consciousness possesses a power of presentation or manifestation.

He connects this with the Īśvarapratyabhijñā, whose account of knowledge and agency appeals to the capacity of living beings to manifest desired forms through cognition.

And this is one of the most fascinating connections in the entire work.

Because the argument isn’t merely:

“Imagine something, therefore you can physically create anything.”

That would be a logical leap.

And a rather dangerous one if you were planning to manifest a helicopter.

The first step is more subtle.

Experience already displays a power of conscious manifestation.

In dreaming, the conscious field presents an elaborate world.

In imagination, objects can be mentally represented without their corresponding external presence.

And these examples become important for the Pratyabhijñā theory of agency.

Now Kṣemarāja takes the argument further.

He says that when this creative power is recognised with intense determination, it can, according to the tantric account, become capable of manifesting things beyond the private domain of imagination.

He even cites the Tattvagarbha on the yogi whose will becomes like a kalpavṛkṣa.

The wish-fulfilling tree.

A tree that grants whatever you desire.

Quite an improvement over ordinary gardening.

But again, distinguish the levels.

The fact that dreams contain generated experiences is observable.

The conclusion that a yogi can therefore manifest public physical objects at will is an additional traditional claim, not something established by the dream example alone.

Kṣemarāja makes both moves, and we should preserve both without conflating them. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now let’s explore the philosophical point more deeply.

Why does the dream matter so much to nondual Śaivism?

Because it destabilises an assumption we make constantly.

That whatever appears to consciousness must be entirely independent of the conscious process through which it is experienced.

In a dream, that assumption becomes questionable.

A whole environment can appear as though it exists independently of the dreamer.

And yet its manifestation is tied to the dreaming condition.

Now, Kṣemarāja uses this to illustrate the larger doctrine of manifestation.

The waking universe, from the supreme standpoint of Trika, is also understood as an expression of consciousness.

But not simply a private dream produced by your personal human mind.

That distinction is absolutely essential.

Universal consciousness is not identical to the ordinary individual imagination.

The supreme Aham is not the contracted ego.

And therefore Trika’s account of cosmic manifestation is not the claim that the world exists merely because your private personality imagines it.

That would be a very crude form of solipsism.

The doctrine is more radical.

The individual mind itself is a manifestation within universal consciousness.

And the world of waking experience is also understood through that universal conscious reality.

The individual doesn’t create Śiva.

Śiva manifests the individual.

And the world.

And their relationship.

Now consider the beauty of that.

Ordinarily you ask:

“How does my consciousness relate to the world?”

But Trika changes the question.

It asks:

How does universal consciousness manifest the distinction between a limited subject and its world?

The first question assumes two fundamental realities and tries to connect them.

The second begins from one reality and explains the appearance of differentiation.

And that is precisely what Kṣemarāja’s interpretation of karaṇaśakti is doing.

The creative power of consciousness is understood as intrinsic to the Self.

And dreaming becomes one accessible illustration of manifestation.

Now, this brings us to an enormous philosophical development.

Because the final sūtras are about to move away from the special question of extraordinary creative powers.

And return to something much more immediate.

The three fundamental movements of manifestation.

Arising.

Remaining.

Subsiding.

And the fourth state of consciousness that underlies them.

But this time, Kṣemarāja is going to connect them to everyday experiences of joy, delight, wonder, and even sensual pleasure.

Which is very tantric indeed.

Apparently the highest metaphysical reality can become apparent while listening to beautiful music.

Excellent.

Finally, a spiritual path with decent acoustics.
