# Śiva Sūtras 3.11

प्रेक्षकाणीन्द्रियाणि

Prekṣakāṇīndriyāṇi.

The senses are the spectators.

Your Eyes Are Sitting in the Front Row Watching Śiva Perform.

LADIES AND GENTLEMEN.

WE HAVE A FULL THEATRE.

The Self is the dancer.

The inner self is the stage.

And the senses?

The bloody audience.

Now this is the sort of metaphor you could spend years contemplating.

Let’s unpack it.

Prekṣaka — spectator, observer, someone who watches a performance.

Indriya — sense faculty.

The plural forms give us:

Prekṣakāṇi indriyāṇi.

The senses are the spectators.

Now, in ordinary life, we think of our senses primarily as channels for acquiring information about an external world.

Eyes receive visual information.

Ears receive sound.

The skin responds to touch.

And so on.

But in the Trika metaphysical system, the senses are powers of consciousness.

They’re not ultimately independent entities capable of knowing without the conscious reality through which experience occurs.

And here’s where Kṣemarāja makes an absolutely beautiful move.

The senses, ordinarily directed outward towards their objects, are described as becoming inwardly aware of their own essential nature.

And this inward recognition is associated with camatkāra.

Astonishment.

Wonder.

Aesthetic delight.

The extraordinary savour of consciousness recognising itself.

Now, you might remember this from the first book.

विस्मयो योगभूमिकाः।

Vismayo yogabhūmikāḥ.

The stages of yoga are wonder.

And now the same aesthetic dimension returns through the theatre metaphor.

The universe is a performance.

The Self is the actor.

The senses are the spectators.

And the experience of recognition becomes a kind of supreme aesthetic delight.

This is where Trika’s philosophy connects beautifully with the wider Kashmiri tradition of aesthetics.

Think about watching a magnificent play.

For a moment, you’re completely absorbed.

The performance draws you in.

The characters, emotions, movement, music and atmosphere form an extraordinary unity.

You experience something beyond simply processing individual bits of sensory information.

There’s a distinctive delight in the manifestation itself.

And Kṣemarāja uses the concept of camatkāra to describe the heightened experience of the yogi whose senses become established in recognition.

But the metaphysical claim goes further than ordinary aesthetic appreciation.

In an ordinary performance, the spectator remains distinct from the actor.

Here, the senses themselves are powers of the same Self that performs the drama.

So the separation between actor and spectators is not ultimate.

The conscious reality is the source of both.

And now comes a rather extraordinary consequence.

The senses no longer have to function exclusively as sources of distraction.

Seeing can become an occasion for recognition.

Hearing can become an occasion for recognition.

The appearance of a sensation can become an occasion for recognition.

Because the senses themselves are not outside Śiva.

Their capacity to disclose manifestation belongs to Śakti.

And this is a major difference between simply suppressing sensory experience and recognising its conscious source.

The point isn’t:

“Never look at anything.”

“Never listen to anything.”

“Never enjoy anything.”

It’s more subtle.

Don’t mistake the objects disclosed by the senses for realities ultimately independent of consciousness.

And don’t mistake the sensory faculties themselves for autonomous sources of awareness.

Recognise the conscious reality through which the entire sensory drama becomes manifest.

Kṣemarāja explicitly invokes the Kaṭha Upaniṣad in explaining the inward turn of the senses.

The ordinary direction of attention is outward.

But the wise seeker turns inward towards the Self.

And in the context of this sūtra, the result is the delightful recognition of the conscious actor through the senses themselves. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

Now imagine standing in a busy marketplace.

People moving.

Voices overlapping.

Colours appearing.

Smells rising from food stalls.

A tremendous sensory performance.

Ordinarily, attention is scattered among its objects.

But in the contemplative interpretation, the practitioner begins recognising the underlying conscious reality through which all these perceptions appear.

The sights and sounds don’t necessarily have to stop.

The theatre continues.

But the spectators discover the true nature of the actor.

And this is why the three sūtras belong together.

नर्तक आत्मा।

The Self is the dancer.

रङ्गोऽन्तरात्मा।

The inner self is the stage.

प्रेक्षकाणीन्द्रियाणि।

The senses are the spectators.

What an extraordinary little metaphysical trilogy.

The universe is a drama of consciousness.

And the yogi, through recognition, discovers the conscious unity underlying the entire performance.

Now, the obvious question is:

How does this recognition become stable?

Because it’s all very well understanding the theatre metaphor.

But you can intellectually appreciate the idea that consciousness is the actor and still spend your entire life absolutely furious about the plot.

So Vasugupta moves to the next question.

What enables the yogi to recognise the subtle conscious reality underlying the performance?

The answer involves a rather important form of intelligence.
