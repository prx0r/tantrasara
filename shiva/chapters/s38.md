# Śiva Sūtras 3.6

मोहावरणात्सिद्धिः

Mohāvaraṇāt siddhiḥ.

Supernatural accomplishment arises because of the covering of delusion.

Congratulations, You’ve Acquired Superpowers. Unfortunately, You’re Still Deluded.

Oh, this is BRUTAL.

Absolutely ruthless.

Imagine you’ve spent twenty years practising yoga.

You’ve mastered concentration.

You’ve performed elaborate contemplations of the elements.

You’ve become adept at extraordinary states of absorption.

And perhaps, within the traditional framework, you’ve acquired unusual siddhis.

You arrive before Kṣemarāja.

“Master, I have attained extraordinary abilities.”

And Kṣemarāja replies:

“Very impressive. That’s Māyā.”

“Sorry?”

“Delusion, mate.”

“But I can do remarkable things!”

“Yes, within the realm of delusion.”

“I CAN LITERALLY—”

“Still Māyā.”

Absolutely devastating.

But philosophically, he’s making a precise distinction.

Siddhi — accomplishment, perfection, supernatural power.

Moha — delusion, bewilderment.

Āvaraṇa — covering, concealment.

And the sūtra says that certain extraordinary powers arise within the covering of delusion.

Why?

Because the powers described here concern differentiated realities.

They belong to the manifested domain of experience.

And the acquisition of unusual abilities within that domain doesn’t necessarily disclose the supreme reality beyond limited subject-object relations.

Imagine somebody becomes extraordinarily skilled at lucid dreaming.

They can manipulate their dream environment.

Change the scenery.

Fly through the sky.

Conjure objects.

Perform impossible feats.

Wonderful.

But does mastery over the dream automatically mean they’ve awakened from it?

Not necessarily.

They may simply have become a very powerful character within the dream.

This is analogous to Kṣemarāja’s argument.

The siddhis are not necessarily the same as liberation.

And the deeper problem is that the practitioner may mistake control over phenomena for freedom from fundamental ignorance.

“I can control this.”

“I can manifest that.”

“I have acquired this power.”

Notice that the entire grammar of limited selfhood remains intact.

A finite subject acquires powers over finite or extraordinary objects.

The structure of identification has not necessarily changed.

Kṣemarāja explicitly differentiates powers obtained through yogic techniques from direct manifestation of the supreme principle.

But he also makes an important qualification.

The same disciplines can contribute to genuine recognition when directed towards their highest aim.

He cites teachings from the Netratantra describing withdrawal from subtle objects, meditation on supreme consciousness, and the contemplative understanding of one’s identity with Śiva. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

So he’s not rejecting practice.

He’s rejecting a confusion about its purpose.

The aim of Āṇavopāya isn’t to become the most impressive individual in the universe.

It’s to recognise the conscious reality in which individuality itself appears.

And this is the wonderful paradox.

You could acquire the power to perform astonishing feats.

And still be bound.

Or you could have no unusual abilities whatsoever and recognise the nature of awareness.

Within this system, the latter is the more fundamental spiritual accomplishment.

Which is a terrible blow to the supernatural entertainment industry.

But excellent news for anyone whose principal skill is sitting quietly and investigating consciousness.
