# Śiva Sūtras 2.8

शरीरं हविः

Śarīraṃ haviḥ.

The body is the sacrificial offering.

Congratulations, You’re the Sacrifice.

RIGHT.

Before anybody gets any ideas, let’s establish something important.

Kṣemarāja is not telling practitioners to physically burn themselves.

The fire in question is the supreme fire of consciousness.

And the body is offered through the dissolution of identification with it as the ultimate Self.

Now let’s unpack the Sanskrit.

Śarīra — body.

Havis — sacrificial oblation or offering; haviḥ is its nominative form.

And this refers us to the ancient Indian sacrificial vocabulary of yajña.

The fire.

The offering.

The act of offering.

The officiant.

The divine recipient.

Ordinarily, these are differentiated elements of a ritual.

Somebody performs the sacrifice.

Something is offered.

The offering goes into the fire.

The ritual is directed towards a divine reality.

But Trika makes an extraordinary internal transformation of this structure.

The body becomes the offering.

Consciousness becomes the fire.

And the false identification of the limited body as the ultimate perceiver is consumed.

Now, notice the specificity.

Kṣemarāja isn’t merely saying:

“Stop being attached to your body.”

He’s talking about the pramātṛ, the knowing subject.

Ordinarily, you identify the knower with your embodied individuality.

“I am this body.”

“My eyes are seeing.”

“My brain is thinking.”

“My personal history defines what I am.”

But remember the very first sūtra.

Caitanyam ātmā.

Consciousness is the Self.

From the standpoint of Kṣemarāja’s nondual metaphysics, the body itself belongs to the field of manifestation.

It is not the ultimate foundation of the conscious subject.

So what does it mean to offer the body?

It means that the body, together with its subtle forms and the structures of limited identification, is recognised as an expression within supreme consciousness.

The body is not destroyed.

Its erroneous status as an independently existing ultimate Self is relinquished.

Imagine standing before a fire.

You take the entire notion:

“I am nothing more than this limited organism.”

And you offer it into the recognition:

“My deepest nature is the consciousness in which this organism, its experiences, and the universe itself are manifest.”

The body hasn’t vanished.

You’ve not stopped eating.

You’ve not magically become impervious to a falling coconut.

But its ontological status within your understanding has changed.

And the beauty is that Kṣemarāja draws on the Vijñānabhairava to explain this inner sacrifice.

He describes the elements, senses, their objects, and mind as offerings into the fire of consciousness.

And the power that performs the offering is itself cetanā, conscious Śakti.

So who’s the priest?

Śakti.

What’s the offering?

The limited structures of embodiment.

What’s the fire?

Consciousness.

And who’s the divine recipient?

Śiva.

So the entire sacrificial process is ultimately an expression of the same reality.

The priest is Śiva’s power.

The fire is Śiva.

The offering belongs to Śiva.

And the sacrifice discloses the nondual nature of the whole performance.

It’s rather like turning up to a wedding and discovering you’re the bride, groom, priest, venue, and the entire congregation.

Except this is an internal metaphysical ritual rather than an unusually complicated marital arrangement.

And this is especially beautiful when compared with the Bhagavadgītā’s vision of sacrificial action.

Different traditions develop the idea in different ways, but the structural resemblance is unmistakable.

The apparent separation between the act, its instruments, its object, and the divine reality is overcome through knowledge.

Kṣemarāja explicitly cites both the Vijñānabhairava and Bhagavadgītā when explaining the sūtra. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

The offering is not a rejection of embodiment.

It is the end of mistaking embodiment for your ultimate identity.

And now we arrive at one of my favourite statements in the entire text.

Because having turned the body into a sacrificial offering, Vasugupta announces that knowledge is dinner.
