# Śiva Sūtras 3.16

आसनस्थः सुखं ह्रदे निमज्जति

Āsanasthaḥ sukhaṃ hrade nimajjati.

Established in the seat, one easily sinks into the lake.

Stop Trying to Sit Like a Pretzel. Your Real Āsana Is God.

Oh, this is beautiful.

Absolutely beautiful.

Because most people hear āsana and immediately think of bodily posture.

Cross-legged.

Back straight.

Shoulders relaxed.

Knees pointing in directions that appear to violate several international conventions.

And of course, the great spiritual dilemma:

“Can I attain supreme consciousness if my left ankle won’t go on my right thigh?”

Fortunately, Kṣemarāja has arrived to rescue us from the yoga-mat industry.

Because he gives āsana a much deeper meaning.

Let’s break down the Sanskrit.

Āsana — seat, resting place.

Sthaḥ — established, standing or abiding.

Sukham — easily, comfortably, without difficulty.

Hrade — into the lake, from hrada.

Nimajjati — one sinks, plunges, becomes immersed.

Now, what’s the seat?

Kṣemarāja answers:

Paraṃ śāktaṃ balam.

The supreme power of Śakti.

That’s the real āsana here.

Not the cushion.

Not the lotus posture.

Not a particularly expensive meditation bench carved by monks from sustainable Himalayan cedar.

The seat is the supreme power of consciousness in which the yogi has become established through identity.

And Kṣemarāja even explains the word through the idea of abiding.

That in which one remains continually established through oneness is the seat.

Now think about how extraordinary that is.

A physical posture is ordinarily something the body performs.

You arrange your legs.

Adjust your spine.

Find a stable position.

And perhaps the posture becomes supportive of contemplation.

Perfectly reasonable.

But Kṣemarāja is concerned with something more fundamental.

What is the conscious standpoint in which the practitioner is established?

You can be physically motionless while mentally wandering around twenty different imaginary universes.

You can sit in full lotus while thinking about what you’re going to eat.

You can remain in perfect bodily symmetry while inwardly composing an extremely rude reply to somebody on the internet.

Congratulations.

Your body is seated.

Your mind is on a world tour.

But the āsana described here is establishment in supreme Śakti.

And from that establishment, Kṣemarāja says the yogi enters the Great Lake effortlessly.

Sukham.

Easily.

Without strain.

And here’s the crucial part.

He explicitly describes the yogi as having relinquished the effort associated with meditation, concentration and other practices.

Now this doesn’t mean the earlier disciplines were pointless.

Quite the opposite.

The progression through Āṇavopāya has brought the practitioner to a condition in which the supreme reality can become spontaneously apparent.

The methods have served their purpose.

And the yogi no longer needs to manufacture the recognition through continual manipulation of attention.

Imagine learning to swim.

At first, you’re terribly preoccupied.

Where do the arms go?

How do the legs move?

Am I breathing correctly?

Why is there so much water?

Have I accidentally enrolled in a drowning course?

Everything requires effort.

But eventually, the movements become integrated.

You can enter the water without continually calculating every action.

Now take that analogy much further.

The yogi becomes established in the conscious source itself.

And immersion is no longer an externally imposed operation.

It becomes natural.

But what’s the lake?

Kṣemarāja describes it as the supreme ocean of immortality, the source of the continuous flow of universal manifestation.

The parāmṛtasamudra.

The ocean of supreme nectar.

The same divine reality we’ve been approaching since the first book.

And remember that earlier sūtra:

महाह्रदानुसन्धानान्मन्त्रवीर्यानुभवः।

Mahāhradānusandhānān mantravīryānubhavaḥ.

Through union with the Great Lake comes the experience of the potency of mantra.

That was 1.22.

And now, in 3.16, the yogi actually plunges into that lake through establishment in the power of Śakti.

It’s an extraordinary return.

Book I described the supreme source from an exalted standpoint.

Book III has brought the limited practitioner towards it through successive practices.

Now the two converge.

And notice that Kṣemarāja doesn’t describe this immersion as bodily annihilation.

He speaks of the subsiding of the saṃskāras of saṃkoca.

The residual impressions of contraction, particularly those tied to identification with the body and other limiting structures.

The yogi ceases to be governed by the conviction:

“I am merely this limited organism.”

That conviction is submerged in the recognition of consciousness.

And here’s a magnificent detail.

Kṣemarāja cites the Netratantra, under the title Mṛtyujit, to describe a supreme state in which the practitioner no longer requires a fixed direction for meditation.

Not above.

Not below.

Not in front.

Not behind.

Not inside the body as a particular spatial location.

Not in external space.

The teaching proceeds beyond reliance on such supports.

This isn’t an instruction for beginners to abandon every practice.

It describes the culmination of the preceding movement towards direct recognition. Sanskrit & Trika Shaivism.

And there is the extraordinary point.

You spend years trying to sit correctly.

Then discover that the highest seat isn’t a physical arrangement.

It’s establishment in your own deepest nature.

And once you’re established there?

You sink into the lake.

Effortlessly.

The body may still be sitting.

But the identity of the practitioner is no longer confined to the body.

The meditator has stopped trying to approach the ocean as though it were something external.

And recognises their identity with the conscious reality represented by the ocean.

Which raises an obvious question.

What happens when the yogi emerges from this immersion?

Well, apparently he starts manifesting worlds.

Of course he does.

It’s Kashmir.

Nobody ever just has a nice meditation and goes home.


Next, at 3.16:

आसनस्थः सुखं ह्रदे निमज्जति।

Āsanasthaḥ sukhaṃ hrade nimajjati.

Established in the seat, the yogi effortlessly sinks into the lake.

And we’re returning to that magnificent image from the end of the first book.

The Great Lake.

The oceanic source of manifestation.

But now we’ll discover why Kṣemarāja’s idea of āsana is much deeper than simply sitting cross-legged on a cushion.

The next fifteen sūtras also take us through the creation of one’s world, the ending of rebirth, the Mothers associated with the Sanskrit phonemes, the infusion of the fourth state into waking, dreaming and deep sleep like oil, the transformation of breath, and the extraordinary declarations:

शिवतुल्यो जायते।

Śivatulyo jāyate.

One becomes like Śiva.

And:

कथा जपः।

Kathā japaḥ.

Conversation becomes mantra-recitation.

Apparently even talking can become worship.

Which is excellent news for a tradition that’s been talking about itself for approximately a thousand years.

Previously, on the Śiva Sūtras…
Right.

We’re back in Kashmir.

And Vasugupta has been absolutely outrageous.

In the first fifteen sūtras of Āṇavopāya, we discovered that the individual Self appears contracted into the mind, the mind becomes bound by its own limited knowledge, and Māyā supplies an entire metaphysical bureaucracy to keep the arrangement going.

Five kañcukas.

Limited knowledge.

Limited agency.

Desire.

Time.

Necessity.

Congratulations. You’ve gone from being infinite consciousness to a man who can’t find his sandals.

But then things got interesting.

The practitioner began withdrawing the tattvas into their source.

The body became a field of contemplation.

The senses were transformed from instruments of bondage into spectators of a cosmic drama.

And Vasugupta dropped possibly the finest theatrical trilogy in the entire history of metaphysics.

नर्तक आत्मा।

Nartaka ātmā.

The Self is the dancer.

रङ्गोऽन्तरात्मा।

Raṅgo ’ntarātmā.

The inner Self is the stage.

प्रेक्षकाणीन्द्रियाणि।

Prekṣakāṇīndriyāṇi.

The senses are the spectators.

Absolutely magnificent.

And just when it seemed the curtain was coming down, we reached:

बीजावधानम्।

Bījāvadhānam.

Attention to the seed.

The seed being Parā Śakti, the supreme creative power of consciousness.

Kṣemarāja told us to establish attention there repeatedly.

Again and again.

Not because Śiva was missing.

But because the practitioner keeps becoming distracted by Śiva’s own performance.

Which is, admittedly, a rather good performance.

Now we’re going further.

We’ve been concentrating on the seed.

And Vasugupta says that when this concentration matures, something extraordinary happens.

You fall into a lake.

Not a geographical lake.

Not Dal Lake, although Kashmir does have a rather beautiful one.

The Great Lake of Consciousness.

And this time you’re not merely observing it.

You’re going in.

Headfirst.

Metaphysically speaking.

Let’s begin.
